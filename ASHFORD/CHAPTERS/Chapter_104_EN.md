CHAPTER 104 — THE MEETING

The house was on the edge of Ashford, where the town began to give way to fields and the street lamps became fewer and the road narrowed into a lane.

I went at eight, as the paper had directed. The house was set back from the road, behind a wall of old stone, with a light in the window of the upper floor.

I knocked.

A woman opened the door. She was older than I had expected, with grey hair and a face that had seen many years and many things. She did not ask my name. She stepped back and let me in.

The house was quiet. The hall was narrow, the stairs steep, the walls lined with books — not the books of a shop, but the books of a house that had been lived in for a long time, and where books were the furniture of the life.

She led me to a room at the back of the house, where a fire burned low and a man sat in a chair by the hearth.

He was old — older than I had expected, older than Vale had been, older than anyone I had seen in Ashford. His hands were folded on his knee, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had seen a great deal and had not spoken of most of it.

He looked at me when I came in.

"You are Mr. Ashcroft," he said.

"I am."

"You have passed three tests. The room. The box. The name."

I did not deny it.

"Do you know why you were invited?"

I had thought about this. I had thought about it for three weeks, since the first letter had come, and I had come to a conclusion.

"You wanted to know whether I was someone who could be trusted," I said. "Not whether I was someone who could solve puzzles. Whether I was someone who would use the solving rightly."

The old man was silent a moment.

"That is partly correct," he said. "But it is not the whole of it."

I waited.

"We are a society," he said. "A small society, of men and women who have an interest in the preservation of certain things. Histories. Objects. Knowledge. We have existed for a long time, in Ashford and beyond. We have members in many towns. We do not seek power. We do not seek influence. We seek to preserve what would otherwise be lost—including the secret of the thirteen signs, and the count that ends at 00:00."

He looked at me.

"I have been watching you for two years, Mr. Ashcroft. I have seen you become a keeper. I have seen you become a hunter. I have seen you find the clock, and the key, and the lock, and the letter of Aurelius Ashcroft. I have seen you refuse to open the door, and refuse to ring the bell, and keep the account when you were told to keep it."

I stood in the room, by the fire, and I could not yet tell what to say.

"We need men like you," he said. "Men who can see what is hidden. Men who can find what is lost. Men who know when to stop, and when to continue." He paused, and his eyes held mine. "Men who can be trusted with a truth that has no name."

He was silent a moment.

"I am not asking you to join us," he said. "I am asking you to work with us."

"Work with you?"

"From time to time, we encounter things that require attention. Things that cannot be handled by the ordinary means of the town. Things that require a man who can see what others do not see."

He looked at me.

"We are not the Order of Severence. We are not the church. We are not the government. We are a small society, and we have no name that is spoken in public."

He leaned forward.

"But we have a great deal of work to do. And I believe you are the man to help us do it."

---

I stood in the room, by the fire, and I did not answer.

I had been a keeper for two years. I had been a hunter since the clock in the market. I had been tested three times, and I had passed, and now I was being offered a place — not a membership, but a working relationship — with a society that had no name.

I did not know what to say.

I looked at the old man. I looked at the woman who had opened the door. I looked at the fire, and the books, and the quiet of the house.

"I will need time," I said.

"Take all the time you need," the old man said. "The door is open. When you are ready, you will know what to do."

I left the house.

I walked home through the dark streets, with the cold in my face and the weight of the offer in my mind.

I did not know whether I would accept.

But I knew that I had crossed a threshold.

The hunt had led me to a door.

And the door was open.

I walked home, and I sat at my desk, and I opened the red book, and I wrote:

*The tests are over. I have met the man who sent them. He is old, and he is careful, and he has offered me a place — not a membership, but a working relationship — with a society that has no name.*

*I have not accepted.*

*But I have not refused.*

*The door is open.*

*I will decide when I am ready. But I have already begun to act as if I have accepted.*
