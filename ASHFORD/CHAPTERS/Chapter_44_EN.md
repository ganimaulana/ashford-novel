# Chapter 44 — The Altered Copy

I returned to the vestry the next morning, because the second copy of the Agreement was the only thread I could follow. Vale was gone, and I could not find him, but the document he had shown me was still in my possession, and the copy I had found in the parish records was still on its shelf. I had compared them once, and I had found differences. I needed to compare them again.

The clerk was at his desk when I arrived. He was a thin man of perhaps fifty, with ink-stained fingers and the particular stillness of someone who had spent years handling documents that no one else asked to see. He looked up as I came in, and he did not seem surprised.

"You are the one who was looking at the old maps," he said.

"I am."

"The volume you took out yesterday—it is still on the shelf. I noticed it was returned, but I did not notice who returned it."

"I returned it."

He nodded, and I saw him measure the question before I spoke it.

"Can you tell me where that volume came from?" I asked. "Who copied it, and when, and for what purpose?"

He turned to the shelf and took down the volume I had examined. He opened it to the title page, where a note had been written in a small, careful hand. I had not noticed it the day before.

*Copied by the parish clerk, 1792, from a document held by the Ashcroft family.*

I read it twice. The Ashcroft name. I had seen it before—in the will—but not here.

"The date is 1792," I said. "That is before the Agreement was signed."

"Copied in 1792," the clerk said, "from a document that was older. The Agreement itself may have been written before that date, or it may have been a copy of an older document. I do not know which."

"And the Ashcroft family held the original?"

"I do not know that either. The note says only that the copy was made from a document held by the Ashcroft family. It does not say whether the Ashcroft family owned the original, or whether they were simply the custodians of it."

I looked at the note again. It did not say that the Ashcrofts had written the Agreement. It said only that a document in their possession had been used as the source for this copy.

I turned to the pages I had examined the day before. I found the passage that differed from Vale's copy, and I read it again. In Vale's copy, the passage read: *The line shall be maintained by the keeper of the house and the keeper of the record, in equal measure.* In the parish copy, the phrase was different: *The line shall be maintained by the keeper of the house and the keeper of the record, and the two shall be one.*

I read it three times. The difference was small, but it was significant. In Vale's copy, there were two keepers. In the parish copy, the two were meant to become one.

"Have you seen this before?" I asked, pointing to the passage.

The clerk leaned forward and read it. He did not speak for a moment. When he did, his voice was careful.

"I have seen it," he said. "I have seen this copy many times, and I have always noticed that phrase. I do not know what it means. I do not know why it differs from other copies. I have asked the older men of the town, and they have told me that the Agreement was written at a time when the families were more numerous, and that the language was adjusted over the years to reflect changes in the families."

"Adjusted by whom?"

"I do not know. The documents were copied, and the copies were sometimes corrected. I do not know whether the corrections were authorized, or whether they were made by the copyists themselves."

I looked at the page again. The difference was not an error. It was a deliberate change in meaning. In one version, the keepers were two. In the other, they were one. I did not know which was the original, or which was the intended meaning, or whether the change had been made to clarify the document or to alter it.

"Have there been other copies?" I asked.

"There may have been. I do not know of any others that survive. The old records are incomplete, and many documents have been lost over the years. Some were destroyed, and some were taken, and some were simply misplaced."

"Taken by whom?"

He looked at me, and I saw him hesitate. "I do not know. I have heard the older clerks speak of documents that were removed from the vestry by people who had authority to remove them. I do not know who those people were, or what authority they had. I know only that some documents are no longer here, and that the records do not say where they went."

I thanked him and left the vestry. The cold was sharp, and the lane was quiet, and I carried the weight of what I had learned. The copy in the parish records was from 1792, and it had been copied from a document held by the Ashcroft family. The wording differed from Vale's copy. The difference was small, but it suggested that the Agreement had been altered, or that there were different versions of it, or that the language had been adjusted over time.

I did not know which was true.

---

I went home and sat at the desk, and I took out the copy Vale had given me and the notes I had made from the parish copy. I placed them side by side and read them again. The differences were not numerous, but they were not accidental. The most significant was the phrase about the two keepers becoming one. I did not know whether that was a clarification, a correction, or a deliberate change.

I wrote in my notebook:

*The parish copy of the Agreement was made in 1792 from a document held by the Ashcroft family. It differs from Vale's copy. The most significant difference is the phrase concerning the two responsibilities. I do not know which version is older or more accurate, or whether the difference arose from copying, correction, or an intentional change.*

I sat with the fire low, and I thought of the note on the title page. The Ashcroft family. My family. I had not known that they had held a copy of the Agreement, or that they had been involved in its preservation. I did not know whether they had written it, or merely kept it, or whether they had altered it over time.

I looked at the disk on the desk. Vale had given it to me, and he had told me that the Order would notice that I had found the Agreement. I did not know whether the Order had been involved in the copying of the document, or whether they knew about the differences between the copies.

I shut the book and sat with the fire low. Outside, the lane was quiet, and the cold was settling, and I thought of the two copies of the Agreement, and the different wording, and the note that said the Ashcroft family had held the original. I did not know whether the changes were accidental or deliberate. I did not know whether anyone else had noticed them.

But I knew that someone had recently handled the same material. When I had returned to the vestry, I had noticed that the volume was not exactly where I had left it. It had been returned to the shelf, but it was turned slightly, and the dust on the cover was disturbed in a way that suggested it had been opened again after I had left it. I had not mentioned it to the clerk. I did not know who had looked at it, or why.

I wrote beneath the entry:

*Someone else may have handled the parish copy since I last saw it. The disturbed dust suggests recent use, but it does not identify the person or purpose. The Agreement exists in multiple versions; I do not yet know what the differences mean.*

I shut the book and sat with the cold at the window. I had not found Vale, and I had not found the answer to the Agreement. But I had found something else: a reminder that I was not the only person interested in the document. Someone else had been in the vestry, and someone else had looked at the same copy, and I did not know whether they had found what they were looking for.


Before I left, I asked the clerk one final question.

"If another copy were found, what would make it more reliable than these?"

He considered the question.

"Nothing, merely because it is older."

"What would matter?"

"The chain."

"The chain?"

"Who copied it. From what. When. Whether the source still exists. Whether the hand can be identified. Whether the copy was made for use or for convenience."

He tapped the book.

"A document does not become trustworthy because it is old. It becomes useful when you know how it came to be."

I thanked him.

On the walk home I thought about that more than I thought about the altered phrase.

The difference between the copies remained important.

But now I had another question: not simply what the Agreement said, but how its words had travelled from one hand to another.
