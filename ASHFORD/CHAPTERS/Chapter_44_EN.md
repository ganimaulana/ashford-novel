# Chapter 44 — The Watchman

On the tenth of March, Crowe came to my door. He did not ask to come in; he stood on the step with the cold air curling around him, and he looked at me with a face that was not anger, but judgment. "You have been accepted," he said. "You have been tested, and you have been accepted. The Order has a place for you, if you want it."

He spoke the last words with the flatness of a man who did not care whether I accepted or not. I had heard that voice before—in the markets, at the pump, when a man offers you something he does not need you to take. It was the voice of one who had done his part and would do no more.

I looked at him, and I was not afraid. I was not sure, but I was not afraid. "I will come," I said. "I will see what you have to offer."

He nodded, and he turned and went down the lane. I followed him through the town, past the church and the market and the houses that had stood for a hundred years, to a door I had not seen before. It was painted the same colour as the wall, and it was not a door that wanted to be found. He opened it and led me through a narrow passage to a room at the back of a building. The room was plain, with a table and a chair and a lamp that burned low. He closed the door, and I heard the lock turn. I was not afraid, but I was aware that I had walked into a place not designed for easy exits.

"Sit," he said. It was not a request.

I sat. He took a place opposite me, and I saw a small box on the table between us. He opened it and took out a coin, setting it on the wood. "This is a test," he said. "It is not a test of your mind, or of your strength. It is a test of your nature. You have kept the agreement, and you have been accepted by the Order. But acceptance is not enough. You must show us that you can serve the Order, not just as a keeper, but as a member."

I looked at the coin. It was plain, with no mark I could see. "What do you want me to do?" I asked.

"You will take this coin," he said. "And you will carry it with you until I ask for it back. You will not spend it, and you will not lose it. You will carry it, and you will wait for my instruction."

I took the coin and put it in my pocket. It was cold against my thigh, and I felt its weight not as metal, but as a question. I did not know what it meant, but I knew it was a test. I went home and wrote in my book: *10 Mar. I have been tested by the Order. I have been given a coin to carry, and I have been told to wait. I do not know what the coin is for, or what the test will be. But I have passed the first test, and I am still the keeper of the agreement.*

The coin stayed in my pocket for seven days. I did not spend it, and I did not lose it. I carried it everywhere—to Margaret's, to Calloway's office, to the lane and back. I did not ask what it was for. I did not know what the test was, but I knew it was a test, and I knew I had to pass it. Each night I would touch the coin through the cloth, and each night I would wonder what it meant to carry something that had no purpose but to be carried.

On the night of the seventeenth of March, I was called to the old mill. The door was open, and the room beneath the town was lit with the same light that came from the walls. Eleanor Shaw was waiting for me, and she was not alone. There were others—men and women I had seen at the meeting, and some I had not.

"Mr. Ashcroft," she said. "You have carried the coin, and you have not asked what it is for. You have passed the test. You are now a member of the Order of Severence." She took the coin from me and held it up to the light. "This is the coin of the Order," she said. "It is a symbol of trust. You have carried it, and you have not used it. You have shown that you can be trusted."

She handed the coin back to me, and I put it in my pocket. "You will need a name," she said. "A name that is not your own. A name that the Order will know you by. What would you like to be called?"

I thought for a moment. I thought of the boundary, and the house, and the agreement. I thought of the watching I had done, and the watching I would do. "Watchman," I said.

She nodded, and the others nodded, and I saw that it was accepted. "Watchman," she repeated. "You have chosen well." She drew a card from her sleeve and placed it on the table. It bore a symbol—a circle, a line, and a dot. "You are the Watchman," she said. "And you are one of us now. Welcome to the Order of Severence."

I took the card, and I went home with it. That night, I sat at my desk and looked at the coin and the card. The coin was plain, but I knew it was more than it seemed. The card had the symbol of the Order, and I knew I had crossed a line. I was no longer just a keeper. I was a member of the Order.

I wrote in my book: *17 Mar. I am the Watchman, and I am a member of the Order of Severence. I have taken the coin, and I have taken the name, and I have taken the symbol. I do not know what the Order will ask of me, but I know that I am part of it now.*

I set the coin and card beside the gear and the bell, and I looked at them all. I had the symbols of the agreement and the symbols of the Order, and I did not know which would be stronger. I did not know what it meant to be the Watchman, but I knew I would find out. The lane was quiet that night, and the house at the end of it stood with its rosemary on the sill, and I felt the weight of the name settle over me like a cloak that had been waiting for its wearer.
