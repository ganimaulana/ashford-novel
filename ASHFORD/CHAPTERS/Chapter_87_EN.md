CHAPTER 87 — THE PRICE

I did not hear from the office for two days after the market square. I had expected something—a word, a summons, a sign that the examination was continuing. But nothing came, and I found myself walking the lane each morning, watching the door, waiting for a note that did not arrive.

On Sunday morning I went to the market again. I had not planned to go. I had simply found myself walking, and the stalls were open, and the lanes were full. I bought nothing. I walked the length of the market and was turning back when I saw Peale.

He was standing by the chandler’s, the same place he had stood before. He was not looking at me. He was looking at the sky, and when he saw me, he nodded once, as though he had been expecting me.

I nodded back. I did not approach him. I did not know what to say to him, and he did not say anything to me. We stood there for a moment, and then he turned and walked away, and I went home.

On Monday morning a note came. It said only: The office. Nine o’clock. Bring nothing.

I put on my coat and went.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open, and the crowd was already there. There were fewer of them than before—perhaps fourteen, perhaps fifteen. Some of the faces I remembered. Some I did not. The man with the notebook was there, standing apart from the others, his notebook closed but in his hand. The older man with the frayed cuffs was there, leaning against the wall by the pump. Ellison was there, standing near the back, his hands in his pockets. Merritt was standing alone by the gate, not looking at anyone. Peale was there, standing near the door, and he looked at me when I arrived and nodded once.

The man I had seen at the market square was there. He was standing near the wall, his arms crossed, his head tilted slightly. He was not looking at anyone in particular. He was looking at the room as a whole, his gaze moving slowly from one face to another. I still did not know his name.

Calloway came out. He did not speak at first. He looked at the crowd the way he always looked at it, as though he had seen it before and knew he would see it again.

“You have completed the tasks,” he said. “Some of you found what you were looking for. Some of you did not. The office does not care which. The office cares how you chose to look.”

He paused.

“The next task is different,” he said. “You will go to the old mill. There is a document there, sealed in an envelope. It is in one of three rooms. The rooms are on the ground floor, the first floor, and the cellar. You will retrieve the document and bring it to this office. You have until noon. That is all.”

He turned and went inside.

We stood in silence.

“Three rooms,” Ellison said. “One document.”

“We need to choose which room to search first,” I said.

“And the others?”

“We cannot search all three at once. We have to decide.”

I looked at the other candidates. Some were already moving. The man with the notebook was walking, his head down, his notebook open. The older man with the frayed cuffs was walking, his hands in his pockets. Peale was walking, his head down, his steps even.

The man I had seen at the market square was still standing near the wall. He was not moving. He was watching the others leave, his head still tilted.

“We should go,” I said.

We walked. The old mill was on the other side of the river, the same place we had been before. The wheel was broken, the roof was sagging, the windows were dark and empty. The door was open, and the wind blew through it with a sound like a voice I could not understand.

We went inside. The ground floor was the largest room, with broken machinery and piles of debris. The first floor was smaller, with a wooden floor and a window that looked out over the river. The cellar was dark, accessible by a narrow staircase near the back of the building.

“Which room first?” Ellison said.

“The ground floor is the largest,” I said. “It would take the longest to search. If the document is there, we should find it early and be done. If it is not there, we have wasted time on the largest room.”

“Or we search the cellar first,” Ellison said. “It is the smallest. We could clear it quickly and move on.”

“The first floor is in between,” I said.

Merritt was standing near the staircase, looking down into the cellar. He did not speak.

I looked at the ground floor. I looked at the stairs. I looked at the cellar door.

I noticed something. A faint light, just visible at the top of the stairs to the first floor, as though a window had been opened that I had not noticed before. It was not much—a sliver of daylight that had not been there when we entered. I did not know whether it meant anything. It could have been the sun shifting behind clouds. It could have been the wind moving a curtain.

I said nothing.

“The cellar is dark,” I said. “If the document is there, it may be hidden. If it is not there, we waste time looking in the dark.”

“But it is the smallest,” Ellison said.

“The smallest is not always the fastest.”

Ellison was silent for a moment. Then he said, “We need to choose.”

I looked at the light on the stairs again. I still did not know what it meant.

“The cellar,” I said. “It is the smallest. If the document is not there, we move to the ground floor. Then the first floor.”

Ellison nodded. Merritt said nothing. He walked toward the cellar stairs.

We went down. The cellar was cold and damp. The light from the window above barely reached the bottom of the stairs. I could see the walls, the floor, the old barrels and crates stacked against the walls.

We searched. I moved through the space, looking at the crates, the floor, the walls. I found nothing.

Ellison was examining the barrels. Merritt was standing near the far wall, looking at the floor.

I looked at the floor. There was a loose stone near the wall. I walked over and knelt.

“There is something here,” I said.

I lifted the stone. Beneath it was a folded piece of paper, not an envelope. I unfolded it. It said: *Not here. Look above.*

I stood up. “It is a false lead.”

Ellison walked over. He read the paper. He said nothing.

Merritt did not move.

“We have lost time,” Ellison said.

I looked at the stairs. I thought about the light I had seen on the first floor.

“We should go up,” I said. “The first floor.”

We went up. The first floor was smaller than the ground floor, but the light was stronger. I could see a window that had been opened, a thin crack of daylight. There were footprints on the dusty floor—fresh prints, leading toward a table in the corner.

On the table was an envelope. But it was not sealed. It was open, its contents gone.

“Someone was here before us,” Ellison said.

I looked at the prints. They were fresh. The window had been opened recently.

“They came through the window,” I said.

Ellison walked to the window. He looked out at the river. “They are already gone.”

I looked at the empty envelope. I thought about the light on the stairs. I had seen it before we went down to the cellar. I had not known what it meant. I had not said anything.

I walked back down the stairs. The man I had seen at the market square was standing near the door, his arms crossed. He was not looking at me. He was looking at the stairs.

“You saw the light,” he said.

I stopped. “I saw it.”

“You did not say anything.”

“I did not know what it meant.”

He did not say anything else. He turned and walked away.

I walked back to the office. Ellison followed. Merritt followed behind us.

I placed the empty envelope on the clerk’s desk. He looked at it. He did not look surprised.

“You did not find the document,” he said.

“No,” I said. “Someone else found it first.”

He wrote something in the ledger. He did not say anything else.

I walked home slowly. I thought about the light on the stairs. I had seen it. I had not known what it meant. I had not said anything.

I sat in the chair by the window and thought about it. I had noticed something. I had not acted on it. I had not known whether it was important. And because I had not known, I had said nothing.

I did not know whether I would have acted differently if I had been certain. I did not know whether certainty was something I could afford to wait for.

But I knew that the cost of not knowing was real. I had seen the light. I had said nothing. And someone else had found the document first.

I did not know whether I would make the same choice again. I did not know whether I could have prevented it. I only knew that the price of uncertainty was not always small. And that the next time I saw something I did not understand, I would have to decide whether to speak. Even if I was not sure.

The empty envelope bothered Leon more than the missing document. An empty container proved that something had been there only if the envelope itself belonged to the task. Otherwise it was simply an empty envelope. He had learned enough not to turn a convenient detail into a conclusion.
