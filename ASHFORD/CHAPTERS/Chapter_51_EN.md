CHAPTER 51 — THE THRESHOLD

I waited for the house to appear again.

It did not come that night, nor the next. I went to the common each evening as the light failed, and I stood at the edge of the grass where the door-shaped press remained, and I watched the twilight deepen. The house did not come.

I did not know whether it appeared on a schedule I had not yet learned, or whether it appeared only when something in Ashford had changed, or whether it had appeared as often as it had in the spring because I had been looking for it, and now that I was watching it would not come.

I went about my days. I kept the account. I walked the boundary. I knelt at the fourth stone, where the hairline mark still lay across its face, and I did not touch it, and I did not try to understand it. I let the days pass.

But I could not let the house go. I had crossed its threshold once, in the spring. I had seen the clock stopped at 00:00, and I had heard the knocking from beneath the floor — three knocks, slow, deliberate, as if someone beneath the boards had been counting, and had reached three, and had stopped. I had not understood it then. I did not understand it now. But I had begun to suspect that the knocking was a language — the language of the count, marking time until the bell would call.

On the third evening, I walked to the edge of the common and found something different. The grass was no longer pressed in the shape of a door. It was pressed in the shape of a room. The shape of the door was still there, at the edge, but beyond it the grass was flattened in a larger rectangle — the shape of a floor, the shape of the house I had entered in the spring. The house had stood here again while I was not watching. It had left its mark and gone.

I knelt. The grass was cold and wet. I put my hand to the grass where the threshold had lain, and I felt nothing — but I had not expected to feel anything. At the centre of the pressed floor, there was a mark. A line, and three strokes. The keeper's sign. Pressed into the earth by a house that did not exist.

I went to the mill that night, not to the door, but to the wall where the oldest stones stood. I carried a lamp. I had not carried a lamp to the mill before. I had not wanted to see too clearly. Now I wanted to see.

I held the lamp to the fourth stone, where the hairline mark ran across its face — unchanged. I held the lamp lower, to the base of the stone, where the earth met the stone. There was a mark there. Not the circle. A different mark, cut into the stone at its base, worn smooth by weather and age. A line, and three strokes. The keeper's sign. On the fourth stone, at its base, hidden where the earth covered it.

I had not seen it before. I had been looking at the crack. I had been looking at the circle. I had been looking at what the Order had told me to look at.

I did not sleep that night. I sat at my desk with the lamp burning, the gear on the desk before me, and I thought about the mark on the fourth stone. The keeper's sign was at the base of the stone. The circle was on the face of the stone. The keeper's sign was older. The keeper's sign was hidden. The keeper's sign was what the house had pressed into the grass, what the door bore beneath its seal, what the vestry book had recorded in the margin of a page the surveyors had never seen.

I had found it three times in three days — on the stone, on the door, on the grass where the house had stood. I did not know whether that meant I was meant to find it, or whether it meant that something was ending.

I opened my notebook and wrote: The keeper's sign is on the fourth stone, at the base, hidden by earth. It is on the door beneath the mill, below the seal, worn smooth. It is in the grass of the common, pressed there by the house that does not remain. I have found it three times. I do not know what it means. I know that Vale knew it, and that he kept it from the Order, and that he kept it from me. I do not know whether he was protecting me from knowing it, or protecting the mark from being found.

I closed the book. I took the gear from the desk and put it in my coat. I did not know what I was carrying it for. But I knew that I would carry it. And I knew that I would go back to the common in the morning, and I would wait for the house to appear again.

And when it did, I would not stand at the edge of the grass and watch. I would cross the threshold. I would go inside. I would find what the house had been pressing into the earth, each time it stood on the common.

The clock in the house was stopped at 00:00 — a time I had not yet learned to read, but one I was beginning to suspect was the key to everything. The knocking had come from beneath the floor, three times, slow and deliberate. I had not learned what it meant. But I had learned that it came when I was close to the sign. And I had learned that the sign was older than the circle, older than the Order, older than the wall. The keeper's sign. And I was the keeper.

I sat in the dark, and I understood that I was standing on the threshold of something. Not the threshold of the house — the threshold of the keeping itself. I had been keeping the door closed. I had been keeping the boundary. I had been keeping the account. But I had not been keeping the keeper's sign. I had not even known it existed. And now that I knew, I could not un-know it. The threshold had been crossed. And I could not go back.
