CHAPTER 51 — THE THRESHOLD

I waited for the house to appear again.

It did not come that night, nor the next. I went to the common each evening, as the light failed and the lamps began to burn along the High Street, and I stood at the edge of the grass where the door-shaped press remained, and I watched the twilight deepen, and the house did not come.

I could not yet tell whether it appeared on a schedule I had not yet learned, or whether it appeared only when something in Ashford had changed, or whether it had appeared as often as it had in the spring because I had been looking for it, and now that I was watching it would not come.

I had read, once, that a man who watches a door too long will begin to doubt that anyone will ever open it.

I did not know whether that was true of houses as well.

I went about my days. I kept the account. I walked the boundary. I knelt at the fourth stone, where the hairline mark still lay across its face, and I did not touch it, and I did not try to understand it. I let the days pass.

And on the third evening, I walked to the edge of the common and found something different.

The grass was no longer pressed in the shape of a door.

It was pressed in the shape of a room.

I stopped at the edge of the grass and looked at it. The shape of the door was still there, at the edge of the pressed area, but beyond it the grass was flattened in a larger rectangle — the shape of a floor, the shape of the house I had entered in the spring, when the clock had stopped at 11:17 and I had heard the knocking from beneath the floor.

The house had stood here again, while I was not watching.

It had stood here, and it had left its mark on the grass, and it had gone.

I knelt. The grass was cold and wet. The shape of the floor was clear — the walls, the corners, the narrow space where the door had been. I put my hand to the grass where the threshold had lain, and I felt nothing, and I had not expected to feel anything, but I had hoped.

I stood. I looked at the common. The grass was dark in the failing light. The shape of the house lay on it like a wound, pressed into the earth, waiting for the house to return and stand on it again.

And at the centre of the pressed floor, where the interior of the house would have been, there was a mark.

A line, and three strokes.

Drawn in the grass, bent into the earth, as if the weight of the house had pressed the keeper's sign into the ground beneath it.

I had not seen it before. I had been looking at the door-shaped press, and I had not walked to the centre of the shape, and I had not seen what the house had left behind.

I stood in the centre of the pressed grass, in the dark, and I looked at the mark.

The keeper's sign. The one that was older than the circle. Pressed into the earth by a house that did not exist.

---

I went to the mill that night, not to the door, but to the wall where the oldest stones stood, where the boundary line had been drawn before the surveyors had mapped it, where the circle and the short line had been carved into the stone.

I carried a lamp.

I had not carried a lamp to the mill before. I had gone in the half-light, in the grey hours, when the shadows were long and the details were soft. I had not wanted to see too clearly.

Now I wanted to see.

I held the lamp to the fourth stone, where the hairline mark ran across its face. The crack — or the mark — was still there, unchanged, a thin grey line in the grey stone. I had knelt beside it each morning and touched it, and I had felt nothing.

I held the lamp lower. To the base of the stone, where the earth met the stone, where the grass grew thin and the roots of the wall had been laid in the older time.

There was a mark there.

Not the circle and the short line. Not the crack. A different mark, cut into the stone at its base, worn smooth by weather and age, almost invisible unless the light was held at the right angle and the eye knew what to look for.

A line, and three strokes.

The keeper's sign.

On the fourth stone, at its base, hidden where the earth covered it.

I had not seen it before. I had been looking at the crack. I had been looking at the circle. I had been looking at what the Order had told me to look at.

I had not looked at the base of the stone, where the keeper's sign had been cut into the stone before the wall was built, before the boundary was drawn, before the Order had ever existed.

---

I did not sleep that night.

I sat in my room with the lamp burning, the gear on the desk before me, the agreement in the drawer, and I thought about the mark on the fourth stone.

The keeper's sign was at the base of the stone. The circle was on the face of the stone. The keeper's sign was older. The keeper's sign was hidden. The keeper's sign was what the house had pressed into the grass of the common, and what the door beneath the mill bore beneath its seal, and what the vestry book had recorded in the margin of a page that the surveyors had never seen.

The keeper's sign was the mark that Vale had known and never spoken of.

And I had found it three times in three days — on the stone, on the door, on the grass where the house had stood.

I did not know whether that meant I was meant to find it, or whether it meant that something was ending, and the marks were appearing because they would not be needed much longer.

I opened my notebook. I wrote:

The keeper's sign is on the fourth stone, at the base, hidden by earth. It is on the door beneath the mill, below the seal, worn smooth. It is in the grass of the common, pressed there by the house that does not remain.

I have found it three times. I do not know what it means.

But I know that Vale knew it, and that he kept it from the Order, and that he kept it from me, and that he died with it still unspoken.

I do not know whether he was protecting me from knowing it, or protecting the mark from being found.

I closed the book.

I took the gear from the desk and put it in my coat.

I did not know what I was carrying it for. But I knew that I would carry it, and that I would go back to the common in the morning, and that I would wait for the house to appear again.

And when it did, I would not stand at the edge of the grass and watch.

I would cross the threshold. I would go inside.

And I would find what the house had been pressing into the earth, each time it stood on the common.

The keeper's sign was not a warning. It was a door.

And I had been standing on the wrong side of it.
