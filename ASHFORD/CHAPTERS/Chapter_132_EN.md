CHAPTER 132 — THE KEEPING OF THE MARSH

I stayed with the marsh people through the autumn.

They taught me the ways of the marsh — the paths, the water, the reeds, the signs of the weather. They taught me how to read the marsh, the way I had learned to read the boundary in Ashford.

The marsh had its own keeping. It was a keeping of water and land, of the paths that changed with the season, of the places that were safe and the places that were not.

I learned it as I had learned the keeping of the boundary.

And I kept the house.

I went to it each day. I entered it. I knelt at the first sign on the floor. I did not move the stone.

I kept it.

I could not yet tell what I was keeping. I could not say what the house was for. I could not determine why the first sign was on the floor.

But I kept it, the way the marsh people had kept it, and the way the keepers before me had kept the boundary.

I kept it.

---

In the winter, the marsh changed.

The water rose. The paths disappeared. The reeds died back, and the marsh became a different place — a place of open water and grey sky, where the land seemed to have vanished beneath the surface.

I stayed.

I did not go to the village. I stayed at the house, in my shelter, and I kept the house, and I kept the ring of stones, and I kept the first sign on the floor.

The marsh people came to me, in the winter, with food and dry wood.

"You are still here," the woman said.

"I am still here."

"You have kept the house through the winter."

"I have kept it."

She looked at me a long moment.

"The house has never been kept through the winter," she said. "No one has stayed in the marsh through the winter, since the marsh people came."

I stood in the cold.

"Then I am the first," I said.

"You are the first," she said. "You are the keeper."

I looked at the house, and the ring of stones, and the marsh.

I had come to the marsh because the count had called me. I had found the house, and the ring of stones, and the first sign on the floor.

I had kept them through the winter.

And I was the first.

---

The spring came, and the marsh changed again.

The water fell. The paths reappeared. The reeds grew, and the marsh became the place I had found, a year ago.

I had been in the marsh for a year.

I had kept the house. I had kept the ring of stones. I had kept the first sign on the floor.

I had not moved the stone.

I did not know what was beneath it.

But I had kept it.

And I understood that the keeping was not about knowing.

It was about keeping.

I had learned that in Ashford. I had learned it at the boundary, and at the door, and at the bell. I had learned it in the chamber, and at the reading room, and at the house at the end of the lane.

The keeping was not about knowing. It was about keeping.

I had kept the house in the marsh. I had kept it the way the marsh people had kept it, and the way the keepers before me had kept the boundary.

I had kept it.

And that was the whole of it.

---

On a day in the spring, I went to the village.

The innkeeper was there, at the inn, and he looked up when I came in.

"You have been in the marsh," he said.

"I have. I have kept the house."

He looked at me a long moment.

"The house," he said. "You have kept it."

"I have kept it."

He nodded slowly.

"Then you are the keeper," he said. "The marsh people have said so. The house has been kept through the winter. And you are the keeper."

I sat at the table.

"I am the keeper," I said. "I have kept the house, and the ring of stones, and the first sign on the floor. I have not moved the stone. I have kept it."

He looked at me.

"And what will you do now?"

I was silent a moment.

"I do not know," I said. "I have kept the house. I have kept the keeping. The count called me here, and I have come, and I have kept."

He nodded.

"Then the keeping is enough," he said. "The keeping is the thing."

I sat in the inn, in the warmth, and I understood that the keeping was the thing.

I had kept the house in the marsh. I had kept it through the winter. I had kept it the way it had been given to me to keep it.

And the keeping was enough.

I did not know what I would do next. I could not say whether the count would call me further.

But I knew that I had kept the keeping.

And that was enough.

The count of thirteen signs moved forward, and the hour of the bell drew nearer.
