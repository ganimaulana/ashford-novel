# Chapter 27 — Helen’s Hour

The Thursday came, and with it the turn of the season toward its middle, and I found I had been four weeks in the town, because the counting came up at the breakfast table of its own accord, the way such counts come when a man has stopped making them: the twelfth of November to the nineteenth of December, and the five weeks wanted one day of their full telling, and in the five weeks the town had given me its two papers and its one house, and had kept from me the one thing the papers were kept about, and I understood that the giving and the keeping were the town's one act, and had been so from the first.

I had taken the bell from the drawer again that morning, testing the theory that had formed in my mind. It had sounded faintly, and I had felt a deep cold settle into my bones that did not lift for hours. My hands shook as I replaced it, and I sat a long while before the fire before I could write.

The lamp burned at nine on the Wednesday, and was carried away along the rooms, and no sound came, and I set it down as the thirteenth reading, and did not set down anything beneath it; and in the night the sound did not come either, and the alternation stood as it had stood, and the house kept itself, and I kept my part of the keeping, which was the watching of it.

It was in the morning, a little before the middle of the day, that Helen came.

She had not come to my door since the first days, and the not-coming of hers was a thing I had taken in without taking it up, because she is one that comes by her own hour and not by the town's, and her hour had not come these three weeks, and I had let it stand; but it came on the Thursday morning, and she stood at my door with her shawl over her arm, and the cold of the day about her, and asked to come in, which is what she had not done before, and I let her in, and she came to the fire, and stood at it the length of a breath, and did not sit, which I took for the way of it.

"I have come," she said, "because there is a thing to say, and the saying of it wants a room, and not the street. I will say it, and I will go, and you will keep it as you keep the other things, and I do not ask you to keep it, because the asking of keeping is the thing that undoes it."

"Say it," I said.

"The man has been at my door," she said. "Not the man of the watch, and not the man of the town. The man of the inn. He came on the Tuesday evening, when the dark was down, and he came to the door as a man comes that has chosen the door, and he asked of me of the lane, and of the houses of the lane, and of how long I had lived in the lane, and I answered him as the lane answers, and he went away with the answer, and I have not seen him since. That is the thing, and I have come to say it, because the man asked of the lane, and the lane is the thing you keep, and the asking of it is the thing you want to know of."

"What did he ask of the houses," I said.

"He asked," said Helen, "which of the houses had been long in one keeping, and which of the houses had changed their keeping, and he asked it as a man asks that is making a list of it, and I answered him of the houses that have changed, because they are many, and I did not answer him of the houses that have not changed, because they are few, and the few are the ones the question wants. He did not ask of the house at the end of the lane. I will tell you that, because it is the thing. He did not ask of it by name, and I did not give it him by name, and the not-asking of it was the asking of it, and I knew it, and he knew I knew it, and we stood at the door with the knowing of it between us, and he went away."

"You have seen him before," I said.

"I have seen him," said Helen. "Not in the town. I will say that, and I will say no more of where, because the more is not mine to say, and I do not keep it for the saying of it. I have seen him, and the seeing of him is the reason I have come to your door, because a man that has been seen before is a man that comes back, and the coming back of him is the thing that is now in the lane, and you are in the lane, and I would have you know the man has been seen before, and not by me only."

She stood at the fire with the shawl over her arm, and I saw then that the coming had cost her something, because the face of her was the face of one that has done a thing she had set her face against, and the doing of it stood in her the way such doings stand, and I did not ask her of the more, because the more was not given, and the asking of what is not given is the thing that shuts the door that has been opened.

"One thing more," she said. "The lamp. You have watched the lamp, and I know you have watched it, because the watching of it is the thing that is seen from the lane, and I will tell you this of the lamp, and it is the last thing I will tell you: the lamp is not kept for the lane. The lamp is kept for the house. There is a difference in the two, and the difference is the thing, and a man that watches the lamp for what it shows the lane will watch it wrong, and a man that watches it for what it shows the house will watch it right. I have kept a lamp in my own house these many years, and I know the difference, and I tell it you because you have watched the lamp, and the watching of it has made you part of the lane, and the lane is come to the place where it wants the knowing of it."

She went then, and I let her out, and she went down the lane with the cold of the day about her, and did not look back, and I stood at the door with the telling of it, and understood that Helen had come to my door for the first time, and had given me three things: the man at her door, the seeing of him before, and the keeping of the lamp; and that the three things stood together as the things of this town stand, and that the giving of them had cost her, and that the cost was the measure of the things, and I went in to the fire, and took the book, and wrote them down.
