CHAPTER 101 — THE BOX AND THE SECOND LETTER

I did not go back to the room above the chandler's shop.

I had given my answer — or rather, I had kept my answer in the red book, and had not delivered it to anyone, because no one had told me how to deliver it. The letter had said only to come to the address before midnight on Thursday. It had not said what to do when I arrived.

I had arrived. I had seen the room. I had read the three accounts. I had understood that the answer could not be known from the evidence. And I had gone home and written it in the red book.

That was the whole of my part.

I could not yet tell whether that was what the sender wanted. I could not yet tell whether there was a correct answer at all, or whether the sender was watching me, or whether the test was already over.

I waited.

---

On the following Tuesday, a box was left at my door.

It was a plain wooden box, small, unmarked, the size of a book. It was placed on the step, in the morning, the way the letter had been placed on the hall table.

I brought it in. I set it on the desk. I did not open it at once.

I sat and looked at it, and I thought about the room above the chandler's shop, and the three accounts, and the lamp, and the silence, and the answer I had written in the red book.

I had not sent the answer to anyone.

And yet a box had come.

I opened it.

Inside, on a bed of dark cloth, lay a quantity of gold coins — more than I had expected, more than I had ever been given at once — and beneath them, a folded sheet of paper, in the same hand as the first letter.

I took out the paper. I unfolded it.

*Your answer was correct.*

*You may consider the matter concluded.*

*Unless you wish to continue.*

I sat at the desk, with the gold in the box and the paper in my hand, and I read the three sentences.

*Your answer was correct.*

I had not sent an answer. I had not told anyone what I had concluded. And yet the sender knew — knew that I had concluded the answer could not be determined, and knew that I had concluded rightly.

That was the first thing.

*You may consider the matter concluded.*

The door was open. I could keep the gold. I could consider the matter done. I had solved — or not solved — a puzzle, and I had been paid, and there was no obligation.

That was the second thing.

*Unless you wish to continue.*

There was another address. On the reverse of the paper, in the same hand, an address and a date: the lending library, on the following Thursday, at an hour past dark.

No explanation. No promise. No threat.

I sat in the room, with the box before me and the paper in my hand, and I understood that I was being given a choice.

I could stop. The gold was mine. The matter was concluded. Nothing more would be asked of me.

Or I could continue. I could go to the lending library on Thursday, and I could see what the sender wanted next.

I did not know who the sender was. I did not know what they wanted. I did not know whether the gold was a payment, or a test, or a trap.

But I had been a keeper for two years. I had been a hunter since the clock in the market. I had learned to distrust the things that arrived unasked at my door.

And I had also learned that the things that arrived unasked at my door were never quite what they seemed.

I put the gold in the drawer. I put the paper in the drawer, with the red book and the agreement.

I did not decide that day.

But I knew, sitting in the dark, what I would do.

---

The lending library was the room above the chandler's shop — the room where Marianne Loxley kept her tables and her ledgers.

I went on the Thursday, at the hour past dark, as the address directed.

The library was lit. The lamp was burning on Marianne's table, and the door was open, and I stood at the foot of the stair and looked up at the light, and I did not know whether I was walking into a meeting, or a trap, or something else.

I climbed the stair.

Marianne was at her table, as she always was, with the lamp and the ledgers. But she was not alone.

A woman sat at the far end of the table, in a plain coat, with her hands folded in front of her. She was neither old nor young. She had the look of a woman who had waited a long time for a door to be opened.

She looked up when I came in.

"Mr. Ashcroft," she said. "You chose to continue."

I did not ask how she knew my name. I had learned not to ask such questions in this town.

"You sent the letter," I said.

"I sent the letter." She did not move. "And you gave the correct answer. I have been watching since you found the clock in the market. I have been watching since you found the key, and the lock, and the letter of Aurelius Ashcroft."

I stood very still.

"You know about the clock," I said. "You know about the key."

"I know about all of it," she said. "I have been watching you for two years. I have been watching you since you became keeper. I have been watching you since you became something else — a hunter, a finder of things that were meant to stay hidden."

I looked at her.

"Who are you?"

She was silent a moment.

"My name is Adeline Brume," she said. "I am an auctioneer. And I have been looking for a man like you for a long time."

She set a paper on the table before her.

"Not to hire you," she said. "Not to employ you. To ask you a question."

I looked at the paper.

"What question?"

She slid the paper toward me.

"There is an item coming to auction," she said. "An old item. It has been in a family for a hundred years, and they do not know what it is. They think it is worth nothing."

She looked at me.

"I want you to tell me what it is worth."

I stood at the table, in the lamplight, and I looked at the paper, and I understood that the test was not over.

The first test had been a room and three accounts.

The second test was an item.

And I did not yet know what the third test would be.

But I knew, standing in the lamplight, that I had walked into something — that the letter, and the box, and the gold, and this woman in the plain coat, were all part of it.

And I knew that I would see it through.

The hunt had begun again.

And this time, I was not sure I was the hunter.

But I was in it.

And I would find out what was at the end of it.
