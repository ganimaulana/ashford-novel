# Chapter 26 — What the Post Office Kept

The Tuesday stood still, the way the middle of a week stands still when the week has done its great things early: the rate was asked and carried, and Hobbs was named and noted, and the town had settled back over both of them as a town settles over what it has done, and there was nothing more to be done of either, and the doing of nothing is the town's way of keeping a thing.

The lamp burned at nine, and was carried away along the rooms, and no sound came; and the sound did not come in the night; and I set it down as the eleventh and twelfth readings, and the alternation stood as it had stood since the Saturday, with the one break of the Saturday kept by itself in my book as breaks are kept, and I let the rule stand, because a rule with one break in it is a rule still, and a man does not throw out his rule for the one night it fails him, when the failing of it is the thing he wants most to understand.

It was the post office that moved the day.

I had gone for the London post at the hour the lane lives by, because that is the hour, and a man that keeps the hour is kept by it, and there was no letter for me, and I stood at the counter while the postmaster sorted the little that had come, and the boy — the nephew — stood at his elbow in the way of boys that are being made into postmasters, and I was on the point of going out, when the postmaster spoke without looking up from the sorting, which is the way of a man that has made up his mind to a thing and means to have it out with himself in public.

"There is a question going about the town, sir," he said, "and it is coming my way, and I will say to you what I will say to them that bring it, because you keep the roll, and the question is the roll's question. There has been asking of the letters that come to the house at the end of the lane, and the asking is not yours, and I would have you know it is not, because I have answered it the way I answer it, and I would have you know the answer also."

"I have not asked," I said.

"No, sir," said the postmaster. "You have not asked, and that is why I say it to you. The boy carried to you the telling of the evening letters, because I charged him to it, and the telling was given to the watch, and the watch is the paper you are on; and there has come asking since, of a different sort, and the asking is where it comes from, and I do not know, and the asking is after what I do not keep, and I do know. That is the two halves of it, and I will say both halves to you, because the town will say them different."

"What does it ask," I said.

"It asks," said the postmaster, "for the letters that go out of the house. It asks who writes from the house, and where the writing goes, and by what hand the writing is carried out; and I answer, as I answer all that ask me: that the post office of this town keeps the letters that come through the post, and the letters that come to the house by the evening hand are not come through the post, and the letters that go out of the house are not gone through the post, and the office has no record of either, and no knowledge of either, and the answer is the true answer, because it is the office's answer, and the office does not keep what it does not carry."

"Who asks," I said.

The postmaster looked up then, and the sorting stood still in his hands, and the boy stood still at his elbow.

"That is the half I do not say," he said, "because I do not know it, and a man that does not know a thing says he does not know it, and that is what I have said to them that ask, and what I say to you. The asking came to the office by a hand, and the hand asked the question, and went away with the answer, and I saw the hand, and I did not know the hand, and the knowing of hands is the business of this office, and I will say that plain: I did not know the hand. That is the thing, sir. There are hands in this town I know, and hands I know of, and the hand that asked was not of either sort. It was a stranger's hand, and the town has had a stranger in it this fortnight, and I keep the post of the town, and I know what I know."

He went down to the sorting again, and the boy went down to his elbow, and I stood at the counter with the telling of it, and took my hat and went out into the street, and did not say more, because there was no more to say, and the postmaster had said the whole of what he would say, and had said the half he would not say in the saying of it.

---

I went home by the long way, because a man that has had a telling wants the length of a street with it, and I put the thing together as I went. The letters come to the house by the evening hand, and go out of the house by the evening hand, and the post office does not carry them and does not keep them; and the office has been asked of the letters that go out, and the asking came by a hand the postmaster does not know, and the town has had a stranger in it this fortnight, and the stranger sits at the wardens' table and names the figure of the rate; and there are, therefore, two papers in the town that ask of the house at the end of the lane — the watch, which asks by the round, and the stranger, which asks by the hand — and the two askings stand about the same house, and I am on one of the two papers, and I do not know yet whether the two papers are kept by the same keeping.

At Margaret's I stood the length of a cup, because that is the length, and Margaret set the cup down and did not speak, which is her way when the speaking is wanted from the other side, and I spoke.

"The post office has been asked," I said, "of the letters that go out of the house at the end of the lane."

Margaret stood at the counter with the cloth in her hand, and she did not ask who had asked, and she did not ask when, and I took that in as the thing she knew already, and the knowing of it was in her face the way the knowing of things is in her face, which is not the way of telling.

"It was to come," she said. "When the rate is asked, the letters are asked next, because the rate is the town's money and the letters are the town's other business, and a town that has let a stranger sit at the money will let him sit at the business. He will want the house, Mr. Ashcroft. They always want the house at the last of it, and the wanting of it comes in at the door the town has opened to them, and the town has opened the door, and it did it with the rate, and it did not know it was the door."

"Who wants the house," I said.

"There is no who in it," said Margaret. "There is a sort of man that wants houses, and the sort has been in the town before, and my mother saw the sort, and the sort comes when a house has been kept long, and the keeping has been kept too long for the sort's liking, and the sort asks of the letters first, because the letters are the proof the house is kept, and the proof is what the sort wants, and the wanting of the proof is the beginning of the ending of the keeping. I have told you that before, in the way of it, and I tell it you now in the plain of it. Mind your keeping."

I went home with the plain of it, and the lamp came up at the end of the lane at the corner hour of nine, and I watched it from the window with the fire low, and understood that the house had kept itself eleven years, and the town had kept the house eleven years, and the sort had come into the town with the season, and had sat at the table of the rate, and had asked of the letters by a hand the postmaster did not know, and the keeping was come to the place where it would be asked of itself, and I was on the paper of the watch, and the watch went past the house on the Saturday, and I did not know yet whether the round kept the house or showed it.

I wrote it down under the day, and shut the book, and let the lamp burn.
