CHAPTER 50 — THE SECOND MARK

I returned to the fourth stone before first light.

The mark in the earth was gone. Not faded. Erased — smoothed over by a hand that had taken care to leave no trace of its own. I knelt and put my fingers to the ground. The earth was soft, recently pressed. Whoever had erased the mark had done so after I had left the mill the night before, after the meeting at the brewery, after I had sat at my desk writing the account.

They had come in the dark, while I was writing. They had smoothed the earth, and then they had gone, and I had not heard them.

I stood. There was a line of footprints in the soft ground beside the wall, leading away from the fourth stone, toward the mill-yard gate. Not the narrow heel I had seen before. A different print — wider, deeper. I followed them. They led to the mill-yard gate, where the earth was packed hard and the prints stopped.

I walked into the mill-yard. The mill stood silent. The door was closed. I had told Crowe I trusted no one. I had told the room above the brewery that I carried the gear. Now there was a man who had followed me from the brewery to the mill and erased my mark before I could read it again. Not Crowe. I knew it was not Crowe's print. This was a different man. And he had followed me.

I did not go to Crowe. I went to the vestry instead.

Before the sun was fully up, I was standing at the steps of St. Bartholomew's, waiting for the clerk. He came at half past seven, with his keys in his hand.

"You are early, Mr. Ashcroft."

"I have a question."

He unlocked the door. The vestry was cold. He lit a lamp and set it on the table where the parish books were kept. "The boundary," I said. "The old boundary. The line that does not follow the roads. I need to see the survey again — and anything else that records the marks on the stones."

The clerk did not move. "There is a mark on the old stones," he said slowly. "A circle with a line through it. The surveyors noted it, but they did not know what it was."

"I know that mark."

"Then why do you need to see it again?"

"Because I have found another mark. A line and three strokes. On the same stones. On the same door. I need to know whether it is in the survey."

The clerk was silent. "There is a mark that the surveyors did not draw. They drew the circle and the line. They left the other out of the record. I do not know whether they did not see it, or whether they chose not to draw it."

He turned to the shelf and took down a smaller book, its spine held together with thread. "This is the vestry book from the year the boundary was last moved. There is a page that the clerk who kept it did not show to the surveyors."

He opened it. The pages were yellow, the ink brown with age. He turned to a page near the end, where the writing was smaller, pressed into the margins. I read it. It was not a mark. It was a note. A line, and three strokes — the same line, the same strokes, drawn in the margin beside a list of names. Beneath it, in the same small hand:

*The keeper's sign. The one that is not spoken. The one that is older than the circle.*

The clerk was watching me. "Your predecessor came to me once, many years ago. He asked the same question. He asked whether the survey showed the second mark. I showed him this page. He looked at it for a long time, and then he told me never to speak of it again."

"Vale."

"Vale." The clerk closed the book. "He said it was not a mark for the Order to know. He said it was older than the Order, and that the Order had been built on top of it. He asked me to keep the book hidden, and I have kept it hidden."

"Why are you showing it to me?"

"Because he is dead. And you have come to ask the same question. The mark has appeared again. And if it has appeared, then the thing Vale was waiting for has begun."

I left the vestry with the image of the note in my mind — a line, three strokes, and the words: *the keeper's sign. The one that is older than the circle.* The circle was the mark on the gear. The circle was what the Order knew. The line and three strokes — the second mark — was older. That was what Vale had known and never told. That was what the man who had followed me had been trying to erase.

I went to the common. It was still early. The house was not there. I had not expected it to be. I stood at the edge of the common, in the wet grass, and I looked at the place where the house had stood. The grass was flattened — not in a square, but in a shape I had not noticed before. The shape of a door. A door, lying flat on the earth, pressed into the grass, as if the house had stood on it and the weight of the house had driven the shape of a door into the ground. I knelt. The shape was clear — a rectangle, narrow, the width of a man's shoulders. I put my hand to it. The grass was cool and damp. It had been pressed for some time. The shape pointed toward the mill.

I did not tell anyone what I had found. I went back to my rooms, sat at my desk, and opened my notebook. I wrote: The second mark is older than the circle. It is the keeper's sign. Vale knew it. The clerk kept the record. The Order does not know it. The grass where the house stood is pressed into the shape of a door. The door points toward the mill. I have been watching the wrong mark. I have been watching what the Order watches. I will watch the other now. I do not know what it opens. But it is older than the Order, and older than the Agreement — and I am the keeper.

I closed the notebook. I took the gear from my coat and held it in my hand. I looked at the mark — the circle, the short line — and I understood that I had been carrying it for the wrong reason. Not because the gear was wrong. But because I had thought it was the only thing. The circle was the lock. The line and three strokes — the keeper's sign — was what the lock was built to contain. I did not know what that meant. But Vale had known, and he had kept it from the Order, and he had died with it still unspoken. Whoever had followed me from the brewery, whoever had erased the mark from the earth, whoever had been watching me from the dark — they knew it too. The question was not what the mark was. The question was why they did not want me to see it.
