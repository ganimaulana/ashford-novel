# Chapter 24 — The Saturday Round and the Empty House

The Saturday came in with the snow gone off under a warm rain, the kind that makes a town smell of wet stone and smoke and all the things the frost had been keeping down, and the round was set for ten, and I did my part of the day as a man does the day before his turn, and let the round wait for its hour.

The lamp burned at nine on the Friday, and was carried away along the rooms in the old way, and no sound came; and I set it down as the ninth reading, and understood that the house kept its two books as the town kept its two books, and that the two books of the house were the light and the sound, and the two books of the town were the paper and the silence, and I did not know yet which of the four was the true one, and whether any of them was kept true.

I slept between the two nines, because that is what the watch does, and I was up at the quarter to ten, and the rain had stood off by then, and the lane was wet and dark, and I took my lantern and went out at my own door, and went the round as it was set for me, from my door to the mill-yard gate corner, and back.

The round went as rounds go. The lane was the lane, and the shutters were the shutters, and the church stood in its place with the steeple over it, and the gate stood at the corner, and I came by it at the quarter past ten, and the gate was shut, and the padlock on it, and I took that in as a man takes in what is right, and went on, and came round again at the quarter to eleven, and the gate was shut, and the padlock on it, and I took that in as a man takes in what is right twice, and went on.

And at the corner hour of eleven, as I came round the third time, the gate stood open, and the padlock hung on the hasp, and the hasp drawn.

I stood there a moment, but I did not go in—a round is for watching, not entering. I held the lantern at the opening and saw only the black, wet yard, empty and still. I shut the gate, replaced the padlock, and continued my round, writing in my book:

*Gate open at eleven, padlock unhung; shut it again; no person seen; no light in the yard. Third round.*

And I understood, as I went on with the round, that the gate had not opened itself, because a padlock does not come off its hasp by the weather, and that a man had been at it, and had come out of it or gone into it, and had left it standing, and had left it standing at the hour of the bell's going, and I did not know which of the two it was, and whether the man was in the yard yet or out of it, and whether the gate was kept for the man's going in or for his coming out.

---

The round came home at the quarter to twelve, and I stood at my own door with the lantern in my hand, and looked at the end of the lane, because that is what the round ends at, and what the round does not end; and the house stood there in the wet dark, with its black windows and its rosemary, and no light in it, because the light had been carried away an hour before, and the house was keeping itself as it kept itself, and I went in at my own door, and set the lantern down, and did not go up the stair.

I sat with the book, and put the three things of the night together: the gate at eleven, and the lamp carried away at ten, and the sound not come at any hour; and I understood that the alternation had broken again, because the sound had not come on the lamp's night, and the gate had opened on the lamp's night, and the opening of the gate was the third thing, and the three things stood together as the three papers stood together about the bell, and did not say the same thing of the night.

And there came, at a little past one, as I was on the point of the candle, the sound I had heard twice before this and had come to know as a man knows the voice of a house: once, and after the pause, twice, low and full and at the ground, and moving along the lane toward the corner, along the water and the stone.

It came on the lamp's night. It came after the gate. It came at the hour of the house's own deep quiet, and I stood at the window with the candle in my hand, and looked out at the lane, and saw nothing in it, and heard nothing more, and set the candle down, and wrote:

*The bell came at one, on the lamp's night, after the gate opened at eleven. The alternation is broken; or the bell and the lamp are not two hands but one hand, and the hand was at the gate first. I do not know which. The round kept the lane and did not keep the gate, and the gate is kept for something that comes and goes, and I do not know whether it came or went tonight.*

---

The Sunday came in with a clear frost after the rain, and the town walking to church on the hard road, and I walked the middle of the lane as the way is, and came by the end house, and took in the rosemary, and gave it out again, and went on to the church with the town, and did the not-seeing as the town does it, with the grace of long practice.

And at the door going out, the verger gave me my hat, and stood so that the giving of the hat took the length of a word.

"The gate, sir," he said, low. "The yard man found it shut this morning, with the padlock to it, and he says it was open when he came to the yard at six, and shut again by the time he came back with his key. He has told the constable. The constable has noted it."

"Noted," I said.

"Noted," said the verger. "And I have told you, sir, because you keep the round, and the round shut it, and the yard man will say it shut itself, and the constable will say the wind, and the bench will not say, and that is the four tellings of it, and you may keep them as you keep the other things."

I thanked him, and went home by the middle of the lane, and understood that the gate had been told four ways before the noon of Sunday, and that the telling of a thing in a town is the keeping of it, and that the town would keep the gate four ways, and would not keep it one way, and that the one way was in my book, and nowhere else in the town.
