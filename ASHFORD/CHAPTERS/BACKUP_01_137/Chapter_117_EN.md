CHAPTER 117 — THE CONSTABLE BEFORE VALE

I looked for Marcus Ferrand again.

I had looked for him once before, in the third test, and I had found nothing — no trace, no grave, no record after the year of his retirement. I had concluded that he had been deliberately removed from the records.

I had been right.

But now I knew that he had used the reading room. I knew that he had been a member of the society. I knew that he had been the constable before Vale.

I went to see Calloway.

He was in his office, at his desk, and he looked up when I came in.

"You have been asking about Ferrand again," he said.

"I have."

"I thought you had finished with that name."

"I had," I said. "Then I found something. He used a room above the chandler's shop. A room with a mark on the floorboard. A room the society took after his death."

Calloway was silent a long moment.

"The room," he said slowly. "The room above the chandler's shop. I have known it was there for years. I could not yet tell Ferrand used it."

"He was a constable," I said. "He was also a member of a society. And he used that room."

Calloway looked at me.

"How do you know he was a member of a society?"

"Because the society took the room after his death," I said. "And because I was given his name, in a test, by the society."

Calloway stood.

He went to the cabinet at the back of the office. He took out a key. He unlocked a drawer that I had not seen opened before.

He took out a box.

"This is the box of the constables," he said. "It was passed to me by Vale, and to Vale by Ferrand, and to Ferrand by the man before him. It holds the things that are not in the records — the things that the constable keeps because they cannot be written down."

He set the box on the desk.

"I have not opened it," he said. "Vale told me not to open it, unless there was a reason. I think you have given me a reason."

He opened the box.

Inside, on a bed of dark cloth, lay a notebook.

Not the red book. Not the book of the first keeper. A different notebook — old, bound in dark leather, its pages yellow with age.

Calloway did not touch it.

"It is Ferrand's," he said. "It was in the box when Vale passed it to me. I have never opened it."

I looked at the notebook.

I reached out. I took it.

It was heavy in my hands.

I opened it.

The first page was not a page of words. It was a page of marks.

The same marks as the tally.

Rows of marks, in the same hand, running across the page.

I stood very still.

The tally from the book of the first keeper.

The marks from the oldest book of the Academy.

And now the same marks, in Ferrand's notebook.

"The marks," Calloway said. "You know them."

"I know them," I said. "There is a page of the same marks in the book of the first keeper. The first keeper wrote that they are the count of the years."

"And Ferrand kept them too," Calloway said.

I looked at the notebook.

Ferrand had kept the count.

The constable before Vale had kept the same count that the first keeper had kept.

I did not know what that meant. But I knew that it was connected.

---

I read Ferrand's notebook that night.

It was not the account of the first keeper. It was not the record of the boundary. It was the notebook of a constable — a man who had watched the town for fifteen years, and who had kept the things that could not be written down.

He had kept the count.

Page after page, the marks ran on, in the same hand, one mark for each year, kept since the first year.

And among the marks, in the margins, he had written notes.

*The count stands at the same number. It has not changed in my time.*

*The stones do not move. The count does not change. The boundary holds.*

*I do not know what the count is counting. But I know that it is the same count that the keeper before me kept, and the keeper before him.*

I read the notes.

Ferrand had kept the count. He had watched it. He had written that it had not changed in his time.

And he had written that he did not know what it was counting.

I turned the pages. The marks ran on. The notes grew fewer.

And on the last page of the notebook, there was a single line, in a hand that had grown tired with years:

*I have kept the count. I have not understood it. But I have kept it, the way the keepers before me kept it, and the way the keeper who comes after will keep it.*

*The count is the thing. And the keeper is not.*

I closed the notebook.

I sat in the dark.

The count was the thing. And the keeper was not.

The same words the first keeper had written. The same words I had written in the red book.

Ferrand had kept the count. He had not understood it. And he had kept it, the way the keepers before him had kept it.

And he had written that the keeper who comes after would keep it too.

I was the keeper who came after.

I sat in the dark, with Ferrand's notebook before me, and I understood that I was not the first keeper to wonder what the count was counting.

And I would not be the last.

But I would find out.

I opened the red book, and I wrote:

*Ferrand kept the count. He kept it for fifteen years. He did not understand it, and he kept it, the way the keepers before him kept it.*

*He wrote: "The count is the thing. And the keeper is not."*

*I am the keeper who came after.*

*I have kept the count. I have not understood it.*

*But I will find out what it is counting.*

*I have Ferrand's notebook. I have the tally. I have the marks of the Academy. I have the first sign.*

*And I will learn the count.*
