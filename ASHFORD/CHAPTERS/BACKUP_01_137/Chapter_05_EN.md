# Chapter 5 — The House That Was Not There

That morning I did not go straight to the café.

I was still thinking about the conversation with Arthur the day before.

Not because there had been anything strange about it.

Quite the opposite.

I liked the way Arthur spoke about history.

He did not recite dates the way Daniel did.

He spoke about places.

About street names.

About how people called a place by a different name from its official one.

I took out the map of Ashford I had bought a few days earlier.

I spread it open on the dining table.

High Street.
Bellweather Lane.
The stop.
Margaret's café.
Arthur's shop.

I tried to recall the routes I had already walked.

Then I noticed something.

There was one part of the town I had never seen.

The district to the east of the town centre.

I had no particular reason to go there.

So I did not go.

I folded the map.

Today I chose to walk to the café.

After several days, I was beginning to understand that the map of Ashford I had bought was not merely a collection of lines and street names. The map showed the town as it ought to be recognised by a newcomer: the main streets, the residential districts, the stop, the town centre, and a few important buildings.

Yet the longer I stayed here, the clearer it became that the map did not tell everything.

It did not show the small shop on the corner.

It did not show the eating-house where people met every morning.

It did not show the old house that had changed its purpose.

And of course it did not show the places known only through people's stories.

Perhaps that was what Arthur meant.

There was a difference between the name printed on the map and the name that lived in people's heads.

---

Margaret's was already fairly busy when I arrived.

Margaret was speaking with a woman near the counter.

I did not know her.

The woman held a cup of tea in both hands.

"I don't know what I ought to do," she said.

Margaret leaned back a little.

"If you've already tried speaking to him, perhaps now is the time to stop chasing him."

"I have tried."

"How many times?"

"Three."

Margaret nodded.

"Then that's enough."

The woman smiled faintly.

"You always make it sound simple."

"Because sometimes it is simple."

I took a seat.

Margaret saw me.

"The usual?"

"I suppose so."

"Coffee?"

"Yes."

She nodded and returned to the woman.

I opened the newspaper.

Before long, the woman left.

Margaret brought my coffee.

"Arthur sends his regards."

I looked up.

"He sends them?"

"He said his burger was safe."

I laughed.

"He also said something about Daniel."

"Naturally."

"He said Daniel once ate his birthday cake."

Margaret shook her head.

"Daniel really has no defence against free food."

"Did he deny it?"

"He wouldn't deny something that was true."

I smiled.

The conversation felt light.

Normal.

I liked mornings like this.

Outside the window, High Street was slowly filling with the activity of morning. A diligence stopped at the stand and several people got down. A shop worker pulled back the iron gate from the front of his shop. From the bakery across the road, the smell of freshly baked bread was occasionally carried on the wind.

I was beginning to recognise the pattern.

Ashford had its own clock.

Morning began with the shops opening their doors, the people going to work, those making for the Academy, and the residents who came to places like Margaret's for breakfast or to read the newspaper.

There was no single great event that made the town feel alive.

It was precisely the collection of small things that made it so.

---

A few minutes later, a man came in.

He carried a large folder.

Margaret recognised him at once.

"Simon."

The man raised a hand.

"I'll only be a moment."

"Everyone says that."

He smiled and sat down.

Margaret made him coffee without asking.

I watched her.

She knew people's orders.

She knew their habits.

She knew who came late.

Who was angry.

Who was looking for work.

Who did not want to be disturbed.

The café was like a place where the small pieces of Ashford's life came together.

I went back to reading the newspaper.

Simon carried a folder that was rather thick, and several sheets of paper showed at its edges. From the way he opened it, I guessed he worked with documents or property affairs.

That did not seem strange in Ashford.

Old houses changed hands.

Buildings were renovated.

Shops changed owners.

And every change left documents behind.

Contracts.
Addresses.
Names.
Notes.

Perhaps that was why people like Margaret often knew who was searching for something.

A small town did not always need a large office to connect one person to another.

Sometimes a single eating-house and someone with a long memory were enough.

---

"Margaret."

Simon called from his table.

"Yes?"

"Do you still have the number of the man who deals with the empty house?"

Margaret stopped.

"Which empty house?"

"On Bellweather."

I lowered the newspaper.

Margaret asked:

"What number?"

Simon opened his folder.

"I don't know."

"Then which house?"

"The house near the end of the road."

Margaret gave a small laugh.

"That doesn't help."

Simon took out a sheet of paper.

"I was given this address."

He handed it over.

Margaret read it.

Then she frowned.

"That's strange."

"What?"

"There's no number."

Simon took the paper back.

"There is."

He pointed.

I looked at it from where I sat.

The address read:

Bellweather Lane

Then a name.

No house number.

I went back to the newspaper.

Nothing strange about it.

Many old addresses were indeed incomplete.

Simon said:

"They say the house is empty."

Margaret:

"Who says?"

"The person who lives next to it."

"Who?"

Simon gave a name.

Margaret nodded.

"I know the house you mean."

"Really?"

"I think so."

I turned.

Margaret looked at me.

"What is it?"

I pointed at the paper.

"Which house?"

"Why?"

"Because Bellweather Lane isn't very long."

Margaret thought.

"The house near the end of the road."

I smiled.

"That's the same answer he gave."

Simon laughed.

Margaret looked at me.

"Don't start."

I went back to the newspaper.

I thought of Bellweather Lane.

The street was indeed not long.

The journey from my house to High Street took only a few minutes on foot. Most of the houses already had clear numbers; some even had a small metal number fixed near the door or the gate.

For that reason, an address without a number felt a little odd.

Not impossible.

Only impractical.

Especially if someone was trying to find one particular house.

---

The conversation ought to have ended there.

But about twenty minutes later, when Simon had gone, Margaret came to my table.

"Are you curious?"

"A little."

"Why?"

"Because an address without a number is usually not very useful."

"True."

"And you said earlier you knew the house."

Margaret nodded.

"I think I know it."

"You're not certain?"

"I've heard of an empty house there."

"How long has it been empty?"

Margaret thought.

"I don't know."

I waited.

She smiled.

"Don't expect me to know everything."

"I don't."

"Good."

She went back to the counter.

I went on reading.

But a few minutes later I closed the newspaper.

There was something that did not fit.

Not something supernatural.

Only simple logic.

If the house was empty and stood on Bellweather Lane, why did Simon not have a house number?

I remembered the walk home the day before.

I had passed almost the whole length of the street.

I knew several of the houses.

My house.
The house next to it.
The house with the white fence.

I did not remember an empty house at the end of the road.

Perhaps I had simply not paid attention.

That was possible.

I opened the newspaper again.

There was no reason to think about it.

---

Towards midday I went home.

I passed along Bellweather Lane as usual.

This time I noticed the houses along the street.

One.
Two.
Three.

I counted without meaning to.

My house:
13.

Opposite it:
12.

The next house:
11.

I stopped.

At the end of the road stood an old house.

Its wooden fence was nearly fallen.

Its windows were closed.

There was no lamp.

No carriage.

I looked for a house number.

There was none.

I moved a few steps closer.

The number plate was blank.

Only two small holes remained where something had once been fixed.

I looked across the street.

House number 13 was visible from here.

I looked back at the old house.

So there was an empty house after all.

I took out my notebook.

I wrote:

Empty house at the end of Bellweather Lane.

I stopped.

Then I added:

No house number.

I closed the book.

There was no reason to go in.

I went home.

On the way back, I realised just how different the empty house was from the other houses on Bellweather Lane.

Most of the houses on the street were old, but still looked after.

There were potted plants by the front doors.

There were carriages in the yards.

There were curtains drawn open every morning.

There were dustbins set out on a particular day.

The empty house had none of those signs of life.

No newspaper at the front door.

No letter box that seemed to be used.

No porch lamp.

Its wooden fence was not entirely fallen, but no one seemed to care to repair it.

The house was not merely old.

The house looked as if it had already stopped being part of the neighbourhood.

---

In the afternoon I opened the map again.

I found Bellweather Lane.

I looked at the house numbers printed on the map.

11.
12.
13.

No house after 13.

I took up a pencil.

I drew a small line at the end of the street.

Perhaps the house had been built after the map was made.

I turned the map over.

There was no date of publication.

I sighed.

I was beginning to make a problem out of something that was actually simple.

I put the map away.

Then I opened it again.

This time I looked at another part.

Bellweather Lane ended not far from a small road that led to a quieter district.

On the map, that part had no particular name.

Only a short line leading to an area of old houses.

I compared it with what I had seen earlier.

The empty house was indeed there.

The map did not say the house did not exist.

Only, the map also did not show its number.

I closed the map.

One more thing to remember:

A map is not always complete.

Arthur had already said something similar in a different way.

I was beginning to understand what he meant.

---

Night came.

I was reading when I heard a knock at the front door.

I went down.

Helen stood outside.

She was carrying some shopping.

I opened the door wider.

"Shopping?"

"Yes."

I took one of the bags.

"Let me."

Helen handed it over.

I carried it to the kitchen.

After setting it down, I asked:

"Helen."

"Yes?"

"Is there an empty house at the end of Bellweather Lane?"

She stopped.

Not for long.

Only a moment.

"Why?"

"I saw it earlier."

"The old house?"

"Yes."

Helen opened the cupboard.

"Don't go there."

I looked at her.

"Why?"

"It's been empty a long time."

"That isn't a reason."

Helen closed the cupboard.

"Leon."

Her tone changed.

More serious.

"Don't go into a house that isn't yours."

I smiled a little.

"I don't intend to go in."

"Good."

She went up the stairs.

I stood in the kitchen a few seconds.

I did not feel suspicious.

Helen had only given sound advice.

An empty house.

Old.

Perhaps unsafe.

No need to enter it.

I went back to the reading room.

But there was one thing that troubled me.

Helen had not asked which house.

She had not asked how I had found it.

She had known at once which house I meant.

I stood a few seconds in the reading room.

Perhaps it meant nothing.

Bellweather Lane did not have many old houses at the end of the road.

If a person had lived here a long time, perhaps it was easy to guess.

All the same, I made a note of it in my memory.

Not as proof.

Only as a detail.

---

The night grew later.

Around eleven o'clock I was about to go to bed.

I passed the window on the second floor.

Without meaning to, I looked out.

The old house at the end of the road was visible from there.

Dark.

I almost walked past it.

Then I stopped.

There was something in one of its windows.

A light.

Very small.

Like lamplight from a room on the second floor.

I stood closer to the window.

The light went out.

I waited.

It did not appear again.

I looked at the clock.

It did not matter.

Perhaps it was only a reflection of the streetlamp.

I drew the curtain.

Then I stopped again.

I opened the curtain.

The light was still not there.

I sighed.

Perhaps it was only a reflection.

That night the silhouette did not merely pass the window. It slowed, as if looking in, before the dark took it. I opened the curtain at once. The street was empty; the lamp was steady. I wrote the time, and added a line I had not written before: *not wind. Not a branch.*

I went into my room.

From the second floor I could see most of Bellweather Lane.

The streetlamps stood at almost equal distances along the pavement. Some houses had porch lamps burning. A carriage passed along the main road and then vanished around the bend.

The houses that had looked alive earlier were now almost all dark.

That was what made a single point of light in the empty house seem so clear.

Yet when the light went out, nothing remained.

Only the old house.

Dark windows.

And the road, quiet once more.

---

I turned the paper over.

On the back there was older handwriting. Not as neat as the writing on the front. Rougher. As if written in haste, or by a hand not accustomed to writing a name.

I could make out a few faint words:

Ashcroft...
Bellweather...

I stared at it for a few seconds.

The name Ashcroft had appeared again and again over the past three weeks: the partial will from Pembroke & Clarke, the documents attached to it, the inventory I could not understand. Now it was on the back of a sheet of paper found in front of an empty house.

This was not coincidence.

Or at least, I wished to believe as much.

I put the paper back into its envelope. Then I opened the notebook.

But before I wrote, I drew another sheet of paper from the cover of the book.

That was where the letter from Pembroke & Clarke lay. I took it from my pocket, laid it beside the paper I had just been examining.

Pembroke & Clarke.
A partial will.
Aurelius Ashcroft.
Bellweather Lane.

One name, again and again.

One name I did not remember, but which seemed to connect everything.

I held the letter. The letter from the solicitor which said that my name — Ashcroft, though not my first name — was entitled to this property. And I, who had come out of curiosity and a few other things I do not care to discuss here, found that the name Ashcroft seemed to cling to Ashford.

Aurelius Ashcroft.

I looked at the name three times.

Once for the letter from the solicitor.

Once for the writing on the back of the paper I had found.

Once for the name that might have belonged to the person who once lived in the empty house.

I marked the page of the notebook.

Later. I would find out who Aurelius Ashcroft was.

And before going home, I walked to the library.

Arthur had said a street name meant more than what was printed on a map. Perhaps it was the same with a person's name: one that appeared again and again, unremarked, held a story that had not yet ended.

I put the envelope into my pocket.

This time I wrote it down.
