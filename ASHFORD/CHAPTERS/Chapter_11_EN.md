# Chapter 11 — A Round of the Watch

The watch-list, being made up in haste and signed in a coffee-house, was posted on the church door by Monday and read out by the constable on Saturday morning — this latter circumstance being, I understood, because nothing about the watching of Ashford in 1816 would be hurried, and the constable had a right to be skeptical of a paper invented before it had been argued about twice.

He was a square man of sixty, who kept one bobby, on loan from the parish by rotation for the market, and wore his staff like a tool he was embarrassed by. When he read the names, he read them as a clerk reads the rolls: aloud, evenly, and with a particular flatness on the names of men he was not obliged to arrest, which he had reason to remember. He read: "Bell weather lane — Ash-croft."

I had signed. Michael had signed first. The old soldier, when Michael proposed him, had stood up straighter than the room, which is a thing a man can do on an empty stomach; and so he signed third, with the hand that remained to him; and the clerk who wrote it down wrote him as "Hobbs, late of the fife."

That was the watch of Bellweather lane on its first day of paper: the constable, the verger, Michael, Hobbs, and myself, and no one about us, all the week, but seemed to think that this was rather well than otherwise, and I had been in the town eleven days.

And yet, walking home to breakfast, I could not feel that the list meant what Margaret had meant at the pump: the names against the price. It meant instead the names against the names. The same names had stood on paper all their lives at the rate-book and the poor-rate and the militia-ballot; but the watch-roll was a paper with a new quality: it did not divide the town into those who paid and those who were paid, it divided us into those who would be asked, and standing with them; — and there is no middle to that division, and it does not wash out.

---

Arthur did not sign.

He told me so at the shop door, before Margaret's was open, with the dryness he keeps for the weather of other men's affairs. "I was offered," he said. "I said no. A man with a shop keeps his watch behind a counter, and he has had one these thirty years, and it's kept."

"They are not asking you to sit up, sir. Only to put a hand to the week when it comes."

"Yes," he said. "And the first thing the week asks is who it belongs to. No: that is what a book does, and what a roll does, and that is the difference between a book and a man, and it is the difference he has to answer for." He saw my face. "You will think my reasons better in three more years, Mr. Ashcroft."

"I think them now, sir. I signed anyway."

"Of course you did," Arthur said, and he took out the shop key and did not look up again, and the door stood open behind him on that smell of paper with the rain cold off it, and I went to school and the shop did not take a word of it, and I have written none of the exchange into this book before this morning for the reason that I did not know how it belonged.

---

Walking home, I saw Hobbs on the High Street.

He stood at the corner where the lane turns toward the mill-yard, looking up at the houses the way a man counts windows when he has lost track of which one is his. He did not see me at first. His good hand was raised slightly, as if measuring something invisible against the roofline.

When I came close enough to speak, he turned.

"Mr. Ashcroft."

"Hobbs."

He lowered his hand. For a moment he looked as though he might say something, then thought better of it. I waited. A man who has learned to speak carefully will not speak at all if given the choice.

"You signed the watch," I said.

"I did, sir."

"Do you know the lane well?"

He looked past me toward Bellweather Lane, then back. "I know it as well as any man that has walked it eleven years without finding what he was looking for."

"And what were you looking for?"

Hobbs did not answer directly. Instead, he nodded toward the end of the lane, where the roofs stood low against the grey sky. "There are houses a man walks past every day and never sees, sir. And there are houses a man sees once and cannot stop seeing."

He said it plainly, without looking at me, and I understood that this was not philosophy but experience.

"The end house," I said.

"The end house." He adjusted the staff under his arm. "Good day to you, sir."

He walked on before I could say more. I watched him go, his stride uneven but determined, a man who had learned to carry himself with one arm and did so without complaint.

I stood there a moment longer, looking up at the same roofline he had been measuring. Whatever Hobbs had been counting, I could not see it. But I had the sense that he had been counting for a long time, and that he was not finished yet.

It belongs here. Daniel's scraped page is in my drawer; the trust-roll has been broken and mended in the town's own keeping at a date within the clerk's own memory; and I have this morning to learn that the man who keeps thirty years of the lane's own papers behind a counter declined to sign a list whose principal business is the lane.

I am not making anything of it beyond the writing of it. A man who declines a watch-list in the year of the corn-price has one thousand reasons that are all his, and I set the fact and the date and let him keep them out of the hundred of them that do not want me. But I did think of it at Margaret's, and I said nothing to Daniel, who was at the counter with the Saturday page in his sleeve.

---

The map in a new town is a book of lies you forgive. It shows the streets as though they joined what they touched, and it drew the lane ending at the house and the garden ending at the wall and the town ending where the coach-road began; and it was only eleven days in, and only a morning's walk toward the eastern end of things, that I found what all of it was standing on: the lane does not end at the house; it passes it.

There is a footpath behind the wall of Marlowe's garden, and it is not on the map, and it carries on between the garden and the next wall — which is the garden of the house that is not empty — down to the gate at the mill-yard corner, which is the end of what I had called the town until that hour.

I walked it slowly, in the morning, because I saw no reason in the world that a stranger on a Saturday would not take the exercise, and had the honesty to want that reason to appear sound.

And the town — the real town, the back-of-it town, the town that the map's lies are made of: the town that lives in the lane at the end of the lane, that lives in the garden wall, and that had the wash out on a Saturday morning for the first time of me being alive in it, and no one of my people had any notion whatever, — was there.

Behind the wall of the empty house, facing the mill-gate where the map ended, is the garden, and the garden, — it was the thing I could not make round — the garden was kept.

Not dressed: kept. A thing dressed is meant to be seen; kept is meant to be had. I looked, past the rails of the Marlowe gate, at a square of ground the size of a room: beds, but in squares, as people grow food and not flowers; the soil turned over; and a long border; there were beans and cabbage in a line together, and a frame for peas that was too old for the peas; and in the shelter of the wall, on a line put up, as I could tell with the glass, to be seen from no road in the world, a cloth in the smoke-water hung to dry; and one little window in the wall — the size of a cat; a cat-window in a garden wall, in a place where a cat can want to get and no eye from no road of the town can see it get.

I stood a long time there, and nothing about it had anything to say to nothing, and I did not walk in and there was no way into a kept garden but through a door of the house that was not there, and I had not the wish: — it had been the other way. And when I looked at the garden wall and the way it joined onto the house itself, then I knew why the wall of the house looked the newer stone; it was not newer stone; a piece had come out to be let out.

There had lived, in that kept garden and in those rooms above that the shutters had shut, a person, and no one in the town had "thought anything" of that person in ten years, — and there was a cloth on a line to prove it against the parish books, and there was no ghost whatever concerned; and my whole trouble was this other trouble: a man living a whole life behind a wall, in the middle of a town, and the town agreeing with itself, — and then to keep on agreeing, — and me not to find that out, and to have the finding out begin to happen in eleven days.

---

The first watch on the Bellweather round was held that night, and the meeting-place of the watching was the post-office door, at the hour of the lamps, and this was arranged without a word to me, and was the first evening that I saw the thing plainly I could remember: the whole round was a social round: Michael had the lantern, Hobbs had the one arm and the staff he held, and it made him six foot.

And when a thing is social the social part is watched before the other part is watched, and Hobbs took two turns past the Marlowe gate before the verger was aware; and then the four of us went past the church, down the hill, up again by the water-gate and back, — and the last lap, the whole of it of a night along the whole of Bellweather-lane, with our own steps and not one to be spoken of, and no one spoke about a thing in this lane at that time of a night, and that is exactly what watching at the lamps is.

And when the lamps were lit and the light in the top window of the end of the lane came on at the hour of it, — the same hour it had come on, I have had it since entered in the book, the same hour both of the previous nights, — Hobbs had seen it before the verger had.

There were two ways to see a thing, as a man at that hour, — he that had served and had been the fife; the verger; — and Hobbs, at the light's coming up; then: — "Is that a light over the empty house, sir," he asked the verger, "and how long has it been empty, sir?" and the verger: — "Forty year, I should say, sir, to know the bell" and Hobbs, with a face turned to the light and no more words whatever, — he then stood with his back to the wind, and spoke: —

"My man there was a light; my wife, her sister, she kept it; and then it was a light that she herself was kept; they said that she was mad, and it was a keeping. A light that is not wanted can be a light wanted to be seen, sir; you mark where the shutters are: — there's the one that is a-plank'd, and the other is a-window, and the light is not a-light that is seen through a window, — it is a-plank'd at the back o' the light; I have seen it done with my own two; the light is wanted there to be seen by that same one that passes by; and it's the one that is a-plank'd there that is kept from the glass by the one that is there."

He was right, and I could not see it at that distance by any glass of mine, and the verger could, and he said nothing whatever, — he was a churchman and had the two keys, and the constable said nothing, and the constable was the man in office and had the staff, and Michael went past, and back, and past a third time, and I was not able to walk any of the length of that lane of mine without a whole party that did not intend to go out of it; — and the light was put out, and the watching of the round did not move.

"We enter a private house, when we have no call from it," said the constable, "and there is no call. And the man that is kept of the one that lives, there is no writ for."

"Is there a complaint?" said the verger.

"There's a sight," the verger said, "and it's been a sight eleven years to the maps, and I have seen it, — and the book is here: — noted; a light seen; no complaint."

So the book said, and the town's new paper had existed eight-and-forty hours before it began what it was made for, and I do not intend to describe how I felt at that, in the manner, for that the whole of it was in how the whole of the writing of it felt, — which is a thing that is done in the town's name by the town's man, and done honestly, and I had no complaint. It was the town's book, and the book had all our names to it, and there, upon that paper, was the very first word against our watch, and that was: —

There is nothing to see here.

It was Hobbs that said it, on the way home. He had kept with me by accident, on his walk to the poor-house on High Street. He had not asked to walk with me; it is the way of a man who has no wife and no regiment, who keeps to the town's own paths. When I offered him the name of a street to go along, he said, "There is a light kept there these eleven years, sir, and it has wanted no one's looking to, till there comes one."

It was the first plain thing he had spoken to me of. I stopped to note it in my book: that a man who has not spoken of anything in eleven years will not speak of it to a stranger by accident.

When we got to the poor-house door, he turned.

"It's the bell you heard, sir, and not the bell."

I waited.

"There's no bell there. But there is a rope, sir." His hand moved slightly, as if pulling something down. "A rope that has been a bell in a man's house. There is no bell, as you will find, in a house where a rope is wanted. There is, when there is a hand that is going up in the dark."

He said it plainly, without ceremony.

"Good night, sir."

There being nothing to be answered, I wished him good night, and he went in.

I stood in the lane at the gate of my own house, with the book shut. I stood there long. The whole town's agreement with itself was the one thing in it that was the whole town's. It took a man of the fife, eleven years silent, to say it plain to the watch.

There was nothing to see. And the town would be the same thing when I knew it.
