CHAPTER 136 — THE ROAD

I did not stay in the town.

I left the house on the hill in the morning, and I walked through the town, past the market square, past the church, and I did not stop. The medallions were warm against my chest — three of them now, three first signs, the warmth of the keeping steady and constant. The count had called me north, to the marsh and to the house on the hill, and I had come. And now the count was calling again, and the warmth did not point north, and I walked south.

I did not know why I walked south.

It was not a decision, exactly. It was more like the way the marsh had led me — a direction that presented itself, a road that seemed to open, a sense that this was the way. The count had led me north, and the count had not undone that leading; it had simply turned, and the medallions had turned with it, and I had followed the warmth as I had always followed it, without asking where it came from or where it meant to take me.

The country changed as I walked south.

The land rose and fell. The fields gave way to woods, and the woods to fields, and the fields to villages, and the villages to towns, and I walked through each of them and did not stop. The people of the south looked at the man from the north the way people always look at a stranger — briefly, with a question they do not ask, and then not at all. They had their own lives, their own keepings, their own silences. And I was a stranger to them, a man walking south with three medallions against his chest and a question he had not yet learned to say.

I did not stop to explain myself. I did not stop to ask for directions. There was nothing to explain and no one to ask — the count was not a direction you could ask a man for. I walked, and the medallions were warm, and I followed the warmth.

---

On the third day I came to a crossroads.

A signpost stood at the crossing, its arms pointing to towns I had not seen and towns I had passed and towns I did not know. I stood at the crossroads, and I did not know which way to go.

I took out the medallions.

I held them in my hands — three of them now, three first signs, warm from my chest. I held them up, and I waited, the way I had always waited at a threshold. In the marsh the warmth had led me unerringly to the house. On the road north it had grown warmer and warmer as I approached the house on the hill. It had always told me where to go. It had always, in the end, shown me the way.

The medallions did not change.

I stood at the crossroads, in the grey light, with the medallions in my hands, and I waited for the warmth to lean one way or the other — toward the north, or the south, or the east, or the west. It did not. The warmth was steady, the same in every direction, and it told me nothing.

I put the medallions back in my coat.

I looked at the signpost. The arms pointed to the four quarters of the world, and I could have taken any of them, and the medallions would have been just as warm on any road. I stood at the crossroads, and I understood, slowly, the way I had understood the marsh and the house and the division of the count — that the count was not calling me to a place.

It was calling me to a choice.

The count had led me to the marsh, and to the house on the hill, and to the medallion of the keeper before the first keeper, and it had shown me the way at every turn. Now it had stopped showing me the way. It had brought me to the crossroads, and set the warmth at a level it would not raise or lower, and left the choosing to me. The count did not care which road I took — or it cared, but the caring was in the choosing, not in the road.

I stood at the crossroads, in the grey light, and I chose.

I chose the south road.

I did not know why. I did not know whether it was right. The medallions gave me no sign, no warmth, no leaning. I chose the south because it was the road that seemed to open, because the land beyond it called to me in a way I could not name, because the count had led me north and north and north, and because I had begun to understand that the journey was mine to make. I chose the south, trusting that the bell would ring when the count was ready, and that the road, whatever it was, would be the right one.

I walked south.

---

For a week I walked.

The land grew warmer as I went south. The fields grew greener. The villages grew larger, and the towns grew busier, and the people in them moved faster, and I moved through them slowly, the way I had learned to move in the marsh — conserving, watching, keeping. The medallions were warm against my chest. They had not changed since the crossroads. They did not lean, they did not grow warmer, they did not tell me where I was going. They only were — warm, steady, constant, and I walked south, and I did not know what I was looking for.

I did not know whether I had chosen rightly.

That was the hardest part of the south road — the not knowing. In the marsh, the count had shown me the way, and I had followed it, and the following had been enough. On the south road, the count was silent, and the warmth was steady, and I could not tell whether I was walking toward the next marker or away from it, whether I was following the count or only the road.

But I walked.

I walked because the keeper follows the calling, and the calling had brought me to the crossroads, and I had chosen the south, and the choosing was done. I walked because there was nothing else I could do. I walked because the medallions were warm, and the warmth was the only compass I had, and even though it told me nothing, it did not leave me.

On the seventh day I came to a river.

It was a wide river, slow and deep, moving through the land the way it had moved for a thousand years. A bridge of old stone crossed it, its arches dark with age, its stones worn by a thousand years of feet and weather. I stood on the bridge, and I looked at the river.

The water was dark. The current was slow. The river moved as it had always moved, without hurry, without pause, without caring whether anyone crossed it or not. It had been here before the town, before the road, before the keeper before the first keeper had divided the count into thirteen. It would be here after all of it was gone.

I stood on the bridge, and I understood that I had come to the end of the road.

Not the end of the journey. The end of the road. The road south ended at the river. Beyond the river, on the far bank, another road began — a road I could see, leading away from the water into the country beyond. I did not know where it went. I did not know whether the count wanted me to cross.

I looked at the river. I looked at the road on the far bank. I looked at the medallions against my chest.

They were warm. They had not changed. They did not lean toward the far bank or toward the near one. They told me nothing — the same steady warmth, the same silence, the same absence of direction I had felt at the crossroads.

And I understood.

The count was not calling me to a place. It was calling me to a choice. It had called me to the crossroads, and I had chosen the south, and I had walked to the river. And now it had called me to the river, and the river had a choice in it — whether to cross, or to stay, or to turn back.

I stood on the bridge, in the grey light, with the medallions warm against my chest and the dark water moving below me, and I held the choice in my hands, and I did not yet know what I would do with it.

The river did not care. It had never cared. It had been here for a thousand years, and it would be here for a thousand more, and it did not care whether I crossed.

I was the one who decided.

And I did not yet know what I would decide.
