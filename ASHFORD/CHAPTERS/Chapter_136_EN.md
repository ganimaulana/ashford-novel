CHAPTER 136 — THE ROAD

I did not stay in the town.

I left the house on the hill, and I walked south, through the town, past the market square, past the church. I did not know why I walked south. The count had called me north. It had led me through the marsh, and to the house on the hill, and to the medallion of the keeper before the first keeper. I had the medallion now. I carried it with my own, three medallions against my chest, the first sign thrice. The count was still calling. I walked south. I did not know where I was going. I did not know what the count was calling me to. But I walked.

The country changed as I walked south. The land rose and fell. The fields gave way to woods, and the woods to fields, and the fields to villages, and the villages to towns. I walked through each of them, and I did not stop. I did not know what I was looking for. I did not know whether the count would show me a sign, or a house, or a person. I walked. The medallions were warm against my chest. All three of them. They had been warm since the night in the house on the hill, when the cold medallion had warmed beside the others. I walked south, and the medallions were warm, and I followed the warmth.

On the third day, I came to a crossroads. A signpost stood at the crossing. The arms pointed to towns I had not seen, and towns I had passed, and towns I did not know. I stood at the crossroads, and I did not know which way to go. I took out the medallions. I held them in my hands. They were warm. I held them up, one in each hand, and I waited. The medallions did not change. They did not grow warmer in any direction. They were warm, steady, the same warmth in every direction. I stood at the crossroads, and I did not know which way the count was calling.

I put the medallions back in my coat. I looked at the signpost. The arms pointed to the north, and the south, and the east, and the west. I did not know which way to go. I stood at the crossroads, in the grey light, and I understood that the count was not calling me to a place. It was calling me to a choice — a choice that would lead to the next sign, and the next, until the thirteen were gathered. I stood at the crossroads, and I chose. I chose the south road, trusting that the bell would ring when the count was ready.

I walked south for a week. The land grew warmer. The fields grew greener. The villages grew larger, and the towns grew busier. I walked through them, and I did not stop. The medallions were warm. They had not changed since the crossroads. I walked south, and I did not know what I was looking for. I did not know whether the count would show me anything. I did not know whether I had chosen rightly. But I walked.

On the seventh day, I came to a river. It was a wide river, slow and deep, with a bridge of old stone crossing it. I stood on the bridge, and I looked at the river. The water was dark. The current was slow. The river moved as it had moved for a thousand years, without hurry, without pause. I looked at the river, and I understood that I had come to the end of the road. Not the end of the journey. The end of the road. The road south ended at the river. Beyond the river, there was another road. I did not know whether I would cross.

I stood on the bridge, with the medallions warm against my chest, and I looked at the river, and the road beyond it. I did not know what I would do. I did not know whether the count wanted me to cross, or to stay, or to turn back. I stood on the bridge, in the grey light, and I waited. The medallions were warm. I did not know what they were telling me. I stood on the bridge, and I waited. And in the waiting, I understood. The count was not calling me to a place. It was calling me to a purpose. I had found the house in the marsh. I had found the medallion of the keeper before the first keeper. I had kept the keeping. And the count had called me to the crossroads, and I had chosen the south, and I had walked to the river. And now I stood at the river, and I did not know whether to cross.

I stood on the bridge, and I waited. And I understood that the waiting was the purpose. I had been a keeper. I had been a hunter. I had been a man who worked with a society. And now I was a man who stood at a river, waiting. I did not know what I was waiting for. But I knew that I would wait. The count had called me. I had come. And I would wait until the count called again.
