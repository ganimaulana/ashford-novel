# Chapter 8 - The Old Report

I walked home with the envelope still in my pocket.

The street was quiet. The lamps had been lit, one by one, as the light faded. I could hear the faint sound of a carriage somewhere in the distance, and the ordinary noises of a town settling into evening — a door shutting, someone calling from a window, the clatter of a bucket in a yard.

I had asked Daniel where to begin. He had given me a name.

The next morning I took the long way to the Market Square.

I did not go straight there. I walked through the lanes I had come to know in the last week — past the small shops, past the houses with their painted doors and tired windows, past the woman who set out her washing at the same time every morning, past the corner where the baker's cart stopped.

I had begun to notice that the town was made of these small repetitions. The same faces at the same times. The same sounds at the same hours. It was comforting, in a way. It made the town feel knowable.

It also made the things that did not fit stand out more.

I reached the Market Square as the morning was warming. The square was not large. It held a few shops, a tavern, the reading room where I had sat the afternoon before, and the buildings that belonged to the town — the constable's office among them.

The constable's office was a plain building. No sign that it was anything special. A door painted the same dull green as many of the others. A window with a faded curtain.

I stood outside it for a moment.

Then I went in.

A man sat behind a desk. He was older than I had expected. His hair was gray at the sides, and his face was the kind that looked as though it had spent years looking at things it would have preferred not to see. He was writing something on a sheet of paper, and he did not look up when I entered.

"There's a bell," he said, without raising his voice. "If you've come to ring it, there's one outside."

"I'm not here to ring anything."

He looked up then. His eyes were gray, like the stone in the square. He studied me for a moment, then nodded once.

"Calloway."

I gave my name.

He gave a short nod. "Sit down, if you like."

I sat.

The chair creaked. I shifted, and the envelope in my coat pressed against my ribs. For a moment the corner of it showed beneath the edge of my coat — a plain white rectangle against the worn wool.

He went back to what he was writing.

I waited.

After a while he set down his pen and looked at me. "Well?"

"I was told you might know something."

"By who?"

"Someone who thought I should come here."

Calloway's mouth moved as if he were about to speak, then stopped. He looked at me again. "And what did they think I'd know?"

"I don't know. That's why I came."

He leaned back in his chair. "You've been asking questions."

"I've been looking at things."

"That's not the same."

"No."

He held my gaze for a moment, then nodded. "All right. Tell me what you're looking for."

I had expected this. I had also not expected it. I told him what I could.

"There's a house on Bellweather Lane. At the end of it. It doesn't appear on the map I have. But it's there. And there's a record of it — an old one — that says it was there in 1791. But there's no number on the record. And the name on it is Bellweather House."

Calloway listened. He did not interrupt. When I had finished, he leaned forward and took a paper from a drawer. He spread it on the desk.

"It's not the first time someone's asked me about that lane."

I looked at the paper. It was a page from a book — an old one, judging by the yellowing. The writing was small and close together.

"When?" I asked.

"Years ago. Not many. Enough." He tapped the paper with one finger. "There've been reports. Odd ones. Not often. Not recent."

"What kind of reports?"

He looked at me. "You tell me. You've seen the house."

"I've seen the outside."

"That's enough for some people."

"What did they report?"

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "Lights. Sometimes. At the window. Doors found open. Things moved. That sort of thing."

I was quiet.

"And you think this is connected to whatever you've found?"

"I think it might be."

"That's not an answer."

"No."

He rolled the paper back up and put it in the drawer. "If you want to see the reports, I can show you. But they're old. And they don't say much."

"I'd like to see them."

"Tomorrow, then. After the morning round."

"I can come now."

He shook his head. "Not now. The morning round comes first. Come back after."

I nodded.

"One more thing," I said.

Calloway looked at me. "Yes?"

"Did you know about the name? Ashcroft."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then: "No. I don't know that name."

"Have you heard it before?"

"No."

I nodded. "Then I'll come back tomorrow."

I stood.

Calloway did not look up from his desk until I had reached the door. Then he said, without turning: "You'd do well not to carry that envelope around too openly."

I stopped.

I looked at him.

"You've been carrying it a while," he said. "In my experience, when someone carries something they can't explain for that long, it's either important or it's going to get them into trouble."

"I don't know which."

"Then keep it out of sight until you do."

I went out.

The bell above the door rang behind me.

I walked home.

---

That afternoon I sat in the reading room with my notebook open. I wrote:

Calloway. Constable. Office near Market Square.

He knew about Bellweather Lane. He had reports. Old ones. Lights. Doors found open. Things moved.

He did not know the name Ashcroft.

He told me to keep the envelope out of sight.

I closed the notebook.

I did not write what I had begun to think — that Calloway had known more than he said, or that he was warning me about something I did not yet understand.

I wrote only what he had said.

---

Later I went back to Margaret's. She was behind the counter when I arrived. She looked up.

"You look as though you've been talking to someone who knows more than he says."

"I went to see Calloway."

Margaret nodded. "And?"

"He knew about the house. He had reports. Old ones."

"And he knew about the envelope."

"I didn't show it to him."

"He knew you had it anyway."

I was quiet.

Margaret wiped the counter. "You'd do well to listen to him. Calloway has been in this town longer than he lets on. That's not the same as knowing everything. But it means he's seen things."

"What kind of things?"

"Things that don't fit. Things people don't report to the constable because they're afraid of what it would mean if they did."

I nodded.

"And the reports he has?" I asked.

"Some of them are true. Some of them are what people say when they're frightened. The trick is knowing which is which. Calloway has been doing this long enough to have an opinion. Whether his opinion is right is another matter."

I said nothing.

Margaret put down the cloth. "You wanted to know where to begin. You've begun."

"Yes."

"And?"

"I don't know yet."

"That's all anyone can say at the beginning."

She smiled, but it was not a kind smile. "Come back tomorrow. If you've been to see him again, you'll have something more to tell me."

"I will."

I left.

---

The next morning I returned to Calloway's office.

He was there when I arrived. He had a stack of papers on the desk — old ones, judging by the yellowing. He looked up when I entered.

"You came."

"I said I would."

He nodded. "Sit down."

I sat.

He spread one of the papers on the desk. It was a page from a constabulary record. The writing was old — older than the paper Arthur had shown me, maybe. The ink had faded.

"This is what I have," he said. "Reports from the last twenty years. Some mention Bellweather Lane. Some don't."

He turned the page. "Here. This one is from 1803. It mentions a light reported at the end of Bellweather Lane. The witness said it was in a window. The constable who took the report — that was someone else, not me — went to look. He found nothing."

I leaned forward. "Did he write down what he saw?"

"He wrote down that he found nothing. Which isn't the same as not finding anything."

I nodded.

Calloway turned another page. "Here. 1808. A report of a door found open. The witness said the house had been locked the night before. The constable who took this one — again, not me — went to check. He found the door closed. Locked. He wrote that down."

"What happened?"

"The report was closed. The witness was told there was nothing to worry about."

"What did the witness say?"

Calloway was quiet. "He said he knew what he'd seen. He didn't say what it was."

I was quiet.

Calloway turned another page. "Here. 1811. This one is different. It mentions a name."

I leaned forward. "What name?"

He did not answer at once. He read the page again. Then he looked at me.

"The report says that a man was seen going into the house. At night. The witness didn't get a clear look at him. But the name the witness gave — the name he thought he'd heard — was Ashcroft."

I stared at him.

Calloway did not move. "That's what the report says. Whether it's true is another matter."

"What did you do with this report?"

"It's here. In the records."

"Did you look into it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Calloway leaned back. "Because the witness was the one who made the report. And because the name didn't match anyone I knew. And because it was easier not to."

I was quiet.

"Easier not to what?"

"To ask questions. To go looking. To make a thing real by paying attention to it."

I looked at him.

He did not look away. "You came here because you found a paper with a name on it. You came because you think the house on Bellweather Lane is connected to that name. You came because you want to know whether someone has been going into that house."

"I did."

"And now you've heard that someone once reported a man going into that house, and the name was Ashcroft."

"It was."

"Do you see how this works?"

I did not answer.

Calloway leaned forward again. "You ask me about the house. I show you reports. The reports mention lights, and doors, and a name. You take that and you build something out of it. Maybe it's true. Maybe it isn't. But the building is yours, not mine."

"I understand."

"Do you?"

I did not answer.

He sighed. "I'm not trying to stop you from looking. I'm trying to tell you that what you find in these records is not the same as what happened. People see things. They report them. Sometimes they're right. Sometimes they're frightened. Sometimes they're lying. The record is what they said, not what was true."

"I know."

"Do you?" He looked at me for a long moment. Then he gestured to the papers. "Take whatever you want from these. Read them. But don't build anything out of them that you can't support."

I took one of the pages. The 1811 report. I held it carefully.

"Can I come back?" I asked.

"If you have something to ask. Not just to look."

"I'll have questions."

"Then come back."

I stood.

"One more thing," I said. "Did you know about the name before I told you?"

Calloway was quiet. "I knew the report existed. I didn't connect it to you."

"I see."

"That's all I can say."

I went out.

The bell rang behind me.

I walked home.

---

On the way I stopped at the café. Margaret was there. Daniel was not.

"You've been to see him," she said.

"Yes."

"And?"

I told her what I could. Not everything. But enough.

She listened. When I had finished, she said: "You have a report now. That's not the same as an answer."

"No."

"But it's something."

"Yes."

She nodded. "Then you're doing what you said you'd do. Gathering facts."

"I am."

"Good. Don't stop."

I said nothing.

"One thing," she said. "Calloway told me you came to see him."

I looked at her.

"He doesn't usually tell people I've been there. But he did today."

"What did he say?"

"He said you asked about the house. He said you had a paper with a name on it. He didn't say which name."

I was quiet.

"He also said," Margaret added, "that you should keep it out of sight. He was more direct than I expected."

"I noticed."

"He's not usually direct."

"No."

Margaret looked at me. "You should be careful who you tell about that paper. And about the envelope. There are people in this town who listen. Not everyone of them is a constable."

"I know."

"Do you?"

I did not answer.

She wiped the counter. "Come back tomorrow. If there's anything more to notice, you'll have noticed it by then."

"I thought you didn't know anything."

"I don't. But I know this. You'll find out more by being careful than by being quick."

I left.

---

That night I sat with my notebook. I wrote:

Calloway. Reports. 1803 — light at window. 1808 — door found open. 1811 — man seen going into house. Name given: Ashcroft.

I added:

Calloway did not know the name before I told him. But he knew the report.

I added:

Margaret knew I had gone to see him.

I added:

Neither of them said what they knew.

I closed the notebook.

I took out the envelope. I held it in my hand.

The paper inside. The two words. Ashcroft. Bellweather.

The envelope with no return address.

The reports at Calloway's office. A light. A door. A man going into the house. A name.

I put the envelope back in my pocket.

I went to the window.

The road below was empty. The streetlamps burned in the distance. One after another, they marked the line of the street without marking anything else.

I thought of the house at the end of the lane.

I thought of the light that had been reported in 1803.

I thought of the door found open in 1808.

I thought of the man seen going into the house in 1811.

I thought of the name.

I did not know whether any of it was connected.

I only knew that someone had been going into the house. Someone had been reported. Someone had been named.

And I had a paper with that name on it, found in my own house, with no explanation of how it got there.

I turned from the window.

I did not write what I had begun to suspect.

I wrote only what I had found.

---

I went to bed.

I lay there and listened to the town.

And I waited for the morning.
