# Chapter 8 — Bellweather House

That morning I did not go straight to the café.

I sat in the dining room with the letter from Pembroke & Clarke and the envelope from the other day on the table before me.

Both were still sealed.

I had already examined the front, the back, the folds, and the paper itself several times.

There was no name of a sender.

No address.

No postmark.

Nothing that could give me a clue.

In the end I put them into a folder.

If I kept looking at them, I would only make the same guesses over and over.

I took my coat.

Today I wanted to do something simpler.

I wanted to be certain of one thing.

That Bellweather House had really once existed.

I looked about the dining room.

This house still felt older than most of the buildings I passed every day in the town centre.

The wood around the windows had been repainted several times.

The door handles felt cold even when the fire was lit.

From outside came the sound of a carriage passing along Bellweather Lane, and now and then the sound of people walking toward the diligence stop.

I was beginning to grow used to this house.

Yet the longer I lived in it, the clearer it became that an old house was not merely a building that was older.

It kept the traces of its changes.

Old paint beneath new paint.

A door no longer used.

A room that had once served a different purpose.

And perhaps, like this town, a history that was not entirely visible.

---

I went to the town library.

The building was not very large.

From outside, it looked like an old building that had been renovated several times.

The library stood not far from the town centre and was easily reached from Market Square.

Its front still kept the old stone façade, but some of the windows and the door looked as though they had been replaced.

Near the door hung a notice board carrying the schedule of activities, information about the library's services, and a few announcements for residents.

The place did not feel like a dead archive building.

People came and went.

A child sat near a bookshelf.

An old man read a newspaper.

Two people carried stacks of books toward a work table.

The library was one of the places in Ashford where the past and daily life met without seeming strange.

I went in and made my way straight to the local reference section.

There were not many people.

A librarian sat behind a desk.

I approached.

"Excuse me."

He looked up.

"Yes?"

"I'm looking for old records about Bellweather Lane."

He pointed at a shelf to the left.

"The town archives are over there."

"The old buildings section?"

"Yes."

I thanked him and walked over.

I spent nearly an hour reading through the documents.

Maps.

Building lists.

Property records.

Changes of street names.

Most of it was not very interesting.

But all of it gave a clearer picture of Ashford.

Bellweather Lane, it turned out, had existed far longer than I had thought.

Some houses had kept the same number for decades.

Some had changed.

There were a few buildings that had been demolished.

And some that had been rebuilt.

Among those documents I began to see how Ashford had changed slowly.

A road that had once lain at the edge of town was now part of the residential district.

A house that had once stood alone had finally been surrounded by other buildings.

Some old buildings had vanished and been replaced by new structures.

Yet a street name often lasted longer than its buildings.

And so did a house number.

The change of a town did not always happen in one great event.

Sometimes it went on over decades, little by little, until the people who lived there scarcely noticed that the shape of their town had already changed.

---

I opened a property list from the end of the nineteenth century.

My forefinger moved along the rows of writing.

Then —

blank.

I turned the page.

On the next page there was another record.

A property was listed at the end of the road.

But its house number was not written.

Its owner's name was not given either.

Only one short note:

Property removed from the active list.

I read that sentence twice.

Removed.

Not demolished.

Not sold.

Not pulled down.

Removed.

I searched the other pages.

There was no explanation.

I took a pencil and noted the archive number.

Then I closed the book.

I did not feel afraid.

I only grew more curious.

I noted the term the document had used.

The active list.

The language felt administrative.

Dry.

As though a building could vanish simply because someone moved one line from one list to another.

There was no explanation of what had happened to the house.

No record of who had lived there afterwards.

No note of whether the building still stood.

Only its status had changed.

And that change of status made the house that had once existed feel like something slowly removed from the town's official memory.

---

I carried the archive number over to the librarian.

"Excuse me."

He looked at my note.

"Yes?"

"I'd like to see the document with this number."

He checked it in the index book.

A few seconds later he frowned.

"This document is not available."

"Damaged?"

"No."

"Then what?"

He turned the page.

"It seems it has already been moved."

"To where?"

He looked at the book a while longer.

"There is no record."

I nodded.

"Very well."

I did not ask further.

I had already gathered enough information.

Or at least enough to know that the record was indeed incomplete.

I looked at the catalogue book on the librarian's desk.

Its search system was simple. Some old documents still had to be searched by reference number or by the place where they were stored. Not every archive had been entered in the main register, and plainly not all information turned up in a single search.

In the year 1816, a document could exist somewhere without being found at once merely by opening the page of its name.

If the record was wrong.

If the archive number had changed.

If the document had been moved.

Someone had to search for it truly.

And sometimes, no one knew where a document had gone.

---

I left the library around midday.

I did not go straight home.

I went to Margaret's.

When I entered, Margaret was speaking with someone near the window.

She saw me.

"Leon."

I walked up to the counter.

"Coffee?"

"Yes."

"You look as though you're searching for something."

"I am searching for something."

Margaret smiled.

"And?"

"I found more questions."

"That is usually what happens."

She made the coffee.

I sat down.

Before long, the woman who had been speaking with Margaret left.

Margaret came to my table.

"The library?"

I nodded.

"How did you know?"

"The dust on your sleeve."

I looked at the sleeve of my coat.

Quite right.

There was a little dust.

"And you're carrying a pencil."

I looked at my hand.

I was still holding it.

Margaret smiled.

"You're beginning to look like Arthur."

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"Depends."

Outside the window, Market Square was beginning to grow busy.

Some people carried shopping bags.

A diligence stopped for a moment at the stand.

Two workers unloaded goods from a tradesman's cart.

On the notice board near the road, a few new sheets had been pasted over the old notices.

I was beginning to realise that the centre of Ashford held a kind of layer of time as well.

An old poster was not always taken down at once.

A new notice was pasted over it.

Shops kept their old signboards even when the owner changed.

Buildings were renovated without erasing the whole of their earlier shape.

Perhaps that was why the town felt old.

Not because everything was left as it had been.

But precisely because change and the past stood side by side.

---

I told her about the building records.

Not everything.

Only the facts I thought relevant.

Margaret listened.

When I had finished, she did not comment at once.

"So the house really was once recorded?"

"Yes."

"And then it vanished from the list?"

"More or less."

"Interesting."

"Have you ever heard the name Bellweather House?"

Margaret thought.

"That name sounds familiar."

"Where?"

"I don't know."

She shrugged.

"Perhaps from Arthur."

"Perhaps."

Margaret took up her cup.

"Why don't you ask him directly?"

"Because he already knows."

"Arthur?"

"There are some things I shouldn't tell Arthur directly. But Margaret..."

Margaret raised an eyebrow.

"Those words sound interesting."

"Arthur knows Bellweather House once existed."

"He also knows who once lived there."

Margaret looked at me.

"Really?"

"Yes."

"He told you?"

"Indeed."

Margaret was silent.

"You're not going to tell me."

"Not now."

Margaret nodded.

"Then you'll have to find it out yourself."

"Perhaps."

"Arthur can't give all the answers. And even if he could — you would have to find the rest yourself. It has always been that way."

Margaret then went back to her work.

I watched the way she served her customers one by one.

A workman ordered lunch.

Another asked for coffee to take away.

An old woman sat near the window reading a newspaper.

No one paid our conversation any attention.

No one seemed to care about Bellweather House.

And perhaps there was indeed no reason for them to care.

For most people in Ashford, an old house that had stood empty for a long while was only an old house that had stood empty for a long while.

A mystery only appeared when someone began to search for its history.

I finished my coffee.

Before I left, Margaret said:

"Leon."

I stopped.

"If you find something about that house..."

"What?"

"Don't believe it right away."

I smiled.

"I'm indeed not quick to believe."

"I know."

She picked up a newspaper.

"Good."

---

I went out.

I went home that afternoon.

I passed along Bellweather Lane.

This time I did not stop.

I only looked at the old house from a distance.

Fence.

Windows.

A roof slightly askew.

Everything the same.

I walked past it.

Then something made me stop.

Not because of that house.

Because of my own house.

I turned toward number 13.

Then I looked back at the empty house.

The distance between them had not changed.

I took out the map.

I opened to the page I had read at the library.

Number 13.

Then the empty house.

No house after it.

I folded the map.

Perhaps it was only a change of administration.

Perhaps the house was simply no longer regarded as a separate address.

Perhaps there was an error in the archive.

All of it was still possible.

I went into the house.

As I opened the gate of my house, I noticed something I had never thought about before.

The number 13 near the door was not newly affixed.

The metal was a little dulled.

There were scratches on its surface.

Beneath it were the marks of two small holes that might have come from an older number plate.

I moved closer.

No other numeral could be read.

I touched its surface for a moment.

Then I drew my hand back.

Perhaps it was only the trace of renovation.

But now I was beginning to notice the things that had once been too ordinary to see.

---

That night I opened my notebook again.

I wrote:

Bellweather House

Beneath it:

Recorded in an old document.

Then:

Has no house number.

Then:

Possibly once had a connection with property number 13.

I stopped.

I stared at the last line.

I had no proof of it.

I crossed it out.

Then I wrote:

The connection with number 13 is not yet known.

I closed the book.

I opened another page and added a few facts I had already confirmed:

1791 — Bellweather House recorded.

End of Bellweather Lane.

No number listed.

Then removed from the active list.

Further document not available.

I read everything once more.

There was no conclusion.

Only facts.

That was better.

If I began connecting everything too quickly, I might see a pattern that was not really there.

I was about to put out the lamp when I heard a sound from outside.

Very small.

Like something falling in the yard.

I stopped.

I waited.

There was no second sound.

I opened the curtain.

The front yard was empty.

I looked toward the road.

The houses across the way were dark.

I was about to close the curtain when I saw something near the door.

An envelope.

I opened the door.

I picked it up.

This time there was no name on the front.

Only an address.

I read it.

Bellweather House.

No number.

No name of a town.

No name of a recipient.

I turned it over.

Empty.

I stared at the envelope for a few seconds.

Then I looked toward the end of the road.

The old house was still dark.

I closed the door.

I carried the envelope to the table.

For the first time, I felt that perhaps someone was indeed trying to direct me toward that place.

But I still did not know who.

---

I took up my pencil.

I wrote one sentence in the notebook:

Bellweather House.

A house that was recorded, then struck from the list.

And now an envelope without a sender finds me.

I stared at the writing.

Then I added:

Do not draw a conclusion before there are facts.

I closed the book.

The night fell quiet again.

And for the time being, I chose to leave it that way.

But before sleeping, I opened the envelope again.

There was nothing inside it.

I examined the front once more.

Bellweather House.

Not a complete address.

Not a person's name.

Only the name of a house.

I laid it beside the letter from Pembroke & Clarke.

Two objects.

Two pieces of information.

One about the past.

One about a lost place.

Not yet enough to form a story.

I put out the lamp.

Outside, a carriage passed along Bellweather Lane. Its light swept across the window of the room for a few seconds, then vanished. The house fell dark again. Ashford was quiet again.

But the envelope on the table — an address with no name and no town, only "Bellweather House" — joined the rest of my list: the latch in the dark, the prints that vanished by morning, the silhouette at the window, the bolt drawn back by no hand I knew. And the dream, which now came to me on more than one night — the boy in the dark coat, saying the same words each time: *Don't be late again.* Each alone I could explain. Together they were no longer curiosity. Something had been coming to this house in the night, and now someone was writing to it as if it were mine.

I remembered what Daniel had told me a few days before, when I asked him where a man ought to report a thing that threatened him but that he could not explain even to a friend. *The constable. Calloway. His office is near the Market Square.*

The next morning I did not go to the café first. I went to the constabulary.

The man behind the desk looked up — and I knew him at once. The grey at his temples. The dark woollen coat, now without the rain. The small pin at his collar, which in the lamplight of the inn I had not been able to make out. It was a constable's pin.

"Mr. Ashcroft," he said. Not surprised. "We met at the inn, the night you arrived. I wondered if our paths would cross again."

I set down my notes — the latch, the prints, the silhouette, the bolt, the dream, the envelope. Facts. "I am not here about the will," I said. "I am here because something has been at my back door in the night, and I would like to know what it is."

Constable Calloway read the list without remark. Then he looked at me in a way I had not earned.

"I will not tell you what I think from a page," he said. "Not yet. I need to see the place."

He came with me to Bellweather Lane. At the front door he paused, then walked slowly through the house — the hallway, the reading room, the stairs, the closed door beneath them that would not open. He stopped longest beneath the portrait of the boy in the upstairs passage. He spoke no word of ghosts. He only grew very still, as a man does who has heard a sound no one else in the room can hear.

"It is here," he said at last, quietly. "Not in the walls. In the remembering. This house keeps what was done in it, and for a few nights each year it lets the keeping show."

Back at his office he drew out a drawer of old reports. "Because I could not name it myself," he said, "I looked for those who came before me." The records were thin and old, but they agreed: the disturbance returned on the same dates, year after year — from the thirteenth to the twenty-first of November. After the twenty-first it ceased, and Ashford went back to itself. No one had been harmed who simply waited it out and did not go looking for it.

I thought of Helen's face when she looked at the portrait. Of the dream that had come on the thirteenth and would not leave. "And the public does not know."

"The public is better not knowing," Calloway said. "Some things are kept only by being left unspoken."

I frowned. "Then if it is a secret, why are you telling it to me?"

For the first time his manner softened — not kindly, but as a man who had asked himself the same question. "Because the secret found you first, Mr. Ashcroft. The moment you opened that door on Bellweather Lane, you were already inside it. I am not giving you a secret. I am only confirming what has been standing behind you for some nights now."

He returned my notes. "It ends on the twenty-first. Lock the doors you lock. Do not go to the empty house at the end of the lane. And when it is over, it will be over."

I did not thank him. I was not sure yet what I had been given.

But as I walked back toward Bellweather Lane, I understood one thing: the list in my notebook was no longer only a list of small, explainable things. Something had begun to answer it — and, by the constable's account, it would answer itself, in a few nights more.
