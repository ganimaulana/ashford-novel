CHAPTER 80 — THE CROWD

I was in the lane again on Tuesday morning, because a note had come on Monday evening in a hand I did not recognise. It said only: The office. Ten o’clock. Bring nothing.

I had been to the office eleven times before. I had been there as a man who was asked to look at a room, to choose between statements, to order objects. I had been there as one of four men who were to be examined together, and as one of four men who had made a decision and were still uncertain whether it was the right one.

But I had never been there as a man who arrived to find twenty-seven others already waiting.

The lane was not quiet. The common was not empty. The office door stood open, but the crowd spilled out onto the step and into the road. Men stood in groups of two and three, their hats in their hands, their coats buttoned against the wind. A few leaned against the wall of the chandler’s shop opposite. Others sat on the low wall by the pump, not speaking.

I stopped at the edge of the crowd.

Until that moment I had thought the four of us were the examination.

I was wrong.

The crowd was not hostile. It was not friendly either. It was waiting. The faces were ordinary, the clothes were plain, the voices were low. No one looked at me as though I were expected. No one looked at me as though I were not.

I stood for a moment, and then I moved forward, because there was nowhere else to go.

A man in a grey coat came out of the office and stood on the step. He was not Calloway. He was not Orme. He was younger, perhaps thirty, with a cap under his arm and a sheet of paper in his hand. He looked at the crowd and did not seem surprised by anything he saw.

“There will be two groups,” he said. “The first group will go in now. The second will wait. When the first comes out, the second will go in. You will be told which group you are in.”

He looked down at the paper.

“The first group is as follows.”

He read sixteen names. I was not among them. The man with the notebook was not among them. The older man with the frayed cuffs was not among them.

I did not know whether I was relieved or disappointed. I only knew that I was waiting, and that waiting was not something I had done well before.

The sixteen men went in. The door closed. The crowd on the step shifted, and I found myself standing beside a man I had not seen before. He was about my age, with a clean coat and a cap that he kept turning in his hands. He looked at me, and then he looked at the door, and then he looked at me again.

“Are you one of the ones who did the first tests?” he said.

I did not know whether I should answer. I said, “I did some tests.”

“Did they tell you what it was for?”

“No.”

He nodded, as though he had expected that answer. “They didn’t tell me either. They sent a letter. I came. That is all I know.”

He looked at the door again. I looked at the door too. There was nothing else to look at.

The crowd was beginning to talk. The men who had been silent began to speak, first in low murmurs, then in ordinary voices. Someone said he had been told to bring nothing. Someone else said he had been told to bring his own paper. A third man said he had been told nothing at all, only to appear.

I listened without speaking. I learned that most of them had received letters in the past week. I learned that none of them knew what the examination was for. I learned that some had done tests already, like me, and some had not. I learned that everyone was guessing.

I stayed where I was. I did not try to make myself heard. I did not ask for attention. I stood and listened, and when a man asked me directly whether I had been examined before, I said yes, and when he asked me what it was like, I said I did not know whether I was allowed to say.

He looked at me with something like respect, or perhaps only with recognition.

“You’re one of the ones who keeps quiet,” he said. “I remember you.”

I did not ask what he remembered. I only nodded, and he moved away to speak to someone else.

The door opened again. The first group came out. Their faces were the same as before, but their shoulders were tighter. No one spoke as they passed. They walked down the lane without looking back.

The young man with the cap returned to the step.

“The second group,” he said. He read the remaining names. There were eleven of us. I was among them.

We went in.

The office had changed. The front room was empty. The clerk was not at his desk. The chairs had been moved against the wall, and a door at the back of the room stood open. I had not noticed that door before. It had always been closed.

We went through it.

Beyond the door was a passage. At the end of the passage was a larger room. I had not seen it before. It was long and low-ceilinged, with a single window high on the wall that let in a rectangle of grey light. There were no chairs. The floor was wooden. The walls were plain. In the centre of the room stood a table, and behind the table stood Calloway.

He did not sit. He did not smile. He did not welcome us. He waited until the last man had entered, and then he spoke.

“This is the examination,” he said. “Some of you have done tests before. Some of you have not. That does not matter now. The office will examine you as a group. You will be given tasks. Some of you will continue. Some of you will not. You will be told when and where to appear. That is all.”

He looked at us. I thought he might add something, but he did not. He turned and walked to the door at the far end of the room. He opened it. He went through. The door closed behind him.

We stood in silence.

The man beside me was the one who had asked me about the tests. He looked at the door Calloway had gone through, and then he looked at the others, and then he looked at me.

“That was it?” he said.

“That was it,” I said.

“He didn’t tell us what to do.”

“No.”

“He didn’t tell us when to come back.”

“No.”

The man next to him, older and heavier, shook his head. “He told us we would be told. That is what he said. We will be told.”

“When?”

“When he decides.”

There was nothing else to say. We stood for a moment longer. Then, one by one, we began to leave. I was not the first to go, and I was not the last. I walked back through the passage, through the front room, out the door, and into the lane.

The crowd had gone. The lane was quiet again. The common was empty. The office door was open, but no one was standing on the step.

I walked home slowly. I did not know whether I had passed anything, or failed anything, or whether there was anything to pass or fail. I did not know why I had been called, or why I had been grouped with ten other men who knew as little as I did. I did not know what the office wanted from us.

But I knew that the examination was no longer a small thing. It was no longer four men in a room. It was a crowd, and a door, and a man who told us nothing, and a longer room with no chairs.

I set the key in the lock and turned it. The door opened. The room was the same. The box was in the press. The papers were on the table. The bundle was tied. The ruled sheet was folded. The service file was on the left. The notebook was closed.

I sat down and looked at the window, and I remembered the man who had said he remembered me.

I had not thought I was worth remembering. But he had remembered me, and I did not know why. Perhaps it was only that I had not spoken. Perhaps it was only that I had been quiet. Or perhaps he had seen something I did not know I was showing.

I did not know what any of them saw when they looked at me.

But I knew they looked.
