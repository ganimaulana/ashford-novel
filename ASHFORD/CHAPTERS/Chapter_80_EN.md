CHAPTER 80 — THE SNOW

The snow came at the end of the year. It came in the night, quietly, the way the important things of Ashford came — without announcement, without warning, filling the world while no one watched. By morning the town was covered — the roofs, the streets, the fields, the common. The snow lay thick and white, and the town was silent under it, the way a town is silent when the world has been made new and no one has walked in it yet.

I stood at my window in the grey morning, and I looked at the snow, and I thought about the first keeper, and the keeper before Vale, and Vale, and me. Four keepers. Four men who had kept the same thing.

The first keeper had built Bellweather House. I had never known his name, and I did not think the town had ever known it, and I understood that the first keeper had not built the house to be remembered but to be kept — that the house was his account, the thing he had left standing so that the keeping would have a place. The keeper before Vale had opened the door. He had opened it because he wanted to know, and he had been ashamed of wanting to know, and he had spent fifty years in the passage, and he had come back in the winter, to my door, to read the account I had written. Vale had kept the door closed for fifty years. He had kept the forgetting, and not the remembering. And I had kept the account.

I stood at the window, and I looked at the snow, and I understood that I was the fourth keeper. And I understood that the keeping had changed. The first keeper had built the house. The keeper before Vale had opened the door. Vale had kept the door closed. And I had kept the account. Each keeper had kept the same thing differently. And the keeping had been passed, keeper to keeper, for longer than the town had a name.

It was a strange thing to be the fourth in a line and to know the line by its acts and not its faces. I knew what they had done. I did not know what they had feared. I did not know what the first keeper had seen, in the deep time of the town, that had made him build a house at the end of a lane and set the first sign beneath it. I did not know what Vale had feared, that he had kept the door closed through all the years of his keeping. I knew only that I had inherited their fears along with their acts, and that the fears were not written in any account, and that I would have to learn them for myself.

I went out into the snow.

The streets were empty. The snow was deep enough that my boots sank into it with each step, and the only sound was the crunch of the snow and the distant sound of a door closing somewhere in the next street. The town was a different place under the snow — a place that had been made new, and that had not yet remembered what it was. The lampposts wore caps of white. The shop signs hung under their burden of snow. The smoke from the chimneys went up straight and grey into a sky the color of the snow itself, and there was no wind, and the silence was so complete that I could hear my own heartbeat in my ears, the way one hears it in the deep of the night.

I walked the length of the High Street, past the bakery — closed, Margaret's fire banked, the bread-warmth gone out of the air — past the brewery, past the shops with their shuttered windows. No one was abroad. The town had not woken to the snow; it had chosen to sleep through it, and I walked through a town that had been emptied of everyone but me, and I understood that this was how the keeping felt — that I was the one who walked while the town slept, the one who kept while the town forgot.

I walked to the church. The snow had drifted against the door of the tower, and I brushed it away with my hand, and the cold bit at my fingers through the wool. The door was locked. I took out the keeper's key. I unlocked it. I climbed the stairs, and the stone stairs were cold through my boots, and the tower was darker than the street, and the air was still and old, the air of a place no one climbs to. I stood in the bell chamber. The bell hung in the grey light, silent, the first sign on its rim. I had not rung it since the night it had rung for me. I stood before it, and I looked at the first sign. The bell was the keeper's bell. It had rung once, for the keeper before Vale. It had rung once, for me. I did not know whether it would ring again.

I stood in the bell chamber, in the snow-light, and I put my hand on the bell. It was cold. The metal was cold against my palm, the way it had been the first time I had climbed the tower. I had not rung it then. I had not rung it now. The bell had rung on its own, when it was ready, and I had answered it. I stood with my hand on the cold metal, and I thought about what it meant to wait for a bell that had rung for me, and to know it might not ring again, and to keep the waiting anyway. The bell was not a thing I rang. It was a thing I answered. And the answering was the keeping.

I lowered my hand. I left the bell. I closed the tower.

I walked through the snow to the end of Bellweather Lane. Bellweather House stood at the end of the lane, under the snow, empty, dark, forgotten. The snow had drifted against its door, and no one had walked to it since the snow had fallen — no one but me. I stood before it. I did not enter. I stood in the snow, before the house, and I understood that the house would be kept. I would keep it. I would keep it empty, as it had always been kept, so that the first sign would have a place. The house had been built by the first keeper for the keeping, and the keeping had passed from him to the keeper before Vale, and from him to Vale, and from Vale to me, and the house had stood through all of it, empty and dark and ready, and I would let it stand.

I walked back through the town, through the snow, to the common. The common was white. The house was not there. I knelt in the snow, at the place where the house appeared, where the keeper's sign lay beneath the frost, and I took out the Tokens. Both of them. They were warm. They had been warm since the bell had rung, since I had set the Token in the first sign in the chamber, since I had understood what I was keeping.

I held them in my hands, in the falling snow, and I looked at the first sign on each. The circle. The three lines. The dot. The first sign, twice. The maker's sign. I had carried one all my life. I had found the second in the kept house. And I had returned both to the keeping.

I sat in the snow, at the place where the house appeared, and I held the Tokens, and I thought about the keeping. I was the keeper — not because I had chosen it, but because I had accepted it. I kept the forgetting, the remembering, the account, the houses, the bell, the Tokens, the place beneath. But I had begun to suspect that keeping was not the same as holding; it was the willingness to let go when the time came.

I understood it, sitting in the snow with the warm metal in my hands: the first keeper had built the house and let it stand. The keeper before Vale had opened the door and let the knowing come. Vale had closed the door and let the forgetting hold. And I had written the account and let it pass to the keeper who would come after me — a keeper I did not know, whose face I would never see, who would take up the red book and the Tokens and the keeping, as I had taken them up, and who would hold them as long as he could, and let them go. The keeping was not a thing that was mine. It was a thing that passed through me, as it had passed through the first keeper and the keeper before Vale and Vale. I was not the end of it. I was the fourth, and there would be a fifth, and the fifth would not know my name, and the account would tell him what I had done, and that would have to be enough.

I stood. I put the Tokens back in my coat. I walked home through the snow. I sat at my desk. I opened the red book. I wrote: The snow has come. The town is silent under it. I walked the town today. I saw the tower, and the bell, and the house at the end of the lane, and the common. I am the fourth keeper. I keep the account. I will pass it on. The keeping will continue.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer, with the agreement, and the map, and my notebook. I left the Tokens on the desk. And I sat in the dark, and I was content — not because the town had been preserved, but because I had stopped expecting it to remember. The snow would melt. The account would outlast me. The keeping was not a victory; it was a tide that would continue without my permission. I was the keeper, but I was not the end of the keeping.

I sat in the stillness as the snow continued to fall beyond the window, and I understood that I was not waiting for the bell to ring. I was waiting for myself to be ready for it. The bell would ring when it rang. The count would end when it ended. And I would be there — not because I was ready, but because I was the keeper, and the keeper was the one who answered.

But as the dark settled, a quieter thought came to me, and it would not be answered by readiness or by the keeping. I had passed the account on in my mind — to a keeper I did not know. And yet the account was still in my drawer, and the Tokens still warm on my desk, and the door still closed beneath the snow. The passing was not a thing I could do in thought. It was a thing I would do with my hands, one day, to a face I had not yet seen. And until that day, the keeping was mine, and I did not know whether I would be able to give it away when the time came — whether I would be able to lay the red book in another's hands and walk out into the snow as the keeper before Vale had walked out into the winter, and not look back. The four keepers had each let the keeping go. I did not know whether I would be the one who broke the line, or the one who carried it on, or whether the keeping would simply take the choice from me, as it had taken so much else.
