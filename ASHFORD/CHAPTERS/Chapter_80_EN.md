CHAPTER 80 — THE ACCOUNT

The autumn came to Ashford quietly. The leaves turned, and the wind carried them down the High Street, and the nights lengthened until the lamps were lit before supper and the dark came early over the fields. The frost began to lie on the grass in the early mornings, white and clean, and the smell of woodsmoke returned to the streets, and the town turned inward, the way a town turns inward when the year is closing. And I kept the account.

I had written in the red book every day since the night I had understood what I was keeper of. I had written what I had learned, and what I had felt, and what I had decided. I had written the account of the place beneath — the chamber, the first sign, the warmth, the understanding that I was keeper of a place and not of a door. And I had begun, in the long evenings, to read back what I had written, the way one reads a letter from a stranger and slowly comes to recognize the hand.

I had not told anyone what I had learned. I had not told Crowe. I had not told Eleanor Shaw. I had not told the clerk. I had kept the account, and I had kept the Tokens, and I had kept the gear, and I had kept the key to the tower. I had kept everything. And I had begun to understand that keeping was not the same as knowing.

I knew what I was keeping. I did not know why. I did not know what the place beneath was. I did not know why it had been kept. I did not know why I had been given the first sign, and why I had been led to the second, and why the bell had rung for me. I knew only that I was the keeper. And that the keeping was the thing.

Some nights I would set down my pen and sit in the dark of my rooms and listen to the town — the last carts, the shutting of doors, the lamplighter's footsteps fading — and I would think about the weight of what I carried alone. It was not the weight of the Tokens. It was the weight of knowing. The town below my window slept above the chamber, above the first sign, above the warmth, and it did not know, and I did not tell it, and the silence between that knowledge and that sleep was mine to hold.

On the first day of October, I went to the vestry. The clerk was there, as he always was, among his books. He was an old man, and I had known him all the time I had been in Ashford, and it had only just occurred to me to wonder how long he had sat among those books, how many years of the town had passed across his desk and been written down by his hand. Forty years, I thought. Forty years of ledgers, of births and deaths and marriages, of rates and accounts, of the careful record of an ordinary town. I had not come to ask about the wall, or the signs, or the Tokens. I had come to ask about the account.

"The records," I said. "The books you keep. The records of the town. What do they record?"

The clerk looked at me. "The records of the town," he said slowly. "The births. The deaths. The marriages. The rates. The accounts. The work of the wardens. The business of the vestry."

"And what do they record of the boundary? The wall? The signs?"

The clerk was silent. "Very little," he said. "The survey records the wall. The book of the wall records the stones. But the signs — the keeper's signs, the first signs — are not in the records. They are kept in the margins, in the hands of clerks who did not know what they were recording."

"In the margins," I said.

"Always in the margins," he said. "I have kept these books for forty years. I have never once written the keeper's sign in them, and I have never once been asked to. There are pages in the book of the wall where the old clerks left blanks, and I have always assumed — I do not know why I assumed it — that the blanks were where the signs belonged, and that the clerks who left them knew it, and left them blank because they knew what the signs were, and knew they were not to be written."

He looked at me. "You are the keeper," he said. "You know what the signs are. Tell me: were those blanks deliberate?"

I did not answer. I stood in the vestry, among the ledgers, and I understood that the clerk had been holding the blanks for forty years, keeping a place for the signs he had never written, and that he had not known what he was holding until he asked me. He was one of the hands, I thought. One of the hands in the margins.

"And the bell?" I said.

"The bell is in the record of the church," he said. "It is recorded as a bell. There is no record of when it rang, or why, or that it is the keeper's bell."

I stood in the vestry. The records of the town did not record what I was keeper of. They recorded the town. They recorded the births, and the deaths, and the rates, and the accounts. They did not record the keeping.

I understood, standing in the vestry, that the keeping had never been recorded. It had been kept in the margins. In the hands of clerks who did not know. In the red books. In the notebooks. In the accounts of keepers who had kept without understanding. And I understood that the account I was writing was part of the keeping. I was writing what the town did not record. I was writing the account of the place beneath.

And I understood that when I was gone, the account would be all that remained.

I went home. The streets were darkening, and the lamps were being lit, and I walked through the lamplight and the falling leaves, and I thought about the clerk among his ledgers, and the blanks he had kept, and I understood that the town's forgetting was not one great act but ten thousand small ones — a blank left here, a bell recorded as a bell there, a sign kept in the margins — and that the forgetting was kept, generation after generation, by people who did not know they were keeping it.

I sat at my desk. I opened the red book. I had been writing in it for weeks. I had written what I had learned. I had not written what I had decided. I had not written what I intended to do.

I sat at the desk, with the red book open, and I thought about the town. The town that did not record the keeping. The town that had forgotten the bell. The town that had forgotten Bellweather House. The town that had forgotten the keeper.

The town had an agreement — the agreement to see nothing, the agreement that had been written so that the town could forget. I had the agreement. The agreement signed by Aurelius Ashcroft. I had read it in the spring. It did not record the keeping. It recorded the forgetting. It recorded that the town agreed to see nothing, and to speak of nothing, and to forget the door and the sign and the bell.

I had been keeper for two months. I had been keeping the account. And I understood that the account was the opposite of the agreement. The agreement was the forgetting. The account was the remembering. And I was the keeper of the remembering.

I sat at the desk, in the dark, and I wrote:

The agreement is the forgetting. The town agreed to see nothing, and to speak of nothing, and to forget. The account is the remembering. I am writing what the town does not record. I am the keeper of the remembering. I do not know what I will do. But I know that I will not forget. I will keep the account. I will remember.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer, with the agreement, and the map, and my notebook. I did not put the Tokens away. I left them on the desk.

And I sat in the dark, and I thought about the agreement, and the account, and the town, and the keeping. The town had agreed to forget; I had chosen to remember. That was my duty. The place beneath was remembered in the account. I would keep it. When the town forgot again, the account would remain — not as a challenge, but as a record that had stopped needing a keeper and started being its own reason.

I thought, then, of the clerk's books — the births and deaths and marriages, the rates and the accounts, the careful record of an ordinary town. They were an account too. They were the town's account of itself, kept so that the town would know who it was. And my account was the other record — the record of what the town was not, the record of the keeping that the town had agreed to forget. Both were kept. Both were necessary. One recorded the life of the town. The other recorded what the town stood on.

I did not know whether the town would ever read my account. I did not know whether it would be found in a hundred years, in a drawer, beneath the parish account, by a man or woman who had no idea what it described. I understood only that it had to be written — that the remembering had to survive somewhere, even if it was only in the margins, in the hands of one keeper, in a red book in a drawer.

I sat in the stillness until the lamp burned low. The Tokens lay on the desk, catching the light. I was the keeper of the remembering. And I understood, at last, that the keeping had never been about the door, or the bell, or the place beneath. It had been about the account — about making sure that when everything else was forgotten, the record would remain.

And yet — and this was the thought that would not leave me, that followed me down the dark of the evening and into the lamplight of my desk — the door was not a record. It was a threshold. It had been opened once in my time, and the bell had rung, and I had gone down. The account kept the memory of that going. It could not keep what waited at the other end of it. I was the keeper of the remembering, and I had written everything I knew, and there was still something I did not know, and the red book could not hold it for me. I did not know whether the writing would ever be enough. I did not know whether the remembering would ever be enough. I only knew that it was mine to do, and that I would do it, and that the keeping, for now, was the writing.
