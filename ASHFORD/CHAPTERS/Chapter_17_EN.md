# Chapter 17 — The Boy at the Post Office

There is one thing a watchman learns sooner than the rounds and the lantern: that a list of names is a thing people come to you about. I had been on the roll nine days, and the town had not made a remark of it, and now the town began to make use of it.

The postmaster's nephew came to my door a little after breakfast, with the hat in his hand, in the way of boys who have been taught to hold their hat when they hold their errand. He had carried letters to my hand twice and had taken off his hat to men he had carried letters to, and I had noticed him the way one notices weather; but this morning he stood at the door as a boy stands that has chosen the door, and that is a different thing.

"If you please, sir," he said, "my uncle said I was to tell you the boy came again last evening."

"What boy."

"The boy that comes, sir. In the evening."

I looked at him, and he looked at his hat, and I remembered what I had written in my book about not saying the boys were one boy until I had seen; and here was the seeing offered to me at my own doorstep, by a child who did not know he was offering it.

"Come in out of the cold," I said. "You may tell me at the door what you may tell me in the house."

"No, sir. My uncle said I was to tell you at the door, because it is a message and not a call." He had the boy's exactness in repeating. "The boy came again last evening, at the hour the evening things come. Two men and a boy, sir, that is what we say at the office, for the things that come by hand. And there was a letter for the house."

"For what house."

"For the house at the end, sir. It is written *Bellweather House*, in the hand I know."

There was a time, three weeks ago, when I would have asked him how he knew the hand, and he would have told me he did not know; and I had learned by now that the office did not keep a register, and that there was nothing for it but the boy's memory, which is the register every office keeps when it keeps none.

"Who gave it him to carry," I said.

"That is what I came to tell you, sir, because my uncle said I was to tell you true, and it is the thing I cannot tell. I was at the door of the office, and the boy came up the street by the pump, and gave it into my hand, and said what is said, and went back by the pump. I looked after him, because that is the custom of the office, and he turned at the corner by Marlowe's wall, and I did not see him after."

"What did he say that is said."

"He said, 'Carry that as you carry the other.'"

The boy looked up from his hat then, and it was not a boy's look, but the look of one who has been set to carry a sentence he does not own, and has come to see whether it will be carried further.

"And before," he said, "the other evenings, it was the same way; and there were two that stood while the one gave it, and one of them had a coat like a gentleman's, and the voice was not a gentleman's voice, and I have told that to my uncle, and my uncle said I was not to tell it again except to the watch. And you are the watch, sir."

"I am the watch."

"Then I have told it to the watch." He put the hat on, and the boy came back into his face at the putting of it on, the way the weather comes back when the wind changes. "The letter is on the sideboard, sir, where the unfranked things lie. My uncle said you might see it at the hour the office is open, and not sooner, because it is a letter that has not come by the post."

---

I went to the office at the hour the office is open, because the office keeps its hours the way the church keeps its hours, and a thing that is to be seen at an hour is not seen before it.

The postmaster was a man of that particular quiet that belongs to men through whose hands everything passes and in whose hands nothing stays. He gave me the letter to look at, and not to take, because it was not addressed to me, and the office is answerable for the letters that are given into its hand, whether they come by the post or by the pump.

It lay in my palm, and it was the letter the boy had said it was: *Bellweather House*, and no name and no town and no sender, in the hand I had seen three times before, at my own desk, with my own letters lying beside it. The paper was such paper as any shop in the town sells by the quire. The seal was broken — the office had not broken it, and the letter had come broken, in the way letters come that have been read by somebody before they are carried.

"There is the same thing every so many days," said the postmaster, "and there has been since before I came to the office; and the office carries what is given it, and takes the penny that is given, and the penny is given always, and the giver is not given. You will understand that I am not the man to make a remark of it."

"I am not asking you to make one."

"Then I will say what I do say, which is this," said the postmaster. "The office is the office of the town, and not of the lane; and there is a man at the bench that has written to the office this month, about the rates that come by hand, because the rates that come by hand are the rates that pay no duty. There is no law in it yet. There is a writing at the bench. I tell you, because you are the watch, and because a letter that is carried for a penny by a boy is a letter that any man may ask about, and a letter that comes by the post is a letter that no man may."

I gave back the letter to the sideboard where the unfranked things lie, and I went out, and I understood that the town had two ways of carrying a letter, and that one of them had been the lane's way since before the office, and that the bench had begun, this month, to look at the way that paid no duty.

I went home and wrote in my book:

*3 Dec. The letter came again last evening, broken, for the house at the end, in the known hand; carried by a boy to the office, given at the corner by Marlowe's wall; two stood while the one gave; one of a coat like a gentleman's, with a voice that was not. The office has carried thus since before the office's postmaster. The bench has written this month about the hand-carried rates. There is, therefore, a letter that goes to the house every so many days, that the town has carried without remark since before I came to it; and there is a bench, this month, that has begun to see the thing the town has not seen since before the office. I am the watch, and I have seen it today. The seeing is now on my side of the question, and I do not know yet which of us it is that has been seen. The price of flour is on the pump again, and my own purse is lighter than it was when I came to this town; the season takes its toll in more ways than one.*

---

In the afternoon the frost broke, and the first soft weather of December came in with it, and I went to Margaret's, because the town comes to Margaret's when the weather changes, and the price of flour is on the pump, and the watch that would know the town must know where the town stands when the pump is read.

She had the room full, as she has it at that hour, and there was a man at the table by the window that I had not seen there before, in such a coat as a man wears that has come up from London and has not put off London with the coach, and he was reading the paper with the manner of a man that reads the paper where he will be seen reading it.

"That is a man from the bench," said Margaret, at the counter, in the voice she uses for the things that are to be heard at the counter. "He came down on the morning coach. He has been at the town hall with the clerk, and he has been at the church with the churchwardens, and he will be here an hour, because a man that has been at the town hall and the church in one morning has had his fill of the keeping."

"What does he want."

"He wants the rates," said Margaret. "The rates that come by hand. There is a writing from the bench, and the bench would have the duty of the post upon the letter that goes by the boy, and the town says the letter that goes by the boy has gone by the boy since before the duty, and that is where it stands, and that is where it will stand until somebody is made an example of, because that is what a writing wants in the end, Mr. Ashcroft — a name."

The man at the window turned the paper then, and looked over the top of it at the room, in the way of men that have been set to look; and his look came to the counter, and rested there the length of a breath, and passed on; and I took it for what it was, and gave it back for what it was, and Margaret set the cup down between us as if it had been an answer.

"Mind your keeping," she said. "There is a man in the room that keeps for the keeping."
