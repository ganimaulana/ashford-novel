CHAPTER 91 — THE FIRST ROUND

We stood in the field, waiting. The posts and ropes had been rearranged into a series of lanes, each marked with chalk lines that crossed and turned. At the far end of the field, I could see a table with small objects on it—bundles of cloth, I thought, or perhaps papers. The man in the grey coat stood at the edge of the field, a sheet of paper in his hand.

"There are thirty-one of you," he said. "Twenty-four will continue. Seven will leave the grounds before nightfall. The task is simple: you will cross the field from this side to the other. The marked lanes indicate the available routes. You may use any lane or any passage that is not blocked. You must pass through one of the four gates, collect the item placed beyond it, and return to this side. The first twenty-four to complete the task will remain. The rest will be dismissed."

He paused.

"You may help one another. You may not obstruct one another. You may not use violence. You will begin when the bell rings."

He stepped back.

I looked at the field. The lanes were not straight. They curved and doubled back. The gates were spaced across the far end, each marked with a different colour—red, blue, green, yellow. I could not see what lay beyond them.

Hunter was already moving toward the starting line. He did not look at anyone. He simply walked, his steps even, his head low.

I walked to the line and stood near the edge. Weaver was to my left. Ellison was further down, his hands in his pockets. Merritt stood apart, his arms crossed.

The bell rang.

The field erupted. Men ran forward, choosing lanes at random. Some headed for the nearest gate. Others paused, trying to read the layout.

I did not run. I watched.

The red gate had the shortest lane, but it crossed a patch of mud that would slow anyone who tried to run through it. The blue gate had a rope barrier that required ducking, which would cost time for taller men. The yellow gate was longer, but its lane was clear and dry. The green gate had a narrow opening that forced candidates to go through single file, creating a bottleneck.

I chose the yellow gate. It was not the fastest route, but it was the most reliable. I walked quickly, keeping a steady pace. I saw Hunter take the red gate—he was fast enough to handle the mud without losing momentum. I saw Ellison take the blue gate, ducking under the rope with practiced ease. I saw Weaver hesitate, then choose the green gate.

I reached the yellow gate, passed through, and picked up the cloth bundle waiting on the ground. I turned and began my return. As I came back, I saw Weaver struggling at the green gate—the narrow opening had caused a jam, and he was waiting for two other candidates to pass before he could get through. He was losing time.

I stopped at the side of his lane. "Wait," I said. "There is another way."

I pointed to a gap in the ropes that I had noticed while passing—a passage that was not marked but was clear of any barrier. I had seen it earlier and noted it as a possible alternative. Weaver looked, nodded, and followed me through the gap. It cost me a few seconds, and two men passed me while I waited. But we both emerged on the other side faster than if he had waited in line.

We crossed the finish line together. I was not among the first ten. I was nearer the middle, close to the threshold. I handed my bundle to the clerk. He wrote something in his ledger.

I stood and watched the others finish. Some crossed quickly. Others struggled. A few did not return at all—they had taken the wrong turn and disappeared behind the posts, only to reappear minutes later, empty-handed.

The man with the notebook was among the last. He had chosen the red gate, but he had spent the first moments of the task studying the layout, trying to determine which route was optimal. He had moved only after several candidates had already passed through the gates. When he reached the mud, he slowed further, picking his way carefully to avoid slipping. By the time he emerged on the other side, the bundle was in his hand, but the field ahead was nearly empty. He crossed the finish line after the twenty-fourth candidate. His face was calm, but his hands were empty.

When the last man crossed, the clerk looked at his ledger and read the names of those who would remain. Twenty-four names.

I heard my own name.

I heard Weaver's.

I heard Ellison's.

I heard Merritt's.

I heard Peale's.

I did not hear the man with the notebook.

He was standing near the edge of the field, his notebook still in his hand. He was not looking at the clerk. He was looking at the ground.

The clerk read the names of the seven who would leave. The man with the notebook was one of them.

The man in the grey coat spoke again. "Those who have been named will gather their belongings and leave the grounds. You will be escorted to the gate. The examination continues for the rest."

The man with the notebook walked toward the main building. He did not look back.

I stood in the field, the bundle in my hands.

I had survived.

But I had not been fast. I had not been strong. I had chosen the reliable route and helped someone else when it cost me a few places. Whether that had been the right choice, I could not say.

I looked at the empty space where the man with the notebook had stood. I thought about his notebook, his pencil, his careful writing. He had been observant. He had been methodical. But he had also been slow to commit, and he had spent too long weighing options while others were moving.

Speed had mattered. So had judgment. Beyond that, I still did not know what the office had seen.

I walked back to my room.

The sun was low. The field was empty.

I sat on the bed and looked out the window. The grounds were quiet. The seven who had left were gone.

The examination had truly begun.
