CHAPTER 60 — THE ROAD NORTH

I rose before the lane did on Monday. The fire was low, and the room was cold, and the kettle took longer to boil than it should have, because I had not tended it properly the night before, and I stood over it and waited and thought about the silence, and about what Vale had not said, and about whether the silence was the same silence that was in the file, and whether the two silences were one silence or two.

The Hartley girl's bowl was gone. The lane had been quiet. The week had continued. I had counted the eight. The arithmetic had held. The box had sat in the press. The leaf had lain on the table. The papers had remained where they were.

I sat in the chair. I did not open the notebook. The kettle boiled. I poured the water and held the cup, and I did not drink it, because the holding of it was enough.

The office did not come. The lane was empty. The morning was mine.

I went to the press. I opened the door. The box was there. The three bundles were on the lid. The uncertain paper lay on top of everything. I lifted the bundles aside and set them on the table, and I reached into the box and took out the district map.

It was the same map Vale had shown me in October. Dark paper, soft from folding, the edges worn. Not of Alderwick — he had said that himself, in the careful way he said things that were true but not the whole truth. "Of the kind a constabulary prints and does not sell." Marked in ink, in several shades, over many years. I had looked at it then, briefly, while he watched, and I had put it back in the box, and I had not opened it again.

I spread it on the table now.

The paper was larger than the table. It overlapped the leaf and the eight papers and the clerk's ruled sheet. I weighed down the corners with the bottles from the box — the two stoppered bottles, labelled in a hand I did not know — and I leaned over it, because the light from the window was not good enough and I had to move my head to see.

The marks were there. Dozens of them. Small dots, most of them, in a lighter ink that had faded to the color of tea. A few circles, in a darker ink that had not faded as much. A handful of crosses, in an ink so dark it was almost black. They were scattered across the paper without pattern that I could see. There were no keys. There were no explanations. There was only the map and the marks and the silence of the man who had made them.

I had seen all this before.

But I had not seen the mark near the road.

It was a short line, drawn in the darker ink, crossing one of the roads at a point north of the town. The road was labeled, in a hand that was not Vale's, with a name I did not recognize. But the direction was familiar. I had walked that direction once, on a Tuesday evening when I had gone north instead of south, when I had followed a road past the common and past the last house and up toward the hills, when I had found the mill and the stones and the Door.

I looked at the mark again.

It was not like the other marks. The dots and circles were placed at intersections, or at bends, or at places where a road met a boundary. They were record marks, the kind a man makes when he is noting that something was there and is still there. But this mark was different. It was a line, not a dot. It was placed on the road itself, not at a feature beside it. And it was in the darker ink, the ink that had not faded, the ink that was newer than the others.

I looked for a date. There was none. I looked for a notation. There was a small one, beside the line, in the same darker ink. It was not a word. It was not a date. It was a letter, or part of a letter, or something that looked like a letter but might have been a number. I could not read it. It was too small. It was too compressed. It was the kind of writing a man makes when he is not expecting anyone else to read it, and when he does not need to explain it to himself because he already knows what it means.

I straightened. I took my pencil from my pocket. I pressed the point against the paper beside the mark and I held it there for a moment, because the doing of that was the doing of something, and the doing of something was better than the doing of nothing, and the doing of nothing was what the room had been asking me to do for three days.

I wrote the date in my notebook. Monday, the day after the file, the day after the silence, the day after the refusal. Then I wrote: Map. Dark line. North road. Notation unreadable.

I wrote nothing else.

I looked at the map again. I looked at the other marks, the dots and circles and crosses, and I thought about what Vale had said — that he had marked it in several shades over many years — and I thought about the journal, which had said nothing about where he had been or what he had done in those years, and I thought about the file, which had said even less, and I thought about the box, which held the map and the journal and the bottles and the button and the ring and the cloth, and I thought that the map was the only thing among them that pointed anywhere, and the direction was north, and the north was where the mill was, and the mill was where the Door was, and the Door was what Vale had guarded for fifty years, and the fifty years was what the file had reduced to four lines.

I folded the map. I put it back in the box, under the bundles, where it had been before. I shut the box. I set it back in the press. I closed the press door.

The leaf lay on the table. The eight papers stood beside it. The clerk's ruled sheet was folded on top. The Hartley girl's bowl was not there. The fire was low. The kettle was cold. The lane was quiet. The week was not over. For now.
