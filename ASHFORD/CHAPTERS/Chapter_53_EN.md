CHAPTER 53 — THE DEAD MAN'S EFFECTS

I opened the box on Sunday.

Not because Sunday was the right day, or because I had planned it for that day, but because I had put it off through Saturday and there was no further day to put it off through, and because the room was cold and the fire was low and I understood that if I did not do it then I would not do it, and that the not-doing would become a habit, and that the habit would become the thing I told myself was respect when it was only fear.

The box was where I had left it, in the press beside the window. I had not moved it since Calloway came. I had not opened it. I had sat with my hand on it the night before, and that was the nearest thing to opening it I had managed, and I was ashamed of that now, though I could not have said exactly what the shame was for.

I took it down.

It was heavier than it looked. He had told me that in August, and he had been right, and the rightness of it came back to me as a physical thing, the weight of it in my hands, and I understood that I had been holding it the way he had held it, carefully, as though it contained something that would break if you were rough with it, though I knew now that nothing in it was fragile.

I set it on the table.

I undid the clasp.

---

The papers were on top.

That was the first thing. Not the letters, not the journal, not the map. The papers. Folded and re-folded until the creases had gone soft, the way paper goes when a man handles it for years without reading it again, or reads it so often that the folding becomes a kind of prayer. I lifted them out and set them aside.

The letters were next. Tied with string that had gone brown with age. I did not untie them.

The journal was beneath the letters. Dark leather, broken spine, the edges soft and dirty from being carried in a pocket. I had seen it before. I had held it before. I had been told to read it in order and I had not read it in order, and I set it aside with the papers and did not open it.

The map was beneath the journal. I unfolded it enough to see that it was not of Alderwick. I had known that. He had told me that. A district map, the kind a constabulary prints and does not sell, marked in ink over many years in more than one shade. I folded it back and set it aside.

The two bottles. The brass button. The ring.

And the cloth.

I lifted the cloth last. It was folded into a square, and the shape was still pressed into the weave where the Gear had lain for fifty years. I looked at it. I did not touch the shape. I folded the cloth back and set it aside with the rest.

Then I went back to the papers.

---

There were perhaps thirty of them. I spread them on the table in the grey light and tried to make sense of them, and for a while I could not.

They were reports. I understood that much. The kind of thing a constable writes when something happens in his parish and the office requires an account of it. A theft. A death. A dispute over a boundary. A man found drunk on the common. A woman missing for three days and then found. The ordinary business of an ordinary town, set down in the ordinary hand of a man who had done it for fifty years and had been good at it, and who had been good at it because he understood that the writing was not the thing but the record of the thing, and that the two were not the same.

I picked up the first one.

It was dated March of 1803. A report concerning a disturbance at the mill. Three men, one of them injured, the matter settled by the constable without reference to the bench. The handwriting was Vale's. I knew it. I had seen it in the Agreement, in the journal, in the notes he had left in the vestry book. Small, precise, unhurried. The hand of a man who wrote things down because he had learned what happened when you did not.

I read it through. Then I set it down and picked up the next.

This one was dated June of 1803. A report concerning a theft from the ironmonger's shop. The matter was unresolved. The constable had interviewed three witnesses and found their accounts inconsistent. He had written: *I cannot determine which of them was telling the truth. I have recorded all three accounts and left the matter open.*

I read that twice.

*I cannot determine which of them was telling the truth.*

I had said that myself, or something like it, in my own notebook, in my own words, and I had not known that I had learned it from him, or that he had learned it from somewhere, or that the somewhere mattered.

I set it down.

I picked up the next.

---

It was the fourth paper that stopped me.

It was a report dated September of 1804. A matter concerning a death at the poor-house. A man named Hobbs. The report was written in Vale's hand. It was three pages long. It described the circumstances of the death, the evidence gathered, the conclusion reached. The conclusion was that the death was natural and required no further investigation.

I read it through.

Then I turned it over, because there was writing on the back, and the writing on the back was also Vale's, and it said:

*Second copy. Returned 14 Sept. Original retained.*

I looked at that for a long time.

Then I went back through the papers and found that not all of them had writing on the back. Some did. Some did not. The ones that did had the same formula, or near it. *Second copy. Returned.* And a date. And sometimes, though not always, a single word or a short phrase that I could not immediately make sense of. *Altered.* Or: *Not as sent.* Or, once, in a hand that was slightly less steady than the rest: *The third time.*

I sat with that.

I did not write it down. I did not need to. I had been taught to keep what I saw separate from what I concluded, and what I saw was this: that some of the reports had been sent out and come back, and that the ones that came back were not always the ones that went out, and that Vale had known it, and had kept the originals, and had marked them, and had said nothing about it to anyone, or had said it only to Calloway, twenty years later, in the form of a remark about copies, and had left me to find the rest.

What I concluded was separate from that, and I kept it separate, and I did not write it down, and the conclusion was this:

Someone had altered the reports between the office and wherever they went after the office. And Vale had noticed. And Vale had kept the originals because a man who has only one copy of a thing does not own it. And Vale had said nothing because saying nothing was safer than saying something, and because he had learned, in whatever life he had lived before Alderwick, that the safest thing to do with a piece of information you cannot yet use is to keep it where no one will think to look for it.

In a box of personal effects.

Among the letters and the button and the ring.

Where it was nothing. Where it was only the papers of an old man. Where no one would think to look for it because no one would think it was anything but the papers of an old man.

I sat with that for a while.

Then I went back to the papers and began again, from the beginning, in order, because that was what he had told me to do, and because I understood now why he had told me.

---

Calloway came on Monday.

He came alone this time, or I thought he came alone until I saw the younger man standing at the end of the lane with his face turned the other way, as though he had been there a while and had heard the knock and had decided not to come up. I noticed that. I noticed also that Calloway did not look back at him.

I let him in.

He stood in the room the way he had stood before, with his weight spread and his hat in his hand, and he looked at the table where the papers were spread, and I understood that he had expected me to have opened the box by now, and that the expecting was not a guess but a calculation, and that he had calculated correctly.

"You have looked at them," he said.

"Yes."

"And?"

"They are reports. Parish matters. Some of them are old. Some of them are very old."

"From Vale's time."

"Yes."

He nodded. He rubbed his jaw with the heel of his hand, slowly, the way he had done before.

"The office has an interest in them," he said.

"I understand that."

"Then you will give them to me."

"I will give you the ones that belong to the office."

He looked at me. I looked back. The room was cold because I had not lit the fire, and I did not offer him a chair because I had not thought of it, and we stood.

"Which ones belong to the office?" he said.

"The reports. The ones that were written in the course of his duty. The ones that went out and were supposed to come back."

"And the rest?"

"The rest are his."

"His papers."

"His papers. His letters. His journal. His map. The things that were not written for the office and were not the office's business."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, someone passed the window, and the cart went by that passed every afternoon, and the town continued around us in the way it had continued around Vale for fifty years and would continue around me for whatever time was left to me.

"You understand," Calloway said, "that I cannot take your word for which is which."

"No. You cannot."

"Then I will need to look at them myself."

"You may look at them. You may not take them."

"That is not what I asked."

"No."

He was quiet again. I watched him decide. I had been taught by the old constable to watch a man decide, and to notice what the deciding cost him, and I noticed that the deciding cost Calloway something, though I could not have said what.

"Very well," he said. "I will look at them. You will be present. You will tell me which are official and which are personal. I will take the official ones. I will leave the personal ones. That is the arrangement."

"That is the arrangement."

He sat down. I sat down. I gathered the papers from the table and began to sort them into two piles, and Calloway watched, and neither of us spoke, and the sorting took a long time because I was careful, and because being careful was the only thing I had that was mine.

---

I separated the reports first. There were nineteen of them. I set them in a pile on the left. The letters, the journal, the map, the two bottles, the button, the ring, the cloth — I set these aside, on the right, and I did not explain them, and Calloway did not ask.

Then I went through the reports one by one.

Some of them were straightforward. A theft. A dispute. A death. The kind of thing that went out of the office and came back and was filed and forgotten. I set these in the left pile.

Some of them had writing on the back. *Second copy. Returned.* I set these in a separate pile, between the two, and I did not explain why, and Calloway looked at them, and I watched him look at them, and I understood that he knew what the writing meant, or suspected, and that the knowing or the suspecting was not new.

"These," he said.

"What about them?"

"The ones with writing on the back."

"Yes."

"What does it mean?"

"It means he kept a copy."

"A second copy."

"Yes."

"And the ones that came back?"

"I do not know."

That was true. I did not know. I knew what I had concluded, and I kept the conclusion separate, and I did not give it to him, and the not-giving was not a lie and was not a refusal, and was the thing Vale had taught me to do when the truth was not yet mine to give.

Calloway looked at me for a moment longer than the answer deserved.

"Very well," he said. "I will take the official reports. The ones without the writing. The ones that are straightforward. The others I will leave, for now."

"For now."

"For now."

He gathered the reports from the left pile. He did not count them, but I counted them, and there were eleven, and the eight that remained were the ones with writing on the back, and I understood that Calloway had left them because he wanted to come back for them, and that the coming back was the point, and that the point was not the papers but the question, and that the question would remain until it was answered, and that Calloway was a man who carried a question until it was answered.

He stood. He put on his hat. He turned toward the door.

"One thing," he said.

"Yes."

"The journal. You said he left a journal."

"Yes."

"Is it among the personal effects?"

"Yes."

"Then I will leave it. For now."

He went out. The younger man at the end of the lane turned and walked with him, and I watched them go, and I noticed that the younger man did not look back, and that this also was a thing done on purpose, and that I had noticed it before, and that the noticing was becoming a habit, and that the habit was becoming the thing I told myself was observation when it might have been something else.

I shut the door.

---

I went back to the table.

The eight reports with writing on the back were still there. I picked them up and looked at them again, in the grey light, and I understood that I had been given a thing I had not asked for, and that the giving was not generosity, and that Calloway had left them because he wanted me to look at them, and because the looking was the thing he could not do himself, and because the looking was the thing that would tell him what he needed to know without his having to ask.

I did not write that down.

I took the eight reports and I spread them on the table, and I went through them one by one, and I compared them to what I remembered of the ones Calloway had taken, and I found that the ones Calloway had taken were clean. No writing on the back. No marks. No indications. They were the reports that went out and came back and were filed and forgotten.

The eight that remained were the ones that had come back wrong.

I did not know how they had come back wrong. I did not know who had made them wrong. I did not know when they had been made wrong. I knew only that they had been made wrong, and that Vale had known it, and that he had kept the originals because the originals were the true thing, and the true thing was the only thing that mattered, and the keeping of it was the only thing a man could do when the world had decided that the true thing was not the thing that would survive.

I gathered the eight reports and I set them aside, in a separate bundle, tied with a piece of string I found in the press, and I wrote on a scrap of paper: *Second copies. Retained. Not returned.* And I put the scrap in the bundle, and I set the bundle in the box, and I closed the box, and I put the box back in the press.

Then I sat down.

Then I took out my notebook.

I wrote:

*Calloway came. Took eleven reports. Left eight. The eight are the ones with writing on the back. He knows what the writing means. He did not say what he knows. He left them for me to look at. The looking is the question.*

I looked at that.

Then I wrote:

*Vale kept second copies of reports that came back altered. He marked them. He kept the originals. He said nothing. He kept them in the box, among the personal effects, where no one would think to look for them.*

I looked at that.

Then I wrote:

*I do not know who altered them. I do not know why. I do not know when. I do not know how extensive it was. I know only that it happened, and that Vale knew, and that he kept the evidence, and that the evidence is now mine.*

I looked at that for a long time.

Then I closed the notebook.

---

I went back to the box the next day.

Not because I had to. Because I wanted to understand.

I opened it and I looked at the arrangement of the things inside, and I understood something I had not understood before.

The papers were on top. The letters were beneath the papers. The journal was beneath the letters. The map was beneath the journal. The bottles, the button, the ring, the cloth — these were at the bottom, beneath everything.

That was not an accident.

That was an arrangement.

The papers were on top because they were the things that belonged to the office. The things that would be asked for. The things that would be taken. The reports. The official documents. The things that were not his but the parish's, not his but the constable's, not his but the institution's.

The letters were beneath because they were his. Personal. Private. The things that were not the office's business.

The journal was beneath the letters because it was more private still. The thing he had written for himself and not for anyone else. The thing he had told me to read in order and not the end first.

The map was beneath the journal because it was the most private of all. The thing he could have burned thirty years ago and did not, and had never been able to give himself a reason for keeping.

And the bottles, the button, the ring, the cloth — these were at the bottom because they were the things that were nothing, and the things that were not nothing but were nothing to me, and the things that were the residue of a life that had been lived before Alderwick, and that had been lived in a way I would never know, and that had left these fragments behind, and that the fragments were all that remained.

He had arranged them.

He had arranged them before he died.

He had arranged them so that the office things were on top, and the personal things were beneath, and the things that were neither office nor personal but only his were at the very bottom, where no one would think to look for them unless they had been told to look, and he had not told me to look, and I had not looked, and the not-looking was the thing he had intended, and the intention was the thing I was only now beginning to understand.

He had separated them.

He had separated them before I ever opened the box.

He had separated them because he knew that someone would come asking, and that the asking would come, and that the separation was the answer to the asking, and that the answer was not a refusal but an arrangement, and that the arrangement was the thing he had left me, and that the thing he had left me was not the box but the method, and that the method was the only thing that mattered.

I sat with that for a while.

Then I closed the box.

Then I put it back in the press.

Then I lit the fire.

Then I sat in the chair and I did not open my notebook, and I did not write anything down, and I let the room be quiet, and I let the fire be warm, and I let the evening come down outside the window, and I did not think about anything in particular, and that was the nearest thing to peace I had had since Thursday, and I understood that the peace was not the absence of the question but the presence of the method, and that the method was the thing he had left me, and that the thing he had left me was enough.

For now.
