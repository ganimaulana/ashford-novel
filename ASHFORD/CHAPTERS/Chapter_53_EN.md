# Chapter 53 — The Margin

The fire had gone out in the night and the room was cold. Rain began just after eight, thin and steady, the kind that does not lift before noon. The constable had not sent for me, and no one had come to the door with a complaint that was not quite a complaint. I had done what errands the weather allowed, and there was nothing waiting for me.

I meant only to enter the Greyhound matter in my ledger. A closed case still needs a line in a book: the dates, the fee, the finding, and then the file where it will keep. I took down the fair copy of the report I had kept for myself, with the notebook behind it, turned to the last leaf, and read the finding as a man re-reads a letter he has already sent, not to learn anything but because his eyes go there.

*The case is closed as far as the available evidence permits. I do not know who the men were or why the sequence occurred. I know only that the reconstruction that best fits the evidence is that there were two of them.*

It was still the end of the matter; nothing had come to the door since. But the rain had given me the whole day and nothing to spend it on, and the report lay open in front of me. I pushed back the cloth, spread the papers across the table, weighed their loose corners down with the inkwell, and set out to prove myself wrong. There were no new facts, and I wanted none.

I began with the day itself. On a clean sheet I drew a long horizontal line and set into it the marks I had established: nine in the morning, the well-dressed man, dark coat, polished boots, no bag, coin paid, no name given, Room 4 for the night. Ten, down the stair, toward the front door; nobody saw him through it. Noon, the key on its hook; nobody knew who had put it back. Two, a man in the dark coat crossing the square toward the church; the witnesses had the coat more sure than the face. Six, the rough coat and the muddy boots, the satchel, the asking for Room 4 by number, the key given up without proof asked. Before light, gone.

Then I looked at the blank of the line between ten and noon, and read aloud from my own notebook, in the voice I had used there: *The question is not who the man is, but what happened during the missing hour.*

Two hours had been missing. Ten and twelve sat at the ends of the blank, plain as fenceposts, and I had called the gap an hour because the word had sat neatly in the sentence. I had written it without looking at the interval itself. I crossed *hour* out, wrote *two hours*, found that worse, and amended it to *missing interval*, which was neither pretty nor false. Small work; and the first of a day spent going back over my own phrases.

My assignment now was to make the one-man explanation strong. I had spent a week calling it weak, and a thing may be weak and still be right.

If one man had done all of it, he must have left the room unwitnessed, or not left it; returned the key unseen before noon; changed coat and boots out of all eyes; acquired a satchel he did not carry in the morning; and come back at dusk in other clothes and surprised no one. Five links, and not one eye on any of them. I recited the timings into the cold room as if someone might object: from Room 4 to the gate was under three minutes; between ten and six the day held eight full hours. He could have done every thing on that list. I waited for the sentence to break, and it did not break; and I noticed that I had wanted it to, that my relief at the two-man story was leaning on a wish, not a measurement.

Seeing, not time, pressed against the one-man story; and the seeing of that day had its edge: the wall at the end of the lane. The maid's window, the stableman's yard, the innkeeper's bar were all the sight the case contained, and every one of those eyes was lent to somebody's ordinary calling; none of the callings was following a stranger toward the church or past the wall. The morning visitor's departure had been, in my own phrase, *an assumption, not an observation*; until that hour I had used the line only against witnesses. I had to set it against myself as well, and the one-man story stayed where it had begun: possible, uncatchable, alive.

The stronger part of the reconstruction was what I had called physical evidence. Room 4 had held two boot patterns: a polished heel near the door, running toward the bed, and a heavier, mud-edged print running toward the window. I had knelt in that dust and thought: two patterns, two boots, two men.

On the sheet I redrew both paths and tried to make them with one man's feet. Polished boots in the morning, from the door to the bed, and out before noon; the key set back on its hook by his own hand; then rough, muddy boots arriving at six, crossing first to the window, to watch the yard he had come by. It fitted. The maid had swept the corridor, not the room; the dust kept whatever order it was given and dated nothing. What the prints proved was one sentence long and contained no man at all: Room 4 had held two pairs of boots, on some day or days. If one pair belonged to each of two visitors, well; if two pairs had hung in one lodger's closet, the room could not object, and neither could I.

Setting the drawn prints back on the scale, everything that remained on it was the counting I had done in the report's last pages: the one-man day required five unseen things to happen; the two-man day required one unseen man, and fewer doings besides. The counting had not changed; it still came out two men. But I could see it now for the first time without the room's voice pretending to agree with it — a balance, not a verdict.

The rain lifted after three. Sitting over the cold papers had worked the chill into my hands, so I put on my coat and went out for bread. My way took me past the Greyhound: the door stood open and warm, smelling of wet wool and something in a pot, laughing inside, and the innkeeper's among it. My way did not take me in. I had no question left that he could answer.

Margaret gave me the loaf without asking, wrapped in the usual paper, while she told me candles had risen again and that the chapel wedding had been counted a good one on account of the weather.

"You have a look," she said, "like a man counting."

"Not money," I said.

She looked at me the way she looks at the dough. "You have looked worse," she said, took what was owed, and let me go. I was grateful for that too.

I went home the long way and stopped at the turn above the yard wall, where the seeing of the afternoon man had ended for every witness in the case. From there I looked down the road the way they must have looked at it. A man on that road was walking toward the church; and that same man could have turned back through the inn's yard, or gone in at a door, or reached the church and done something else entirely after the last eye left him — and every one of them would have sat behind the same testimony. Toward the church. The walking was seen, and the direction was named, and nothing past them was. No one watched me stop. No one watched me turn. I came in by the grey of the afternoon, lit the candle early, and took from the drawer the pen whose ink dries brown-black, so that any mark I made in my own report would never be mistaken for the report.

I read it from the top and ringed every verb that worked harder than the facts worked under it. I found one that I had to change. The evening visitor, I had written, *showed* by his manner that he expected the room. The manner was all any of them had seen: the man had not asked whether a room was to be let; he had asked for the key to Room 4, and had been surprised by nothing in the bar. *Showed* quietly carried me from what the innkeeper saw to what I believed, a line drawn under a word until it looked like part of the writing. I amended it: *behaved as a man expecting it.* Weaker words, and none of them false.

The other verbs, when I hunted them down, confessed to being what they were. *Consistent with. I assumed. May have.* And in the finding, *supports* — the evidence *supports* the conclusion that there were two of them. I had chosen that word with more care than any other in the report, in the first writing, to stop the sentence short of a proof; and it had been holding the sentence there ever since. I drew one line beneath it, the mark that is not a ring, and left it standing exactly where it was.

For my third sheet I ruled three columns and wrote across them POSSIBLE, PROBABLE, PROVEN, and moved in only the claims that had to find their place. The morning man, the coin, the promise, the key on the hook at noon, the satchel, the stairs at six: the room's whole day, proven because I had stood inside the room and handled its register and its dust. The like face, height, and walk: resemblance observed, identity unknown; into the first column. The one man: possible; alive after all the day's attacks; into the first column. The two men: probable; the lighter load of unseen things; into the second. The identity of either man, and the reason for any of it: nowhere in the last column, and nowhere in the second but as surmise.

One thing I still had to look at straight. The claim that kept my finding standing, the second man knowing the room's number, had no observation under it at all. In the one-man story the answer lived inside the question: he knew the number of his own room. In mine, the second man knew it from somewhere, and no evidence in the case said where, and the ways I had listed in the notebook — told by the first man, or known from a previous visit, or from some other source I had not found out — were guesses wearing the coats of options. I wrote against the dangling claim, in brown-black: *manner of knowing, unknown.* The gap did not overturn the finding. It was on the two-man side, where I had never wanted to put it, and now it lay flat between me and the finding and would not move back.

The whole shape of that day had been said, over the bar, before I wrote any of it down: *I am certain it was the same face. I cannot say it was the same man.*

The finding stood as it stood. I made no second report and no return to the inn; what had gone to the constable had gone, and this copy asked nothing of anyone but my own eyes. I put the sheet of columns against the finding inside the cover, and small, in a corner where I would see it if the file ever lived another life at this table: *Tested against my own attacks. Stands as before. Not proven.* Then I folded the drawn-out day into its place, laid the notebook on top with its pages rucked as they had come, went to the shelf where the finished things were kept, set the Greyhound bundle in among the other closed cases, shut the box, and turned it until its number faced the middle of the room. There was no work left in it.

The fire wanted feeding. I went out through the returning rain for the kindling at the coal-corner and brought it in armful by armful, and stacked it under the sink — all but a small bundle, which I set in the hall by the door, and looked at, and judged, and thought enough to last until Thursday, and a little over.
