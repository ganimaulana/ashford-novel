CHAPTER 99 — THE LAST TWELVE

The note came before dawn. I had not slept well. The granary still smelled of grain and dust in my memory, and the weight of the crate I had carried lingered in my shoulders. When I opened the door, the paper was on the floor. It said only: The armoury. Six o'clock. Bring nothing.

I dressed and went down. The courtyard was grey and cold. Fourteen of us stood in the dark, waiting. I counted them—thirteen others, including myself. The number was smaller than it had been the day before. Two men were gone. I did not see them leave. I did not ask.

Hunter was there, standing near the wall, his hands in his pockets. He was not looking at anyone. He was looking at the armoury—a long, low stone building with a tiled roof and heavy doors, set apart from the main complex at the far end of the eastern grounds.

Calloway came out of the main building. He waited until the murmuring ceased.

"This is the individual stage," he said. "You will no longer work in groups. You will each receive a ledger. The ledger lists eight items stored in the armoury. You will enter the building, locate each item, and bring it to the clerk's desk in the main hall. You will not open the armoury doors yourself. The clerk will admit you one at a time. You will have until the bell rings at noon. The twelve candidates who complete the most items will be recorded as passing. The remaining two will be recorded as not passing."

He paused.

"The ledger contains location descriptions. Some are accurate. Some are not. The building has two floors. The loft is accessible by a narrow staircase. The east storeroom has been cleared and is empty. You will decide which entries to trust. You will decide which items to pursue. You will decide how to carry them. The office will observe all of it."

He turned and walked back inside.

The clerk stepped forward with fourteen ledgers, each bound in dark leather. He handed them out without speaking. I took mine. The cover was plain. I opened it.

The first page read: "Armoury Register — Individual Retrieval." Below that were eight entries:

1. Set of brass weights, medium, west storeroom.
2. Bundle of correspondence, light, clerk's desk, main hall.
3. Coil of hemp rope, heavy, east storeroom.
4. Tin lantern, light, loft, on the packing case.
5. Crate of nails, heavy, under main stair.
6. Leather satchel, light, loft, on the hay bale.
7. Iron key, medium, east storeroom, on the hook.
8. Bundle of blank forms, light, west storeroom, on the shelf.

I read it twice. The entries were simple. The weights were noted. The locations were specific.

I looked up. The other candidates were already reading their ledgers. Some were turning pages quickly, their eyes scanning. Others were reading slowly, their faces closed. Hunter was standing still, his ledger in his hand, his head down. He was not reading. He was looking at the armoury.

I looked at the armoury too. The building was older than the granary, its stone darker, its windows smaller. The main doors were heavy, bound with iron. To the left of the main doors, I could see a smaller door, probably leading to the east storeroom. But the building had been here for years. The east storeroom had been cleared—Calloway had said so. If it was cleared, it was empty. Entries three and seven—rope and key—both placed in the east storeroom, were likely wrong.

I looked at the main stair. It rose from the centre of the main hall, wide and wooden, its treads worn. Entry five—crate of nails under the main stair—seemed unlikely. The space beneath the stair was enclosed, not open. A crate would not fit there unless it had been placed deliberately, and the ledger gave no indication that it had been.

I had not yet entered the building. I had not seen the west storeroom or the loft. But from the outside, I could already see that two of the eight entries were probably erroneous. A third was questionable.

I turned the page. The back of the ledger carried a single instruction: "You may not carry more than two items at once. You may not re-enter after you have left. You may not ask for assistance. You may not open doors that are already open. You may not force any door or lock."

I read it again. "You may not re-enter after you have left."

That was the constraint. Once I left the armoury, I was done. No second trip. No correction. I had to choose which items to pursue and in what order, and I had to live with the consequences.

I looked at the clerk. He was standing near the armoury door, a list in his hand. He began to read names.

When my name was called, I walked to the door. The clerk unlocked it and pushed it open. The interior was dim, the air cool and dry. The main hall was large, its stone floor swept clean. At the far end, a staircase led to the loft. To the left, a corridor led to the west storroom. To the right, a smaller door stood closed—probably the east storeroom.

I stepped inside. The clerk closed the door behind me.

I stood still for a moment, letting my eyes adjust. The hall was quiet. I could hear the faint creak of the building settling. I looked at the clerk's desk—a heavy oak table near the door, with a row of drawers. I looked at the main stair, wide and worn. I looked at the corridor to the west.

I opened my ledger and reviewed the entries.

Entry two: bundle of correspondence, light, clerk's desk, main hall.

I walked to the desk. It was heavy, its surface scarred with use. I opened the top drawer. Inside were papers, tied with string. I lifted them out. They were light, exactly as the ledger said. I carried them to the designated collection point near the door—a table where candidates were to place their items. I set them down.

Entry eight: bundle of blank forms, light, west storeroom, on the shelf.

I walked to the corridor. The west storeroom door was unlocked. I pushed it open. The room was narrow, lined with shelves. Most were empty. But on the far shelf, near the window, I saw a bundle of papers tied with rough twine. I carried them back to the collection table.

Two items. Both correct. Both light. I had used minimal effort.

I returned to the main hall and stood for a moment, considering the remaining entries.

Entry one: brass weights, medium, west storeroom.

I had already been in the west storroom. I had not seen brass weights. The shelf where the forms had been was the only shelf with anything on it. If the weights were there, they would have been visible. Either they were on a different shelf I had not noticed, or the entry was wrong. I decided it was wrong.

Entry four: tin lantern, light, loft, on the packing case.

The loft. I looked up at the staircase. It was narrow, the steps steep. Carrying a light load up would be manageable. The packing case would be near the top of the stairs, probably against the wall. This seemed plausible.

Entry six: leather satchel, light, loft, on the hay bale.

Hay bale. In an armoury? The building smelled of oil and iron and old wood. I had not seen hay. But packing cases were often packed with straw or hay to prevent movement. If there was a hay bale on the loft, the satchel would be on it. This also seemed plausible, though less certain than the lantern.

I decided to pursue both loft items. They were light. The stairs were narrow but climbable. I would make two trips up and down, carrying one item each time. That was within my strength.

I climbed the stairs. The steps creaked under my weight. The loft was dim, light coming through a high window. The floor was wooden, covered with dust. Against the far wall stood a large packing case, its corners reinforced with iron. On top of it sat a tin lantern, exactly as the ledger described. I picked it up. It was light. I carried it down and placed it on the collection table.

I climbed the stairs again. I looked around the loft. There was no hay bale. The packing case was the only object of any size. The floor was bare except for dust and a few scattered nails. If there had been a hay bale, it would have been against the wall or in a corner. There was nothing.

Entry six was wrong.

I descended the stairs. Three items completed. Correspondence, forms, lantern. All light. All correct. I had avoided the east storeroom entirely. I had dismissed the brass weights and the crate of nails based on my observation of the west storroom. I had checked the loft and found only one of the two items listed.

I had twenty minutes before the bell. I could attempt one more item. The remaining entries were:

3. Coil of hemp rope, heavy, east storeroom. (Probably wrong — east storeroom cleared.)
5. Crate of nails, heavy, under main stair. (Probably wrong — space too small.)
7. Iron key, medium, east storeroom, on the hook. (Probably wrong — same as above.)

I had no reason to trust any of these. But I had time for one medium item if I found one that seemed plausible.

I walked to the main stair and knelt. The space beneath it was enclosed by a low wall. It was dark. I could see nothing inside. A crate would not fit. Entry five was wrong.

I stood. I looked at the east storeroom door. It was closed. If I opened it, I would find nothing. The clerk had said it was cleared. I would waste time and energy for nothing.

I decided to stop. Three items was enough. I had been careful. I had verified what I could. I had not wasted effort on locations that were clearly wrong.

I walked to the collection table and stood beside it, waiting.

The bell began to ring.

The clerk entered. He looked at the table, at the items I had placed there, then at his ledger. He wrote something down.

"You have completed three items," he said.

I did not know whether that was good or bad. I only knew that I had made my decisions and could not change them.

The clerk turned and left. I walked out of the armoury.

The courtyard was filling with candidates. Some were carrying items. Some were empty-handed. I saw Hunter standing near the wall, his ledger closed, his arms crossed. He did not look at me.

I saw the candidate with the scarred knuckles running toward the armoury door, his face flushed. He had left too late. The clerk shook his head and wrote in his ledger.

I saw Peale standing near the fountain, his ledger in his hand, his face unreadable. He had not moved. He had been standing there since the beginning, reading and re-reading his ledger, unable to decide.

The clerk called out the first twelve names.

I heard my own.

I heard Hunter's.

I heard Bishop's.

I heard Ford's.

I heard Pearce's.

I heard Ellison's.

I heard the names of the others I had seen around the courtyard.

I did not hear Peale's name.

I did not hear the name of the candidate with the scarred knuckles.

The two men stepped forward. They did not speak. They did not look at the rest of us. They gathered their coats and followed the man in the grey coat toward the main building.

Twelve of us remained.

The clerk closed his ledger. He looked at us, standing in the courtyard, the morning light behind us.

"You are the last twelve," he said. "The next stage is the final examination. You will be told when to assemble."

He turned and walked away.

I stood in the courtyard, my shoulders aching from the climb up the loft stairs. I looked at the armoury, at the door that had closed behind me, at the collection table where my three items lay. I had completed fewer items than Hunter. I had seen him carry four—correspondence, lantern, forms, and a fifth item I could not identify. He had moved through the building like a man who knew exactly what he was doing.

But he had missed the leather satchel. It had not been on the loft, but if it had been, he would not have seen it. He had dismissed the loft too quickly.

I had completed three items. It had been enough. But it had been close.

I thought about the ledger. I had identified three erroneous entries out of eight. I had trusted my observation of the building over the written word. That had been the right choice. But I had also missed the leather satchel—not because I had dismissed it, but because it had not been there. My observation had been correct. The ledger had been wrong.

The examination was not about finding the most items. It was about deciding which items were worth finding.

I walked back to my room. The courtyard was emptying. The twelve of us would have time to rest before the final stage. I sat on the bed and looked out the window. The armoury stood at the far end of the field, dark and silent.

I thought about the two men who had left. The candidate with the scarred knuckles had moved too quickly, assuming the ledger was correct, wasting time in the east storeroom. Peale had moved too slowly, unable to trust any entry, frozen by uncertainty. Both had failed for opposite reasons. Both had been understandable.

I thought about Hunter. He had been fast and efficient, but he had also been willing to dismiss an entry without verifying it. That was a risk. It had paid off this time, because the satchel had not been there. But if it had been, he would have missed it.

I was slower. I was weaker. But I had verified what I could, and I had not pretended to certainty where I had none.

The final examination would be different. It would not be about items or ledgers or buildings. It would be about something else entirely. And I would have to face it with the same observation and the same judgment, but without a group to share the manifest with, without Bishop to carry the heaviest load, without Ford to pull me up when I stumbled.

I would be alone.

But for now, I was one of the last twelve.

I waited for the next note.
