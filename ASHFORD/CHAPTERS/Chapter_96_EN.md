CHAPTER 96 — THE DIVISION

The bell rang at noon. I had been sitting on the bed, the list of names still folded in my pocket, waiting. When I heard the sound, I rose and went down to the courtyard.

The candidates were already gathering. I counted them as they arrived—nineteen others, including myself. Twenty of us remained. The man with the notebook was not among us. The older man with the frayed cuffs was not among us. They had been eliminated in earlier rounds. I did not know where they had been sent.

Calloway stood on the low steps of the main building. He was holding a sheet of paper, but he did not read from it. He waited until we were silent.

“The examination is entering its next phase,” he said. “You will now be divided into groups. The groups have been determined by the office, based on your performance in the previous tasks. You will not be told the criteria for the division. You will be told your group assignment. You will then proceed to your designated area. Each group will be given a task. The task will be different for each group. You will not be told how your performance will be evaluated.”

He paused.

“The clerk will read the group assignments.”

He stepped back. The clerk stepped forward, carrying the ledger. He opened it and began to read.

I listened. I heard Ellison’s name called with three men I did not know. I heard Peale’s name called with three men I had barely noticed. I heard Weaver’s name called with Merritt and two others. I heard Hunter’s name called with three men who looked as though they had worked together before.

Then I heard my own name: “Leon Ashcroft, with Bishop, and two others.”

I did not know the two others. They were candidates I had seen but never spoken to. Bishop I knew from the crate-moving task. He was quiet, competent, and did not speak unless necessary.

I looked for Bishop and found him standing near the back. He nodded when he saw me. I nodded back.

The clerk closed the ledger. Calloway stepped forward again.

“You will go to your assigned areas. The tasks will begin at dawn. You will be told more when you arrive. You are dismissed until then.”

He turned and walked back into the building.

The candidates dispersed. Some walked toward the dining hall. Others stood in groups, talking in low voices. I saw Weaver standing near the well, his hands in his pockets, his face unreadable. I walked over to him.

“You are not with me,” I said.

“I am not,” he said. “But I am with Merritt.”

“That is better than being with strangers.”

“Perhaps,” he said. “But I do not know whether the office grouped us together because we are useful, or because we are expendable.”

I did not answer. I did not know either.

The names that had become familiar over the past days were now scattered. I did not know whether the office intended to break the alliances we had formed, or whether it simply did not care which of us worked together.

I looked around the courtyard. Hunter was already walking toward the eastern gate with his group. The three men with him moved as though they had worked together before, falling into step without discussion. He did not look back. Ellison was speaking with his new companions, his hands moving as he explained something. Peale was standing apart, watching the others with the same quiet attention he had shown from the beginning.

I walked toward the eastern gate, where my group was supposed to assemble. Bishop was already there, standing with the two other candidates. I did not know their names. One was tall, with dark hair and a quiet face. The other was shorter, with a coat that was too thin for the weather.

“You are Leon Ashcroft,” the tall one said.

“I am.”

“I am Ford. This is Pearce.”

Pearce nodded. He did not speak.

“We are to go to the storehouse,” Ford said. “The same storehouse you visited yesterday.”

“I know the way.”

We walked. The grounds were quiet. The gate was open. Beyond it, I could see the storehouse—a low building of stone and timber, its roof sagging, its windows boarded. I had been there before, but I had not expected to return.

We reached the door. It was unlocked. I pushed it open and stepped inside.

The interior was dark, but a lantern had been lit on a table near the far wall. On the table, there was a single object—an old lock, iron and rusted, with a keyhole that was empty. Beside it lay a folded paper.

I walked over and unfolded the paper. It read: “This lock was made by Aurelius Ashcroft. It was found in the old mill. You are to determine whether it can be opened. You are not to open it. You are to report what you observe.”

I read it again. Aurelius Ashcroft. The name was familiar. I had seen it in the Agreement, in the documents Vale had left behind. I had not expected to encounter it here.

Ford came up beside me. “What does it say?”

I handed him the paper. He read it silently, then handed it to Pearce.

“Aurelius Ashcroft,” Ford said. “I have not heard that name.”

“I have,” I said. “But I do not know what it means.”

Bishop walked to the table and examined the lock. He did not touch it. He simply looked at it, turning his head slowly.

“It is old,” he said. “The iron is rusted, but the mechanism is intact. The keyhole showed little wear.”

I considered the implications of that, but I did not speak them. The observation was enough. I knelt beside him and looked more closely. The rust was uneven, thicker on the sides than on the face, as though it had been handled more often in some places than others. The keyhole was not perfectly centered—it sat slightly to the left of the lock's centre line. Whether that was deliberate or the result of age, I could not tell.

I looked at the lock. It was heavy, the metal dark with age. The keyhole was small, the shape of it irregular. I did not know whether it was meant to be opened, or whether the instruction was itself a test.

“We are not to open it,” I said. “Only to observe.”

“Then we observe,” Pearce said.

We stood around the table for a long time. I looked at the lock from every angle. I looked at the paper, the handwriting, the way the words were arranged. I looked at the keyhole, the rust, the faint marks on the surface.

I did not know what the lock was for, or why Aurelius's name was on it. But the name had appeared in the examination, and I did not know why.

Ford knelt beside the table. “There is a mark on the underside,” he said. “A symbol. A circle with three lines.”

I went to look. There was indeed a mark, faint but deliberate—the same symbol I had seen on the Agreement, on the documents Vale had kept. It was the mark that had first drawn me into the keeping. The lines were shallow, worn smooth by time, but the circle was clear. I traced its outline with my finger, not touching the metal itself.

“I have seen that mark before,” I said.

“Where?” Bishop asked.

“In papers that belonged to the man who kept the door before me.”

Ford looked at me, then at the lock. “He kept a door?”

“He kept something,” I said. “I do not know what. But he kept it.”

Pearce spoke for the first time. “And the lock?”

“I do not know that either.”

They did not ask more. I did not offer more. The silence that followed was not uncomfortable. It was the silence of men who had learned not to press for answers that were not offered.

I folded the paper and put it in my pocket.

“We have observed,” I said. “We should report to the clerk.”

We walked back to the courtyard. The clerk was still standing near the main building, his ledger open, a pencil in his hand. He looked at us as we approached.

“You have completed the task,” he said.

“We have,” I said. “We observed the lock. It is old. The keyhole is not worn. The mechanism appears intact. The paper says it was made by Aurelius Ashcroft.”

The clerk wrote something in his ledger. He did not look surprised.

“You may go,” he said.

The clerk’s expression had not changed when I spoke Aurelius Ashcroft’s name. He had written it in his ledger as though he had known it would be there.

I walked back to my room. The sun was low. The field was empty.

I sat on the bed and took out the list of names from the previous task—ten names, including my own. I did not know what it meant. Aurelius Ashcroft had made the lock, and the office had placed it before me. The same symbol from the Agreement was carved into its underside. I did not know why.

The division had scattered the candidates I had come to know. Weaver and Merritt were together, but I was with strangers. Hunter was already moving toward his own task. I did not know whether I would see him again.

I thought about the clerk’s face when I spoke Aurelius’s name. He had not been surprised. He had written it down as though he had been waiting for it. That was what unsettled me more than the lock itself—the office had known what they were placing before me, and they had chosen not to explain. They had simply set it in my path and waited to see what I would do.

I did not know what they wanted. But I knew the examination was no longer only about completing tasks. The clerk had known Aurelius's name before I spoke it. The symbol on the lock matched the one on the Agreement. The office had placed both before me without explanation.

They were not testing whether I could find things. They were testing whether I would recognise them when I saw them. And whether I would understand what that recognition meant.

I did not know the answer to that yet. But I knew the question was now mine to carry.
