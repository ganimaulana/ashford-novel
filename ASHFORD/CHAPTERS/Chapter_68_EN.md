CHAPTER 68 — THE KEPT RESPONSE

I carried both medallions for a week.

I did not go to the common. I did not go to the mill. I did not go to the eastern marker. I carried the two medallions — one warm, one cold — and I went about my days, and I waited.

I did not know what I was waiting for. I had taken the second medallion from the house at the end of Bellweather Lane, and I had carried it, and I had tested it at the keeper's sign, and it had not answered. I had not tested it anywhere else. I had not taken it back to the house at the end of the lane. I had not taken it to Vale's house. I had not taken it to the house that appeared at the edge of the common.

I had carried both medallions, and I had waited, and on the eighth day, I went back to the house at the end of Bellweather Lane.

I did not know why I went. I had taken the second medallion from that house. I had not expected to return. I had not expected the house to matter again.

But I went. I walked the length of Bellweather Lane, past Vale's house, to the place where the lane narrowed and turned and ran out into the fields. The house stood where it had stood. Empty. Dark. Forgotten.

I stood before it. I did not open the door. I took the second medallion from my coat, and I held it in my hand, and I stood before the house. It was cold. It had been cold for a week. I had carried it against my skin, and it had stayed cold, and I had begun to think it would never warm.

I stood before the house at the end of Bellweather Lane, with the cold medallion in my hand, and I waited.

And then it warmed.

Not slowly. Not gradually. It warmed as if it had been waiting — as if the house had reached out and touched it, and it had remembered.

I stood very still. The medallion was warm in my hand. It had not warmed at the keeper's sign at the common. It had not warmed at the door. It had not warmed anywhere I had carried it. It warmed here, before the house at the end of Bellweather Lane, the house where it had been kept, the house where it had waited, the house that had held it in a windowless room, in a box, for longer than anyone could remember.

I held the warm medallion in my hand, and I looked at the house. It was the same house. Empty. Dark. Forgotten. But the medallion was warm. And it had warmed for this house, and for no other.

I put the medallion back in my coat. I stood before the house for a long time. I had carried the second medallion for a week, and it had stayed cold. I had brought it back to the house where it had been kept, and it had warmed. I did not know what that meant. But I knew that it was not an accident.

---

I walked home slowly. I sat at my desk. I took out both medallions. My own medallion — warm. The second medallion — warm. Both warm now.

My medallion had warmed at the common, at the keeper's sign, at the house that appeared, at the door. The second medallion had warmed at the house at the end of Bellweather Lane, at the house that had been kept, at the house that had held it.

Two medallions. Two houses. My medallion — the carried one — answered the house that appears. The second medallion — the kept one — answered the house that is kept.

I looked at the two medallions, both warm now, side by side on the desk. I understood, for the first time, that there were two houses, and two medallions, and two first signs — and that I had been wrong to think they were the same.

My medallion was the first sign of the house that appears. The second medallion was the first sign of the house that is kept.

Two houses. Two signs. Two medallions. And I was the keeper of both.

I opened the red book. I wrote:

The second medallion warms at the house at the end of Bellweather Lane — the kept house. It does not warm anywhere else.

My medallion warms at the common — at the house that appears — and at the keeper's sign, and at the door.

Two medallions. Two houses. The carried medallion answers the house that appears. The kept medallion answers the house that is kept. I am the keeper of both.

I do not know what the two houses are. I do not know why there are two medallions. But I know that they answer different houses. And I know that both have answered me.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer. I did not put the medallions away. I left them on the desk, both warm. Two medallions, side by side. The first sign, twice.

And I sat in the dark, and I thought about the two houses — the house that appears and the house that is kept — and about the two medallions that answered them, and about the door, and the signs, and everything I had been carrying all my life.

I had not known there were two. I had carried one medallion, and I had thought it was the whole. I had found the second, and I had thought it was the same. It was not the same. It was the other half.

The house that appears was the keeper's house — the house of the keeper's sign. The house that is kept was the maker's house — the house of the first sign. And I had two medallions, two signs, two houses. I was the keeper of both.

I did not know what it meant. But I knew that I would find out. And I knew that the two houses were not two mysteries. They were one mystery, divided. And I was the keeper of the whole.

---

The next morning, I went to the common before the light was fully up. I took both medallions. I knelt at the edge of the grass, where the keeper's sign was pressed into the earth. I held my medallion over it. It warmed.

I held the second medallion over it. It stayed cold. I had expected it to stay cold. It had stayed cold at the keeper's sign before. It warmed only at the house at the end of the lane.

I put both medallions back in my coat. I stood at the edge of the common, in the grey morning light, and I thought about the two houses, and the two medallions, and the two first signs.

The house that appears. The house that is kept. One appears when the keeper needs it. One is kept so that the maker is not forgotten. Two houses, one mystery. Two medallions, one sign, divided.

I walked home. I sat at my desk. I took out the red book, and I wrote:

Two houses. Two medallions. Two first signs. One house appears. One house is kept.

My medallion answers the house that appears. The second medallion answers the house that is kept. I am the keeper of both.

I do not know what the two houses are for. But I know that they are the same mystery, divided in two. And I know that I was given the medallion that answers the house that appears, and that I found the medallion that answers the house that is kept.

I was not given one. I was given both. I have been carrying the whole, all my life, without knowing that there was a whole.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer. I left both medallions on the desk. And I sat in the dark, and I looked at them, and I understood that the mystery had never been about the door. It had never been about the signs. It had been about the two houses, and the two medallions, and the keeping of both.

And I was the keeper of both. The house that appears. The house that is kept. I would go to both. I would learn both. And I would keep the whole.
