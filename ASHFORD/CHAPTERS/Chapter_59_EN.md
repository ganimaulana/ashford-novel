CHAPTER 59 — THE OTHER SILENCE

I did not open the notebook on Sunday afternoon. The office had come and gone. The file had been seen and returned to Calloway's coat. The leaf lay on the table. The eight papers stood in their bundle. The box was in the press. The kettle was cold. The fire was low.

I sat in the chair and looked at the wall opposite, because looking at the wall was the easiest thing to do when the room had been filled with questions and was now empty of them.

The Hartley girl's bowl was gone. I had returned it Thursday evening. The lane had been quiet. The house at the end of it had been shut. The week had continued, because the week always continued, whether a man wanted it to or not.

I rose after a time and went to the press. I opened the door. The box was there. The three bundles were on the lid. The uncertain paper lay on top of everything. I lifted the bundles aside and set them on the table, and I reached into the box and took out the service journal.

It was the same dark leather, broken spine, pocket-dirty edges. The same journal Vale had given me in October, with the instruction to read it in order and not the end first. I had read it once, in the weeks after the funeral, when the house was too quiet and the days were too long, and I had read it the way a man reads a thing he has been told not to read quickly, and I had finished it and closed it and put it back in the box, and I had not opened it again.

I opened it now.

The pages were soft. The ink was faded. The handwriting was smaller than Vale's usual hand, tighter, the way a man writes when he is recording a thing for himself and not for an audience. The first pages were garrison-town years. A price of bread. A disliked sergeant. A horse destroyed on a road Vale never named. A name that appeared four times and then stopped appearing, without explanation.

I turned the pages slowly. The Alderwick transfer entry. Then several blank pages. Then ordinary parish business — a rate collected, a drunkard discharged, a meeting of the vestry — as though a door had closed on the earlier man and the later one had agreed not to mention him.

I had read it before. I had noticed the omissions. But I had not seen the file, and the file made the omissions look different. The file was four lines of clerk's hand. The journal was eighty pages of Vale's own hand. And between the two records there was nothing. No intelligence work. No counter-intelligence. No Keeper role. No Door. No Gear. No investigation of compromised officers. No February inquiry. No mention of why a man who had held the desk for fifty years had been reassigned to a town that had no need of him.

The journal said nothing about the door beneath the mill. Leon had come to think that was a decision.

I closed the journal. I set it on the table beside the leaf.

I thought about the conversation in August, when he had given me the box and I had asked why he was leaving it to me. He had said there was no one else. He had said I came back. He had said I ask before I take. He had said if he made it more than that I would start behaving like a man who was chosen, and men who believe they were chosen stop checking their own work. He had left the question there, and I had accepted the answer, because the answer had been enough at the time.

It was not enough now.

Not because the file had revealed a secret. Because the file had revealed nothing, and the nothing was the same nothing that had been in the journal, and the same nothing that had been in the room for eight months whenever I asked a question that touched the edges of things he did not explain.

I thought about the coat on the back of the press. He had told me twice to take it. I had refused both times. He had not explained why he wanted me to have it. He had simply said "Take it" and left it at that.

I thought about the quotation. "It is the same meaning. It is not the same sentence." He had corrected me not because the words mattered but because the precision mattered, and the precision was a habit he had learned somewhere that was not Alderwick, and he had never told me where.

I thought about the hedge. An hour on a hedge. The way he had argued for the man who was wrong because the man who was right had been insufferable about it. The way he had stopped in the middle of a sentence, waited, then continued. "It is only the breath." Nothing important. Unless it was.

I thought about the journal again. "There is a great deal in it I did not want anyone to find. That is why it is in a box and not in an office." He had said that in August. The box was not the office. The journal was in the box. The journal said nothing about the things that were not in the office either. The office had its four lines. The journal had its garrison years and its blank pages and its parish business. The two records did not touch. They were two silences, separated by eighty years of leather and paper, and they were the same silence.

I did not reach a conclusion. I did not decide that Vale had been hiding things from me. I did not decide that he had been protecting me. I did not decide that he had been testing me. I simply noted the pattern, the way a man notes a mark on a shelf and does not assume he knows what made it.

The pattern was: Vale had a question he would not answer. He had a subject he would not explain. He had a past he would not name. He had a reason for leaving me the box that he would not state. He had a way of stopping short, of waiting, of letting a silence do the work of a sentence, and I had accepted it because he was Vale and I was Leon and that had been the arrangement.

The arrangement had ended. The silence remained.

I picked up the journal. I closed it. I put it back in the box, under the bundles, where it had been before. I shut the box. I set the box back in the press. I closed the press door.

The leaf lay on the table. The eight papers stood beside it. The clerk's ruled sheet was folded on top. The Hartley girl's bowl was not there. The fire was low. The kettle was cold. The lane was quiet. The week was not over. For now.
