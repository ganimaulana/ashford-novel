# Chapter 59 — The Paper Route

The week after the gate, the weather turned mild, and the frost that had held the lanes for a fortnight gave way to a damp, slow thaw. The roads became soft, and the gutters ran with brown water, and the fields beyond the hedges lay under a thin mist that did not lift until midday. I did not go to Vale on the Thursday that followed—I had a small matter for Calloway, a signature on a report that had been waiting—and when I went up the lane on the Friday instead, I found the cottage door closed and the fire unlit, which was not the way of that house. The smoke from the chimney had been cold for hours, and the windows were dark.

I knocked twice before he answered. The door opened slowly, and he was standing by the table with a letter in his hand, the paper creased and folded small, and he did not put it down when I came in. The fire was laid but not lit, and the room was colder than usual.

"You came on Friday," he said.

"The Thursday was taken."

He nodded, and laid the letter on the table, turning it over so that the address faced the wood. "I have an errand," he said. "The paper I use for the press—the old one in the shed—has run out. I would go myself, but the walk to the stationer's is longer than I care to take alone, and the ground is soft."

I said I would walk with him.

The stationer's shop stood on the far side of the market square, a narrow front between a chandler's and a baker's, with a sign that had not been painted in my memory. We walked through the lane and across the square, and Vale moved slowly, with the stick he had used for as long as I had known him, his coat buttoned to the neck against the damp. He did not speak for the first half of the walk, and I did not press him. The square was half-full, the market stalls dismantled for the day, the cobbles wet and shining under the grey sky. A few women carried baskets; a boy drove a sheep towards the slaughterhouse with a stick and a shout; a cart laden with sacks of flour stood outside the baker's, its driver asleep on the seat. The church clock struck the half-hour, and Vale did not look up. The smells of wet wool, coal smoke, and the yeast from the bakery mixed in the cold air, and I felt the damp settle on my coat as we walked.

He paused at the corner of the square, where the lane opened towards the church, and looked at the building for a long moment. The tower rose grey and square against the sky, its battlements worn, its windows narrow and dark. "The tower," he said, "was rebuilt in the year of the bad harvest—that was 1782, I think. The stone came from the quarry at the edge of the parish, and the mason who set it never saw it finished. He was dead of the ague before the last course was laid. They put his name on a plaque inside, but the plaque has since been covered by the new organ pipes."

I had not asked. He had not offered an explanation. He turned and walked on.

At the stationer's, he asked for a ream of the thick paper, the kind that took ink without bleeding, and the stationer—a small man with spectacles and ink-stained fingers—knew him by name and by the order. "The usual," he said, and Vale nodded. The stationer went to the back to fetch it, and I stood by the counter, watching Vale's hands. He had not touched the letter since he had turned it over, but I saw that his gaze had gone to the window, to the street outside, and that his attention was not on the shop. The shop was cramped, with shelves of ledgers and boxes of quills, the floorboards worn smooth by years of feet, and the air smelled of paper and gum arabic. I turned over a few sheets of the writing paper on the counter, feeling its weight and texture, and the stationer returned with the ream wrapped in brown paper, tied with string.

"You have been here before," I said. It was not a question.

"Many times," he said. "The paper is good. The man is honest. That is all I require."

The stationer returned with the paper wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. Vale paid him in coin, counted out from a purse that he kept in his coat, and we walked back the way we had come. The air had grown colder, and the grey was thickening into rain. Vale walked more slowly than before, and I matched his pace without comment.

At the edge of the square, he stopped again. This time he did not look at the church. He looked at a house on the corner, a tall narrow building with a slate roof and a door that had been painted black within living memory. The windows were dark, and the steps were worn, and the iron railing had rusted in several places. There was nothing about it that seemed to call for a second glance—no nameplate, no sign, no evidence of occupation. But Vale stood and looked at it for a long time, and his hands were still on the stick. The rain had begun to fall more steadily, running down the gutters and dripping from the eaves of the house, and still he did not move.

"Who lived there?" I asked.

"No one," he said. "Not now. A man named Ferrand, once, who kept records for the town. He was before my time, but his name is still on the books. The house was sold after his death, and it has stood empty since."

He said it without emotion, and he walked on. But I had heard the name before—Ferrand, in the office, the elder clerk had mentioned it in passing, a keeper of records who had been before even Calloway's predecessor. I had not thought of it then. I thought of it now, because Vale had said the name as if it belonged to a man he had known, and the house had been empty for longer than a man's memory.

We walked home in the rain, which came down steady and cold, the drops striking the cobbles and the roofs with a hissing sound that filled the lane. Vale went inside and set the paper on the press without comment. He made tea, and we sat in the usual silence, and he did not mention Ferrand or the house or the tower. The letter was still on the table, its seal broken. I could not read the address from where I sat, and I did not ask what it contained. By then I had learned that silence in Vale's house did not always mean secrecy. Sometimes it meant simply that a thing had not reached the hour for being spoken. The rain continued past the window, and the fire burned low before I left.

That evening, I wrote in my notebook:

*We walked to the stationer's today. Vale bought paper. He stopped at the church and told me about the stone, and he stopped at a house on the corner and said it had belonged to a man named Ferrand. He did not say why he stopped. He did not say why he told me. The letter on his table remains unmentioned.*

I did not write what I had not seen. I had not read the letter, and I did not know why Vale had looked at the house. I did not turn a glance into a fact simply because it had caught my attention, and I had learned, from the months of his teaching, that the thing not written is sometimes the thing that matters most.

The rain fell all night, and the wind came up, and I sat with the window closed and the fire burning, and I thought about the house on the corner, and the man whose name I had heard twice now—once from the elder clerk, once from Vale—and I did not know why the name should return to me in the same week that Vale had spoken it aloud. That was not an answer. It was only a question, and I was learning to live with questions that did not have answers yet.

Before we left the stationer, Vale asked whether the next delivery would be on Tuesday. The man behind the counter said yes, and Vale told him to put the parcel aside if he was not there.

"You expect to be back?" I asked once we were outside.

"I expect to need paper."

That was answer enough. We crossed the square slowly, and I found myself carrying the parcel without being asked. Vale did not thank me. He only adjusted his pace to mine.
