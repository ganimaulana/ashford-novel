# Chapter 50 — The Afternoon

I had finished the work Calloway had given me. The report was filed, the facts separated from the conclusions, and the questions that could not be answered were left unanswered. I was learning that an unfinished question did not always require another day of investigation.

I woke the next morning without a question waiting for me. The fire was cold. A cart passed in the lane, followed by the bark of a dog and the opening of a door. I listened for a while, then rose. There was work to do, but none of it required an answer.

I went to the pump at noon. The women spoke of flour and coal. The baker knew my name. The verger nodded when I passed. Margaret set the bread aside without my asking. I had been in Alderwick long enough for these things to become ordinary, and I realized that I had stopped noticing when it happened.

In the afternoon I walked toward Vale's cottage. I had not planned to go. I simply found myself taking the road that led there, and by the time I noticed, I was already at the gate.

He was in the yard with a length of oak across two sawhorses. The plane moved in long strokes, and thin shavings curled to the ground. He did not look up when I entered.

"The Greyhound case is closed," I said.

He set down the plane and straightened. "I know."

"How?"

"Margaret told me. She said you had been at the inn for three days, and that you had not come out looking confused." He picked up the plane again, ran his thumb along the blade. "That is how I know."

I stood in the yard and watched him work. The board was about six feet long, a little over a foot wide, and he was planing the edge smooth. He had marked a line with a pencil and a square, and he was working down to it, taking off thin shavings with each pass. The shavings were almost translucent, and they curled and fell, and the wood beneath them was pale and smooth. The scent of oak rose in the cold air.

"Can I help?" I asked.

He looked at me. "Do you know how to use a plane?"

"I know how to watch."

He handed me the plane. "Then watch."

I took it. The weight was heavier than I expected. The body was cast iron, worn smooth by years of use, and the blade was sharp and gleaming. He showed me how to set the blade, how to test the depth on a scrap, how to hold the body steady and let the weight of the tool do the work. His hands moved with the certainty of long practice. He adjusted the blade with a few turns of the knob, then handed it back. "Try it."

I planed the edge. My first pass was too deep. He adjusted the blade, handed it back, and said nothing. The second was better. After that he let me find the rhythm myself.

"You are learning," he said.

"I am learning."

"Good. It is not a virtue. It is a habit." He handed the plane back. "Keep at it. The edge is still a little proud in the middle."

I continued. I worked the plane over the board, adjusting my stance, finding the angle that gave the cleanest cut. The shavings fell faster now, and the edge grew smoother. I could see the line he had drawn, and I was slowly approaching it. I worked for perhaps an hour. We did not speak. The plane moved over the wood, and the shavings fell, and the frost began to melt in the sun. It was the most ordinary afternoon I had spent in weeks, and it was the first time I had felt still since I arrived.

When I had finished the edge, he took the board and held it up to the light. He ran his thumb along the edge and nodded. "It will do," he said. He set the board down and looked at me. "You have a steady hand. That is not something I can teach."

I did not know what to say, so I said nothing. He carried the board into the cottage, and I followed. He paused at the threshold. The board was long, and the door was narrow. "Take the other end," he said. I did. We carried it together, stepping carefully through the doorway, and set it on the table inside. The fire was low, and he set the kettle on the hob. He did not ask me to stay. He did not ask me to leave. He simply went about his work, and I stood in the warmth of the room.

I sat in the chair by the fire, and he sat in his. The kettle began to steam. He poured two cups and set one on the table beside me. The tea was dark and hot, and I wrapped my hands around the cup and felt the warmth seep into my fingers. The room was quiet, and the fire crackled, and the light through the window was pale and thin.

"You have not asked about the case," I said.

"Did you want me to?"

"I do not know. I thought you might want to hear the conclusion."

He took a sip of his tea. "I heard the conclusion when you walked in. You walked differently. You did not walk like a man who was still carrying something."

I considered that. "I was not carrying anything. I left it at the Greyhound."

"And that is why you are here."

I did not answer. He did not press. We sat in silence, and the fire settled, and the afternoon passed slowly. I looked at the board on the table, the smooth edge I had planed, the line he had drawn. It was a small thing, a piece of wood, but it had occupied my hands and my mind for an hour, and I had not thought about the Greyhound at all.

He spoke again, after a long pause. "You have been here before, but you have not worked before. There is a difference between visiting and doing."

"I have been visiting."

"And now you have done something. That is how a place becomes yours."

I did not answer. I drank the tea. The fire crackled, and the wind outside picked up, rattling the window. He did not say anything more, and I did not need him to. The silence was comfortable, the kind that did not need to be filled.

I stayed longer than I intended. When I left, the evening had settled over the town. I walked home slowly, aware that I had come to Vale without a question, and that I had stayed without needing one. Somewhere along the way, visiting him had stopped being part of my investigation.

That evening, I wrote in my notebook:

*The work is finished. I spent the afternoon with Vale, planing oak and drinking tea. He did not ask why I came. I did not know why I came. I only know that I will go again. The work was ordinary, and the silence was enough.*

I shut the book. The fire was low, and the wind was up, and I sat in the dark and listened. The case was closed, and the town was quiet, and I had no question for the next day. That was enough.

When I arrived, he looked at the board.

"Still straight?"

"I think so."

"That is not how you check."

He took the square from the table and held it against the edge.

The line was true.

He handed the square to me.

"Your turn."

I checked it.

"True."

"Now you know."

It was a small thing.

But I understood why he had made me do it.

He had never been interested in giving me answers that could be borrowed.

He wanted me to know how to check.

We finished the tea.

Before I left, he said, "Come tomorrow."

"I will."

"Bring the square."

"Why?"

"So you can check whether the board has moved."

I looked at him.

He smiled.

This time I knew it was a joke.


When I left, I carried the memory of the afternoon more than the chair or the board.

There was no revelation in it.

No hidden message.

Only the quiet knowledge that I had begun to belong somewhere without deciding to.

That frightened me less than it should have.

When I reached my own door, I realized I had spent the entire walk thinking about nothing that needed solving.

That was new.

I had not become less curious.

I had simply learned that curiosity did not have to be fed every hour.

The next morning would bring its own work.

For tonight, the fire was enough.

I put the square beside my notebook.

Tomorrow I would take it back.

For tonight, I left it where it was.
