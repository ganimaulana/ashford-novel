# Chapter 39 — The Letter That Arrives Tomorrow

I went back to Mrs. Hart's house on the morning of the nineteenth, because a letter that arrives tomorrow is a letter that cannot wait for tomorrow to be read. I had been thinking about the letters since I had first seen them, and I had been thinking about what they meant—not the words they contained, but the shape of them, the way they came from the future and described a world that had not yet arrived.

Mrs. Hart opened the door and let me in. She looked tired, the way a woman looks when she has been waiting for something that has not come.

"Another letter arrived this morning," she said. "It is on the table."

I went to the table and picked it up. It was the same hand, the same paper, the same date: tomorrow. I opened it and read:

*The constable will ask you about the letters. Tell him you have destroyed them. Do not keep them. Do not show them to anyone else.*

I looked at Mrs. Hart, and she looked at me.

"Did you show anyone else?" I asked.

"Only you," she said. "And the constable. I showed the constable last week, before you came. He did not say much. He only looked at them and said he would send someone."

I read the letter again. It was not a prediction. It was a warning. It was telling Mrs. Hart to hide the letters, to deny their existence, to make them disappear. And it had been written before I had arrived, before I had seen the letters, before the constable had sent me.

I sat down at the table and looked at the letter. I had been thinking of the letters as predictions, as descriptions of what would happen if nothing was done. But this letter was different. It was not a description of a future that would occur. It was an instruction to prevent a future from occurring.

I looked at the date on the envelope. Tomorrow. The letter had been written after I had seen the letters, but it had been sent before I had seen them. It had come from a future that already knew what I would do.

I folded the letter and put it in my pocket.

"Do not destroy the letters," I said. "Keep them, and do not show them to anyone else. And if another letter arrives, tell me before you read it."

Mrs. Hart nodded, and I went home to write it down.

---

*19 Jan. The letters are not predictions. They are responses. They are written after something happens, and sent before it happens. They are a conversation between the present and the future, and I am part of the conversation. I do not know who writes them, or how they are sent, or why they choose Mrs. Hart. But I know that they are not warnings. They are part of something larger—a pattern that I am beginning to see. The room at the Greyhound changed when it was listened to. The letters arrive when they are expected. The future is not fixed. It is a conversation, and I am learning to speak.*

I shut the book and looked out at the lane. The house at the end of it stood with its rosemary on the sill and its glass open to the cold, and the lamp was not yet lit, because the hour was not yet come. I had not solved the letters, but I had learned something: the letters were not prophecies. They were part of a dialogue. And dialogue, unlike prophecy, could be answered.

The question was: who was on the other side?
