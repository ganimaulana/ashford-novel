CHAPTER 73 — THE FIRST TEST

I went to the office on Friday morning, because Wednesday had ended with Calloway's words and the constable had said he would send for me.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open. I had been there six times before. But I had never been there as a man who was expected to take a test, and I had never been there as a man who was not answerable to the office at all.

I stepped inside.

The room was the same. The desk was the same. The shelves were the same. The book with the nine lines was on the lower shelf. The middle shelf was bare. The clerk was there, copying into the ledger. Calloway was there, standing by the fireplace. Orme was there, sitting in the chair opposite the desk.

Calloway looked up. He set the paper down. He took off his hat and set it on the desk.

"You have accepted," he said.

"I have," I said.

"The examination has a first test," he said. "It is not a trial. It is not an interview. It is a room. You will go into the room. You will look at what is in it. You will come out and tell the office what you saw. The clerk will write what you say. You will sign it. The record will be the office's record. The seeing will be yours."

"What is in the room," I said.

"That is for you to find out," Calloway said. "The clerk will show you."

He nodded at the clerk. The clerk stood up. He put on his hat. He walked to the door. He opened it. He looked out at the lane. He turned back.

"Come," he said.

I followed him. We went through the door. We went down the passage. We went up the stairs. The clerk did not speak. He opened a door at the top of the stairs. He stood aside.

"The room is yours," he said. "You may look. You may touch. You may write. You may not remove anything. You may not open anything that is sealed. You may stay as long as you need. The constable will wait."

He closed the door.

The room was small. The walls were plain. The floor was wooden. The window was high. The light came through it in a rectangle on the floor. There was a table in the centre. There was a chair beside it. There were shelves on one wall. There was a door at the far end, closed.

On the table were three objects.

The first was a stone. It was rough, the colour of wet gravel. It had a circle cut into one face, and inside the circle were three short lines, and at the centre of the lines a dot. The cut was shallow.

The second was a key. It was iron, the bow shaped like a leaf. The bit was worn. The shaft was straight. It was not a modern key. It was not an old key. It was a key that had been made for a lock that no longer existed.

The third was a letter. The paper was thin. The ink was brown. The address was written in a hand I did not recognise. The seal was unbroken. The envelope was not torn.

I looked at each thing in turn. The stone was the stone. The key was the key. The letter was the letter. The table was the table. The chair was the chair. The shelves were the shelves. The door was closed.

I touched the stone. It was cold. The cut was clean. The lines were shallow. The dot was at the centre.

I touched the key. It was iron. The bow was shaped like a leaf. The bit was worn. The shaft was straight.

I touched the letter. The paper was thin. The ink was brown. The seal was unbroken. The envelope was not torn.

I turned the letter over. There was no writing on the back.

I looked at the shelves. There were books on them. The books were ordinary. The bindings were worn. The titles were not visible.

I looked at the door at the far end. It was closed. It was wooden. It had a handle. It did not have a lock.

I looked at the window. It was high. The light came through it in a rectangle on the floor. The rectangle was still.

I sat in the chair. I took out my notebook. I opened it to a fresh page. I wrote what I had seen, in order. What I had been told, in order. What I had concluded, in order. I kept them apart, the way Vale had taught me.

What I saw: a small room. A table with three objects: a rough stone with a circle, three lines, and a dot cut into one face; an iron key with a leaf-shaped bow and worn bit; a sealed letter with brown ink and an unrecognised hand. A chair. Shelves with ordinary books. A closed door at the far end. A high window with a rectangle of light on the floor.

What I was told: the room was mine to look at. I might touch. I might write. I might not remove anything. I might not open anything sealed. I might stay as long as I needed. The constable would wait.

What I concluded: the objects had been placed. The stone was stone. The key was iron. The letter was paper. The room was a room. I wondered why they had been placed there.

I closed the notebook.

I stood up. I opened the door. The clerk was there. He had been standing in the passage. He did not look surprised.

"You have finished," he said.

"I have," I said.

"You will come with me," he said.

I followed him back down the stairs. We went through the passage. We went into the room. Calloway was there. Orme was there. The clerk sat in his chair.

Calloway looked up. He set the paper down.

"What did you see," he said.

I told him.

He looked at the clerk. The clerk wrote quickly, the same small precise letters. He wrote: Room contained one rough stone with circle, three lines, dot; one iron key, leaf-shaped bow, worn bit; one sealed letter, brown ink, unrecognised hand. Table, chair, shelves with books, closed door, high window. No other objects. No marks on walls. No marks on floor.

He set the pencil down. He turned the page. He looked at me.

"Sign," he said.

I signed my name at the bottom of the line.

Calloway looked at me. He did not say anything. He looked at the paper in the clerk's hand. The clerk held it out. Calloway took it. He read it. He set it on the desk.

"The test is not finished," he said. "You have done what was asked. The record is made. The office will consider it."

He looked at Orme. Orme looked at the floor. He did not say anything.

"There will be another," Calloway said. "The constable will send for you."

He stood up. He put on his hat. He walked to the door. He opened it. He looked out at the lane. He turned back.

He went out.

The clerk looked at me. He did not say anything. He turned back to the ledger. Orme stood up. He put on his hat. He looked at me.

"The office has a duty," he said. "The duty is not finished."

He went out.

I went out.

The lane was quiet. The house was shut. I set the key in the lock and turned it. The door opened. The room was the same.

I did not know what the test had been for. I did not know whether the stone and the key and the letter were what they appeared to be, or whether they were there for some other reason. I did not know whether Calloway was testing my observation or testing something else. I did not know whether Orme was satisfied or whether he had not yet decided.

But I knew the examination had begun.
