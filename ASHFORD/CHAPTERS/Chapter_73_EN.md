# Chapter 73 — The Missing Object

The file was thick when Orme handed it to me. Not thick with papers, but thick with the weight of what was inside — a single object, reported missing, and a dozen statements about where it had been and who had last seen it. I had expected a short case. The thickness surprised me.

"The constable who reported this thought it was stolen," Orme said, leaning against the table. "The constable who investigated thought it was misplaced. The constable who filed it thought it was misfiled. All three wrote reports. All three are wrong."

"All three?"

"All three agree that the object was last seen in the evidence room on a Tuesday morning. They disagree on what happened to it." He opened the file. "Tell me what you see."

The object was a seal — not a wax impression this time, but a physical seal: a disc of lead, impressed with the constable's mark, used to secure evidence boxes. It was the kind of thing that was counted once and then forgotten, until someone needed to open the box and found the seal had been broken or removed. The constable who reported it missing — Constable Bramwell, south district — said he had placed it on the evidence shelf at 0900 on Tuesday and found it gone at 1130 when he returned to retrieve it.

The three reports:

**Report A — Constable Daines, who investigated:** "Searched the evidence room. No sign of the seal. No signs of forced entry. Shelf 3, position 7, empty. Concluded seal was misplaced during morning inventory."

**Report B — Constable Marsh, who filed:** "Cross-referenced with the Tuesday inventory log. Seal was logged out at 0900 by Constable Bramwell, logged in at 1130 by Constable Bramwell. No other entries. Concluded seal was misfiled in the inventory."

**Report C — Constable Bramwell, who reported:** "I placed the seal on Shelf 3, position 7, at 0900. At 1130 the position was empty. No one else was in the evidence room. The door was locked both times. I believe the seal was stolen."

I read all three. I read them twice. The third time, I noticed something the second reading had not shown me: Daines's report said "no signs of forced entry," but Bramwell's report said "the door was locked both times" — and Marsh's report said nothing about the door at all.

"The seal was on Shelf 3, position 7," I said. "Bramwell logged it in at 1130. Daines found the position empty at 1130. Marsh's log says Bramwell logged it in. But Daines and Marsh were not in the room at the same time, and Bramwell logged it in without being seen."

Orme nodded. "Go on."

"Someone can log an object into a shelf position without the object being there. They can write the entry, and the log will show the object, and the shelf will show nothing. That is not misplacement. That is misrecording."

"And?"

"And the door was locked both times" — I tapped Bramwell's report — "but Daines found 'no signs of forced entry.' If the door was locked and nobody forced it, then either someone had a key, or the lock was not engaged properly."

"Daines did not test the lock." Orme was quiet. "He looked for the seal. He did not look for the lock."

I wrote that: *Daines looked for the seal. He did not look for the lock.* "Marsh's log says the seal was logged in at 1130. But who witnessed the log-in? Nobody. Bramwell alone logged the seal in, alone locked the door, alone left the room."

"Bramwell's report says no one else was in the evidence room. Daines's report says he searched it. If Daines was in the room after Bramwell, then someone was in the room."

"Except Daines and Bramwell were there at different times. Daines at 1145, Bramwell at 1130."

"So who logged the seal in at 1130, if Bramwell was placing it at 0900 and Daines was searching at 1145?"

I thought about that. The log was a book, kept on the desk in the evidence room. Anyone who entered the room could write in it. The entries were not counter-signed. The seal was logged in at 1130, but Bramwell said he had not returned to the room between 1130 and 1145.

"Someone else was in the evidence room at 1130," I said. "Someone with a key, or someone who knew the lock was faulty. Someone who knew the log was not witness-checked."

"And the seal?"

"May still be on Shelf 3, position 7. May have been taken. May have been moved to another shelf. May have been logged in by someone who knew it was not there."

"And the correct conclusion?"

"The seal's location is not established. The lock is faulty. The log is not witness-checked. Someone with access can move items without detection. The question is: who else had the key?"

I looked at the next page of the file. There was a list of who had keys to the evidence room: Bramwell, Daines, the sergeant, the constable in charge, and one entry I did not recognise — *P. Ingle, clerk — duplicate key for cross-reference.*

"Ingle keeps a duplicate key for cross-reference." I wrote that. *Ingle. Duplicate key. Cross-reference.* "That is not normal."

"No," Orme said. "But it is documented."

"The cross-reference would explain the log entry at 1130. Ingle entered the seal at 1130, logged it on Shelf 3, position 7, but did not place it there. He recorded an object that was not present."

"And why?"

"Because the book said to. Because someone told him to. Because the real seal was already somewhere else, and the log was meant to show it where it was not."

"And the real seal?"

"I do not know where it is." I closed the file. "I know someone moved it. I know Ingle had the means. I know the log was altered to cover the movement. I do not know who told Ingle to do it, or why, or where the seal went."

Orme was quiet for a long time. "You stopped at four people. Who is the fifth?"

I thought about that. The key list had five names: Bramwell, Daines, the sergeant, the constable in charge, and Ingle. Three of them had made reports — Bramwell, Daines, and Marsh. The sergeant and the constable in charge had not.

"The sergeant and the constable in charge," I said. "They have keys. They do not appear in the reports."

"They do not appear because they did not enter the room."

"Or because they did, and their entry was not recorded."

"That is the fifth question." Orme closed the file. "You see what is established, and what is not. The seal is not in the room. Ingle had the means. Someone with authority could have ordered the cross-reference. The question is whether it was ordered, or whether it was taken."

I wrote: *Seal: not in room. Ingle: had means. Authority: unidentified. Theft vs. order: not established.*

"And the answer? Was it stolen?"

"That is not what I was asked." Orme was watching me. "You were asked what is established. You found the means. You found the gap. You did not find the motive or the hand."

I closed my book. The seal was still missing. The log was still altered. Ingle was still holding a key that should not exist. But I had not named a thief. I had not claimed the seal was stolen. He had not claimed it was ordered. I had found the gap, and I had left it there.

"How do you score this?"

"By the questions you stop asking," he said. "You asked who has the key. You did not ask who used it. You asked whether the log was altered. You did not ask why. The score is provisional. You will be scored again on the fifteenth."

I did not ask what score he had given me. I had learned that some questions were answered later.

The walk home was quiet. The market had begun to empty, and the lane was empty, and the cottage at the end of it was dark. I passed the baker's shop, and the smell of bread came out into the cold air, and I did not stop. I went to my own door and let myself in, and the room was quiet, and the notebook was on the desk.

I opened the notebook and wrote the date, and I wrote: *One seal. Five keys. One altered log. One unidentified hand. I did not name the thief. I asked who had the means. I did not ask why.*

I closed the notebook and sat in the chair by the window. The fire had gone out, and I did not light it. I sat until the light failed, and I thought about the cross-reference — a duplicate key kept for convenience, a log entry that did not require a signature, a system designed for efficiency and built for error. Vale had worked inside that system. He had written about it, probably, and he had kept a second copy. The office kept what it needed. The rest was left for the keeper to find.

In the morning I would write to Ingle and ask about the cross-reference protocol. That was a question about procedure. The answer would tell me whether the system was built for error, or for something else.

---

The next morning I walked to the office before the sun had cleared the rooftops. I had slept poorly — not from worry, but from the weight of a question that would not settle. The cross-reference protocol was not just a procedure. It was a question about who the office trusted, and who it did not.

Orme was in the back room when I arrived. The clerk was there, copying into the ledger. Calloway was there, standing by the fireplace.

"You have a letter," Calloway said, setting the paper down. "From Ingle, at the north records."

It was typed, on official paper. The content was brief: *Duplicate key for cross-reference is standard in districts with high turnover. The key remains with the filing clerk. Access is logged when the clerk is present. Ingle's access was logged at 1125 on Tuesday, twelve minutes before the seal was reported missing.*

I read it twice. "Twelve minutes before."

"Before what was reported."

"Not before what happened."

Orme was quiet. "The log shows the key was used. It does not show what was done with it."

I wrote that: *Key used at 1125. Purpose not established. Access logged, not witnessed.*

"That is the difference between procedure and proof," Orme said. "The office logs access. The office does not guarantee truth."

I closed the letter and held it. The seal was still missing. The key had been used. Someone had entered the room with authority, or with opportunity, or both. But the question — who, and why — was not answered by the log.

"The score is provisional," Orme said. "You asked who had the means. You found the key. You did not find the hand that took the seal, or the reason it was taken. That is still not established."

I wrote: *Means: established. Hand: not found. Reason: not asked.*

"That is all I am asking for," I said.

"That is all the office requires," he agreed.

---

I went home and wrote in my book: *One question leads to another. The key was used. The seal was not found. Someone with the key entered the evidence room at 1125. They did not take the seal from the shelf in front of them. They either moved it before Bramwell placed it, or moved it after he left, or never moved it at all and the seal was never where they logged it.*

The question was not simple. It was not even a question. It was a door that opened into another room, and that room opened into another, and the further I walked, the more I understood that Vale had known exactly what he was writing about when he wrote about access logs and witness requirements and the difference between procedure and proof. He had been describing this room.
