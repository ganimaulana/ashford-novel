CHAPTER 63 — THE MISSING NAME

I returned to the papers on Wednesday morning, because the morning was for returning to things, and the returning was the part of the work that asked nothing of me.

The eight tied papers were on the table. The leaf with nine lines was beside them. The clerk's ruled sheet was folded on top. The service file was on the left, where I had left it. My notebook was open.

I had looked at the ruled sheet the day before and seen that it contained eight lines. I had looked at the leaf and seen that it contained nine. I had looked at the service file and seen that it contained four lines and a postscript that pointed to the entry-book. I had looked at the entry-book through the leaf and seen that it contained a ninth line with a blank margin. I had looked at the eight papers and seen that their backs carried marks that agreed with the leaf.

What I had not looked at was the names.

I turned the leaf over. The front carried the nine lines, in the clerk's hand. I read them again, slowly, because reading slowly is the way a man reads a thing he has already read and is looking for what he missed the first time.

The first line: *Theft at the ironmonger's, June 1803.*
The second line: *Death at the poor-house, September 1804.*
The third line: *Boundary dispute at the north field, March 1805.*
The fourth line: *Drunkard found on the common, November 1807.*
The fifth line: *Woman missing three days, recovered, January 1811.*
The sixth line: *Fight at the market, May 1812.*
The seventh line: *Fire in the yard, August 1814.*
The eighth line: *Theft from the mill, February 1816.*
The ninth line: *Inquiry made at the house in the lane concerning office papers, late February. Account of the same transmitted to the office.*

I read them again.

The first eight lines named a place. They named a thing that had happened. They named a date. They did not name a person, except in the general sense — "a woman missing," "a drunkard," "a fight" — but the names of the people involved were not in the lines. The lines were about the parish business, not about the people in it.

The ninth line named a place: *the house in the lane.* It named a topic: *office papers.* It named an action: *inquiry made.* It named a date: *late February.* It did not name a person. Not the person who made the inquiry. Not the person who answered it. Not the person who wrote the account. Not the person who received it. The line was about an event that had happened at a place, and the event involved papers, and the account had been transmitted, and that was all the line said.

I looked at the ruled sheet. Eight lines. The clerk had written the same subjects, in the same order, with the same dates, beside each a mark. He had written them in his own hand, small and precise, and he had stopped at the eighth line, and the ninth line was not there.

I looked at the service file. Four lines. Name. Appointment. Reassignment with the reason left blank. Death. And at the foot: *See entry-book, February last.*

The file pointed to the entry-book. The entry-book pointed to the ninth line. The ninth line pointed to a paper that was not there. The paper that was not there was the account of the inquiry. The inquiry had been made at the house in the lane. The account had been transmitted to the office. The office had not received it. The clerk had not marked it. The shelf was bare. The book was not.

I sat with that.

I thought about the February visitor. I had not seen him. I had been told about him by Calloway, on Saturday, in this room. He had come to the door. He had asked questions in the right order. Vale had given him nothing. Calloway had said the man was not sent by the office. I had said I knew. Calloway had said he knew from the man, and I had known from the book, and we had both known since Monday.

The book knew the man had been there. The book did not know his name.

I looked at the ruled sheet again. Eight lines. The clerk's hand. The same hand that had written the file. The same hand that had written the marks. The same hand that had made the leaf.

I thought about the marks. Eight of them. One for each line. One for each paper. Each mark meant the paper had come and been stood on the shelf. The ninth line had no mark. The ninth paper had not come.

I thought about the nine lines as a set. Eight of them had names attached — not personal names, but subjects, places, actions. The ninth had a place and a topic but no person. No inquirer. No inquired. No account-keeper. No receiver. Just the event, floating, attached to nothing but the line itself and the blank margin beside it.

I did not write this down. I did not reach a conclusion. I simply looked at it, the way a man looks at a thing he has noticed but does not yet know how to name.

The office kept a book. The book kept a mark. The mark kept a promise. The promise had not been kept. The book said the eight papers were in the office's hands. The shelf said they were not. The clerk said the ninth paper had never come. The constable said the February inquiry was separate. The file said Vale was constable and nothing else. The file said he was reassigned and did not say why. The file said he died in office and did not say what the office had lost. The ruled sheet said eight lines. The leaf said nine. The ninth line had no mark. The ninth line had no name. The man who had made the marks knew the marks were not the whole of the record. The man who had left the blank margin knew the margin was the only certain thing. The knowing of it was the not-knowing of it. The not-knowing was the paper that was not there.

I closed the notebook.

I carried the box to the table and opened it. I looked at the three bundles on the lid. I looked at the uncertain paper that had no name, lying on top of everything. I thought about the file. I thought about the leaf. I thought about the ninth line with its blank margin and its missing name. I thought about the ruled sheet with its eight lines. I thought about the eight papers with their dates and their marks and their returns. I thought about the shelf that was bare. I thought about the book that was not. I thought about the clerk's hand, which had written forty years of marks and had left the ninth line nameless. I thought about the February man, who had asked questions in the right order and had received no account and had left no name. I thought about the road between the desk and the shelf, and the paper that had been sent and had not come, and the account that Vale had written and had not marked, and the account that was not there.

The map was in the box. The file was in Calloway's coat. The journal was in the box. The stone was in the wall. The mark was on the stone. The track was north. The road was south. The lane was quiet. The week was not over. For now.
