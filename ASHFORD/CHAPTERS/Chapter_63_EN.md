# Chapter 63 — The Pot

The Thursday came cold and still, with a sky the colour of old iron and a frost that had not lifted since dawn. The lane was silent except for the crunch of frozen grass under my boots, and the hedges were white with rime. I went up the hill without a reason beyond the walk itself—the day was ordinary, and I had no errand, no question, no piece of business. The cottage door was ajar, a sliver of darkness against the grey wall, and the smoke from the chimney rose thin and straight into the air, barely moving in the stillness. I knew before I reached it that he was inside, that the fire was lit, that the day would unfold as Thursdays did. I did not hurry. The lane was not long.

He was at the table when I came in, a pile of potatoes and a knife before him. The fire was already burning, and the room was warm, carrying the smell of wood-smoke and the faint earthiness of the potatoes. He looked up and said, "Good. You can peel these." I did not ask what we were making. I took off my coat and hung it on the hook by the door, the hook that had held it for months now, and I sat down across from him and took a potato and the knife. The potatoes were small and knobby, the kind that grew in the garden behind the cottage—I had seen the patch in summer, a tangle of green leaves and white flowers. They were dirty, still crusted with soil, and I scraped the peel with the blade. The knife was the one I had sharpened on the rainy Thursday, the one with the worn wooden handle that fit my palm. I recognised it by the curve of the handle, by the slight nick near the bolster. He had kept it out, not in the press, as if he had expected to use it. We worked in silence for a time, the only sounds the scrape of the blade against the skin and the crackle of the fire. The peelings fell into a bowl between us, a growing pile of brown and curling strips.

After a few minutes, he said, "You have been doing that for a while. Peeling potatoes." "I have done it before." "Not here." I shook my head and continued. He watched my hands for a moment, then returned to his own work—he was slicing the potatoes I had already peeled, cutting them into chunks for the pot. The knife moved through them with a soft, clean sound.

I had not taken the knife from him before, though I had seen him do it many times. He was watching my hands now, not with instruction but with the quiet attention he gave to any task being done well. I did not slow or speed my pace. I finished one potato and reached for another, and the rhythm of the work became steady, like the ticking of the clock on the mantel.

"There is a pot on the hob," he said. "The water is hot. When the potatoes are done, you can put them in." I did not ask what we were making. He did not tell me. We worked in the same rhythm, he at the table and I at the table, and the fire crackled and the kettle was not on the hob, because the pot was already there. I understood that he had been preparing this before I arrived, that the water had been set to heat, and that he had known I would come. The steam from the pot fogged the window slightly, and the room grew warmer.

When the potatoes were peeled, I put them in the pot, and he added salt and a handful of dried herbs from a jar on the shelf—I caught the scent of thyme and rosemary. He stirred the pot with a long wooden spoon, and the steam rose in a thick cloud, carrying the smell of cooking vegetables and herbs. I stood by the fire and watched him, the warmth on my face. He moved slowly, deliberately, the way he always did, and there was nothing in his movements that suggested difficulty or change. He was simply doing what he had done a hundred times before, adding a little more water, adjusting the heat. The pot simmered, and he set the spoon across the top and turned to the table.

"You can set the table," he said, and I took the plates from the press—two plain white plates, the same ones we always used—and the bread from the basket on the counter. The bread was the same loaf from the baker on the corner, the round one with the dark, flour-dusted crust. I cut two slices and put them on the plates, and I set them at our places on the table. He did not mention the bread. He did not need to. When the stew was ready, he lifted the pot off the hob and set it on a trivet in the centre of the table. He ladled it into the bowls, and we ate.

We ate at the table, the two of us, the stew hot and thick, the potatoes soft, the broth dark with herbs. The bread was good, crusty and warm. We ate in the quiet that had become the shape of our meals together—no need for talk, just the sound of spoons against the bowls and the fire settling. He did not offer advice or explanation. He ate, and I ate, and that was the whole of it. The fire burned low, and the light outside the window was pale and grey, and the room was filled with the warmth of the stove.

After the meal, I took the plates and bowls to the sink. The water was still warm from the kettle, and I washed them with the same cloth he always used, a frayed piece of linen that hung by the sink. I dried them with a clean towel and put them back in the press, stacking the plates, the bowls, the spoons. He sat in his chair by the fire, watching me, but not in a way that demanded attention. I felt his gaze on my back, not weighing or judging, but simply present, the way a man watches a familiar task being done. When I had finished, I dried my hands and sat in my usual chair across the hearth. The fire had settled to a bed of coals, glowing orange and red, and the room was warm and quiet.

"The tea is in the canister," he said. "The kettle will take a moment." I put the kettle on the hob and watched it until it began to steam, then poured the water into the pot. I let it steep for a few minutes, the way he had taught me, and poured two cups. I took the one with the chip on the rim—he had set it at my place without thinking, as he always did—and handed him the other. He held it in both hands, the way he always did, letting the warmth seep into his palms. We sat in the warmth of the fire, the cups between us.

"Thursday again," he said. "Thursday again," I said. The fire crackled, and the light through the window had begun to fade from grey to a deeper, watery grey. We sat without speaking, and the silence was comfortable, the kind that had grown between us over the months. The rhythm of the week was built into the cottage now: the fire, the cups, the bread, the quiet. I had stopped counting the Thursdays, because the counting had become the thing I did without thinking, like the walk up the lane or the way I sat in the chair with the same curve in my back.

When the tea was finished, I took the cups to the sink and washed them too, rinsing them and setting them on the rack. He remained in his chair, his hands resting on the arms, his eyes on the fire, watching the coals shift and settle. I dried the cups and put them back on the shelf, the chipped one in its place, beside the plain one.

"I will see you next Thursday," I said. He nodded, a slow movement, and I went to the door and put on my coat. The light was fading outside, the lane already half in shadow. The smoke from the chimney rose thin and straight into the still air, and the frost was forming again on the cobbles. I closed the door behind me, and I walked down the lane without looking back—not because I was avoiding it, but because there was nothing to see that I had not already seen a hundred times. The lane was the same, the cottage was the same, and Thursday was Thursday.

That evening, I wrote in my notebook:

*Peeled potatoes for the stew. He did not ask me to stay. I did not ask if he needed help. We ate together, and it was ordinary.*

I did not write what I had seen: that he had set the chipped cup at my place without thinking, that he had used the knife I had sharpened on the rainy Thursday, that he had known I would come before I knew it myself. I did not write those things because they were not the story. The story was the stew and the bread and the quiet, and that was all I needed to write.

The fire burned low, and the wind came up, and I sat with the window closed against the cold, thinking about the day. It had been a Thursday like any other Thursday—the same lane, the same fire, the same cups. That was enough.
