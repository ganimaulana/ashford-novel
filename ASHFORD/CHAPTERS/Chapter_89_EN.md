CHAPTER 89 — THE GROUNDS

I did not hear from the office for four days after we delivered the box. I had expected a note, a summons, something that would tell me whether the blank paper was the right thing to bring. But nothing came, and I found myself walking the lane each morning, watching the door, waiting for a note that did not arrive.

On Monday morning a note came. It did not say The office. It said: The market square. Nine o'clock. Bring nothing.

I put on my coat and went.

The square was full. I had not seen so many candidates together since the first day at the office. I counted, moving my eyes slowly across the crowd, and reached thirty-one before I lost count. Some of the faces I knew. Some I did not. The man with the notebook was there, standing apart, his notebook closed but in his hand. The older man with the frayed cuffs was there, leaning against the pump. Peale was there, standing near the fountain, and he looked at me when I arrived and nodded once. Ellison was there, standing near the chandler's, his hands in his pockets. Merritt was there, standing alone by the gate, not looking at anyone.

The man I had seen at the market square was there. He was standing near the wall, his arms crossed, his head tilted. He was not looking at anyone in particular. He was looking at the crowd as a whole. I still did not know his name.

John Weaver was there, standing near the edge of the square. He looked at me and nodded. I nodded back.

Calloway came out of the office. He did not speak at first. He walked to the centre of the square and waited until the crowd was silent.

"The examination will continue elsewhere," he said. "You will be taken to a location outside Alderwick. You will remain there until the examination is complete. Not everyone who arrives will remain until the end."

He paused.

"You will walk. Follow the cart."

He pointed to a cart that had drawn up at the edge of the square. It was loaded with crates and bundles. A man in a grey coat sat on the driver's seat. He did not look at us.

Calloway turned and walked back inside.

We stood in silence for a moment. Then the cart began to move. The man in the grey coat did not call out. He simply urged the horse forward, and the cart rolled out of the square and down the lane.

We followed.

I walked near the front, not because I wanted to lead, but because I wanted to see where we were going. The cart moved slowly, and we moved slowly with it. We passed the church, the market, the old mill, and then the houses became fewer and the fields opened up. The road turned to dirt, and the cart rolled on.

We walked for an hour. Then two. The sun rose higher, and the morning grew warm. Some of the men began to fall back, their steps slower. Others kept pace. I walked steadily, conserving my strength, watching the cart and the man in the grey coat. He did not look back.

We passed a crossroads, a small wood, a stream. Then the road turned again, and I saw the grounds.

They were walled. The wall was high, made of grey stone, and it stretched along the road for as far as I could see. There was a gate, iron and heavy, set into the wall. The cart stopped at the gate, and the man in the grey coat climbed down and opened it. He did not speak. He gestured for us to enter.

We went through the gate. I walked slowly, looking at the wall, the gate, the ground inside. The grounds were larger than I had expected. There was a courtyard, paved with stone, and beyond it a long building with a tiled roof and a row of windows. There were other buildings too—smaller ones, set back from the main building, some with chimneys, some with doors that stood open. At the far end of the grounds, I could see an open field, grass and dirt, with posts and ropes laid out in patterns I did not understand.

The cart stopped in the courtyard. The man in the grey coat climbed down and stood by the horse, waiting.

Calloway was standing near the main building. He had arrived before us. He did not explain how. He simply stood there, watching us enter, his face as unreadable as always.

"You will be housed in the main building," he said. "There are rooms on the upper floors. You will be assigned a bed. You will not leave the grounds without permission. You will be told when and where to assemble. There will be meals in the dining hall. There will be tasks. There will be examinations."

He paused.

"Some of you will not complete the examination."

He turned and walked into the building.

We stood in the courtyard, looking at each other. Then the man in the grey coat began to hand out slips of paper. Each slip had a number on it. I received number fourteen.

"You will find your room on the second floor," he said. "There is a bed, a table, a lamp. You will leave your belongings there and return to the courtyard in one hour. That is all."

I walked into the building. The interior was plain and functional. The floors were wooden, the walls were whitewashed, and the stairs were wide and worn in the centre. I climbed to the second floor and found my room. It was small, with a narrow bed, a table, a chair, and a window that looked out over the courtyard. I set my coat on the bed and stood at the window.

I could see the other candidates moving across the courtyard. Some were already heading back down. Some were standing in groups, talking in low voices. I saw Ellison standing near the well, his hands in his pockets, looking at the main building. I saw Peale walking toward the field, his head down, his steps measured. I saw the man from the market square standing near the gate, his arms crossed, his head tilted.

I left my room and returned to the courtyard. The man in the grey coat was standing near the cart. He did not speak. He waited until most of us had assembled, and then he said:

"You will each take one of these."

He pointed to a pile of canvas sacks near the cart. They were heavy, filled with what looked like sand or grain.

"You will carry them to the storehouse at the far end of the field. You will place them inside. You will return for another. You will continue until all the sacks have been moved."

He paused.

"There is no time limit."

I looked at the sacks. There were perhaps forty of them. I walked over and lifted one. It was heavy—heavier than I had expected. I could carry it, but I would not be able to carry it quickly. I looked at the field. The storehouse was at the far end, perhaps two hundred yards away. The ground was uneven, grass and dirt and patches of mud.

I picked up the sack and began to walk. I moved slowly, balancing the weight across my shoulders. I saw other candidates moving past me. Some carried the sacks easily, their steps quick, their faces calm. Others struggled, as I did, their steps slow and careful.

I reached the storehouse, placed the sack inside, and turned back. I walked back to the cart. I took another sack. I carried it to the storehouse. I returned. I took another.

The sun moved across the sky. I lost count of how many sacks I carried. My shoulders ached. My legs burned. I saw candidates who had finished—they stood near the cart, watching the rest of us. I saw candidates who were still working, like me. I saw some who had stopped, sitting on the ground, their faces pale.

I kept moving.

The man from the market square passed me once, carrying two sacks, one under each arm. He did not look at me. He moved steadily, without effort.

Weaver passed me once, carrying a single sack, his steps slow but even. He nodded as he passed.

I did not stop. I did not allow myself to stop. I thought about the examination, about the tasks I had completed, about the light on the stairs and the blank paper. I thought about what Calloway had said—that some of us would continue, and some would not. I did not know whether carrying sacks would decide anything. But I knew that stopping would answer a question I did not want to answer.

I carried the last sack to the storehouse. The storehouse was full. I set the sack on top of the pile and walked back to the courtyard.

The man in the grey coat was standing near the cart. He looked at me, then at the others who had finished. He did not say anything. He did not write anything down. He simply stood there, watching.

No one was told they had succeeded. No one was told they had failed. The task was simply over.

We stood in the courtyard, waiting for something—an announcement, a sign, a dismissal. None came. The man in the grey coat turned and walked away.

I walked to the dining hall. It was a large room with long tables and benches. There was bread and cheese and water. I sat down and ate slowly. I did not look at anyone. I did not speak.

I found my room and lay down on the bed. The window was dark. I could hear the wind outside. I could hear voices in the corridor, low and muffled. I did not know what the next day would bring.

I thought about the grounds—the wall, the gate, the field, the buildings. It was not a place I had ever imagined the Constable owning. It was not the office. It was not a room or a vestry. It was a place designed for something larger, something that required space and time and many people.

I sat up and looked out the window. The courtyard was empty. The main building was dark. But at the far end of the field, near the storehouse, I could see a light—a single lamp, glowing in a window I had not noticed before. Someone was there. Someone was watching.

I did not know whether it was a clerk, a guard, or Calloway himself. I only knew that the observation had not stopped. It had simply moved to a place where I could not see it as easily.

I lay back down and closed my eyes.

I waited for dawn.
