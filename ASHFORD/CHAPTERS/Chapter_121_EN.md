CHAPTER 121 — THE WATCHER

I searched for the keeper of the tally through the spring.

I walked the boundary. I watched the stones. I watched the chamber. I watched the reading room, and the tower, and the house at the end of the lane.

I was looking for someone who added marks to the count.

I did not find him.

The stones did not change. The marks did not grow. The count did not change.

I began to think that I had been wrong — that the marks had appeared, and that nothing more would be added, and that the tally was complete.

Then, on a night in April, I saw him.

---

I was at the boundary, at the eastern marker, on the night of the full moon.

I had gone to watch the stones, because the stones were old, and the full moon showed them clearly, and I wanted to see whether the marks had changed.

The eastern marker stood in its place, the first sign at its base, worn smooth by years.

I knelt beside it. I looked at the stone.

And I heard it.

A sound. A small sound. The sound of a mark being made.

I looked up.

A figure stood at the far end of the line of stones, at the corner where the wall turned.

It was dark. I could not see the figure clearly. It stood still, at the corner, in the shadow of the wall.

I stood.

I did not call out.

I walked toward the figure, along the line of stones, and the figure did not move.

I came closer.

The figure was a man. He wore a dark coat. He stood at the corner of the wall, with his hand on the stone.

He was not young. He was not old. He had the look of a man who had been standing there for a long time.

I stopped a few paces from him.

"Who are you?" I said.

He did not answer.

I came closer.

He turned.

I saw his face in the moonlight — a face I could not yet tell, lined with years, with eyes that had seen a great deal and had not spoken of most of it.

"You are the keeper," he said.

"I am."

"I have been waiting for you."

I stood in the moonlight, at the corner of the wall.

"You have been keeping the tally," I said.

"I have been keeping the tally," he said. "I have been keeping it for a long time."

"How long?"

He did not answer.

"The count," I said. "What is it counting?"

He was silent a moment.

"The count," he said, "is counting the keepers."

I stood very still.

"The count is counting the keepers," I said.

"The count is counting the keepers," he said. "One mark for each keeper, since the first keeper set the boundary. The tally is the record of the keepers." He looked at me, and in his eyes I saw the weight of every mark he had ever made. "You are the current mark, but you are not the last."

I looked at him.

"There have been many keepers," I said.

"There have been many keepers," he said. "More than you know. More than the records show. The tally is the record of them all."

"And me?" I said.

"You are a keeper," he said. "Your mark is in the tally."

I stood in the moonlight, and I understood.

The tally was not a count of years. It was a count of keepers — one mark for each keeper, since the first keeper set the boundary.

And my mark was in the tally.

"Then the tally is complete," I said. "When the last keeper has kept, the tally is complete."

He looked at me.

"The tally is not complete," he said. "The keeping is not finished. There are marks to be added."

I stood very still.

"There are keepers to come," he said. "Keepers after you."

"How many?"

He did not answer.

"Who are you?" I said.

He was silent a moment.

"I am the one who keeps the tally," he said. "I have kept it since the first keeper. I will keep it until the last."

He turned.

He walked along the line of stones, into the dark, and I did not follow.

I stood at the corner of the wall, in the moonlight, and I watched him go.

The keeper of the tally.

The one who had kept the count since the first keeper.

And I understood that the keeping was not mine alone. It was older than me, and larger than me, and it would continue after me.

I walked home through the fields.

I sat at my desk. I opened the red book. I wrote:

*I saw the keeper of the tally tonight. He has kept the count since the first keeper. He will keep it until the last.*

*The count is counting the keepers. One mark for each keeper, since the first keeper set the boundary.*

*My mark is in the tally.*

*The keeping is not finished. There are keepers to come.*

*I am not the last keeper.*

*But I am a keeper.*

*And my mark is in the count.*

The count of thirteen signs moved forward, and the hour of the bell drew nearer.
