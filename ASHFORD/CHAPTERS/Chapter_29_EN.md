# Chapter 29 — The Shape of the Asking

The Saturday came in with a frost that held the lane by the throat until noon, and the watch was set for ten, and I went about the morning as a man goes about the morning before a round: with the knowledge that the night would bring the lantern and the gate, and the not-knowing of what the gate would show.

I had not slept well. The bell remained in the locked drawer. I had decided not to carry it until I understood more about it. I had carried the bell for days, and I had put it down, and the putting down of it had not made the weight lighter. It had only changed the place where the weight was carried.

I went to the pump for water, because the water was wanted, and the pump was the place where the town spoke before the market opened. The women were at the pump with their shawls and their buckets, and they spoke of the season and the price and the bench, and I listened, and the listening was the asking.

"The man from the inn," said one of them, "the one that has been at Margaret's every day this week. He was at the church this morning, before the light, and the verger saw him at the tower door."

"The tower door," said another. "What does he want with the tower?"

"He did not go in," said the first. "He stood there, and he looked at it, and he went away. The verger said he did not touch the lock, did not put his hand to it, just stood and looked."

"Looking is not asking," said a third. "Looking is the thing a man does when he is learning the shape of a place before he asks."

I took my water and went home, and the words stood in my mind the way the nod had stood, and the gate had stood, and the bell had stood. I put them together with what I had, and the sum came out as sums come out in this town: that the man had been at the post office, and at Helen's door, and at Arthur's counter, and now at the tower, and that he had looked at each of them, and that the looking was the thing he did, and the asking was the thing he did not.

I could not tell whether he was learning the town or checking something he already knew.

---

The round met at ten at the constable's door, and there were six of us, as there had been six before: the constable, who read; Michael, who stood forward; the verger, with his lantern low; the two farmers from the north end; and I, who carried the book. Hobbs was not there. The place where Hobbs would have stood stood empty, and the round did not look at it, and that is how a town keeps the absence of a man that is missed.

"By the orders," said the constable, "the lane from the Ashcroft door to the mill-yard gate, and the church in the going past, because the church is at the corner. The night is quiet. I would have it stay quiet."

"The night is always quiet," said Michael. "It is the quiet I am come to watch."

We went down the lane, six lanterns making six circles of the frost, and I watched the end of the lane as we went, and the upper window stood black, and the hour was past the hour, and the lamp had been carried away already, and the house was keeping itself in the dark the way it had been keeping itself in the light.

At the corner we came to the church, and the churchyard stood with its stones in the frost, and the tower stood at the west end of it, and the round stopped at the tower door, because a round stops where there is a door.

The verger put up his lantern, and stood with it at the door, and did not put the key in.

"It is locked," he said.

"You know it is locked," said the constable.

"I know it is locked," said the verger. "I am looking at the lock. The lock has not been turned this week, because there is no mark in the frost at the foot of the door, and the frost has stood since Thursday, and there is no mark. The man who stood here this morning did not touch the lock. He stood where I am standing, and he looked, and he went away. I do not say this is a complaint. I say it, because it is the round, and the round writes."

I wrote it, standing at his shoulder with the book against the lantern: *At the tower door, the frost undisturbed; the man from the inn stood here in the morning, looked, and went away.*

"There is no complaint," said the constable. "The man may look at a tower. The tower is not the round's. We go on."

We went on, and the round went past the church and down by the wall to the mill-yard gate, and the gate stood as it had stood before, shut, with the latch down, and the frost was unbroken at the hinge, and the hand that had oiled it had not oiled it again. I wrote the gate down, and the round stood at the gate with six lanterns making six circles of the dark, and the dark beyond the gate was the dark of the corner, and the corner was where the sound had gone, and I was the only man of the round that knew it, and that is the thing a man learns of a round: that the round sees the place, and the man sees what the place has done.

"Is there anything at the gate?" said the constable.

"The gate is shut," said the round, one after another.

"Then it is noted," said the constable. "Noted; the gate shut; no complaint."

We came back by the lane, and the upper window stood black as we came, and the rosemary stood in its pot in the dark, and the round came to the constable's door at a quarter before twelve, and the round was done, and the round had seen nothing, and I had learned by then that the seeing nothing is the round's way of seeing, and that a town that keeps a round keeps it the way it keeps the rest of its keeping.

---

The round broke up at the door, and Michael stayed, because Michael is a man that stays, and the two farmers went north with their lanterns, and the verger stood with his lantern low, and the constable looked at the three of us the way a man looks at a round that has not gone as he would have it go, though it had gone as the orders say.

"There is a man come down from the bench," said Michael, "and I would know what the bench wants with a town that keeps itself."

"The bench wants the rates," said the constable.

"The bench wants the names," said Michael. "It is the same thing in the writing. A rate is a name with a price to it, and a town is a list of the names, and the bench has come down to read the list, and I would know which of us is in the reading."

I stood with the book in my hand, and I thought of what Arthur had said, that the writing wants a name, and of what Margaret had said, that the writing wants a name, and of how the two of them had said the same thing at different counters, and had not known they said it; and I understood that the town had been saying it all month, in its several ways, and that the saying is the sound a town makes when it feels the writing coming.

"I am going home," said the verger, "and I am going by the church, because that is my way, and I would see the tower door again, because I have written it once and I would not write it twice. The round is done. Good night, Mr. Ashcroft. Mind your keeping."

He went down the lane with his lantern low, and Michael looked after him the way Michael looks at a thing he has not yet found the use of, and then he looked at me.

"You write the round," he said.

"I write the round."

"Then you write what the round sees," said Michael, "and I would have you write this: that the round saw nothing tonight, because the round looks at the doors, and the thing that is done in this town is not done at the doors. Write that I said it, because I would have it in the book, and because a thing in the book is a thing that belongs to somebody, and I would have that thing belong to me."

I wrote it, standing at the constable's door with the frost smoking at the lantern: *Michael, at the breaking up of the round: that the round saw nothing, because the thing is not done at the doors.* And I put the book away, and I went home by the lane, and the house stood at the end of it with its black window and its rosemary, keeping itself, and I understood, as I went in at my own door, that the round had gone past the church and past the gate and past the house, and had not gone past the thing, and that the thing was closer than the round, and had been closer since the first night, and was in the writing now.

---

I sat at the desk with the fire low, and I did not take out the book, because I had written enough that week, and the writing of it was the keeping of it, and the keeping of it was the thing I had been doing since I came to the town. I sat with the cold at the window, and the lamp came up at the end of the lane at nine, and I watched it, and I thought of the man at the tower, and of the questions he had asked, and of the questions he had not asked.

A question about letters might concern a letter. A question about houses might concern a house. The same was true of papers. His visit to the tower told me only that he had gone there and looked.

I had been looking at the town as a set of things: the bell, the lamp, the gate, the papers, the names. The man from the inn looked at the town as a set of places. He looked at the post office, and Helen's door, and Arthur's counter, and the tower. He was learning the shape of the town, and he was doing it without saying what he was learning.

That was the thing that troubled me most. I had learned to read the town by its absences: the bell that did not ring, the lamp that appeared, the gate that opened. He was learning the town by its presences. He was looking at the places that kept things, and he was not asking what they kept. He was asking only that they be there.

I did not know whether that meant he already knew what they kept, or whether he did not care.

I put the pen down, and I did not write. I let the thought stand, and I let the night stand, and the lamp burned at the end of the lane, and I watched it, and I thought of what Helen had said: *I do not know whether it does anything at all.*

I did not know whether the man from the inn did anything at all. I knew only that he had been seen at the tower, and that he had not touched the lock, and that the frost had shown him, and that the frost had shown him not because he had moved anything, but because he had stood still.

I went to bed knowing only that the stranger was still in Alderwick and that he was still asking questions. I did not know what he wanted.
