CHAPTER 69 — WHAT WAS NOT TAKEN

I rose before the lane was awake on Sunday morning, because Saturday had left me with a question that would not wait for the week to turn.

The fire was low. The room was cold. I put the kettle on and stood over it while it heated, and I thought about the shelf in the office, and about the middle shelf that had been empty since Monday, and about the clerk's hand moving the pencil across the ledger without looking up, and about the way the office kept things without knowing whether they mattered.

I had restored the arrangement on Friday. The bundle was tied with my knot. The ruled sheet was folded on top of the leaf. The service file was on the left, front facing the air. The notebook was closed. The box was in the press.

I sat in the chair. I looked at the table.

I looked at the bundle first. The string was the same. The papers were the same. The scrap with my writing was still in the fold. The knot was my knot — the double loop, the end tucked under. I had retied it on Friday, after undoing the single loop and finding the papers unchanged. The retying was my doing. The undoing had happened before.

I looked at the ruled sheet. It was folded on top of the leaf, once, lengthwise, the way I had folded it before. I had refolded it on Friday, after finding it folded beside the leaf. The refolding was my doing. The undoing had happened before.

I looked at the service file. It was on the left, front facing the air, the way I had left it on Thursday morning. I had turned it back on Friday, after finding it turned the wrong way. The turning back was my doing. The undoing had happened before.

I sat back.

Three things had been changed. The knot. The fold. The turn.

Everything else had been left as it was. The eight papers. The leaf. The notebook. The journal. The map. The box. The bottles. The button. The ring. The cloth. The uncertain paper.

Nothing had been taken. Nothing had been added.

I thought about the office. The clerk had been copying lines into a ledger, the way a man copies a thing he has already copied a hundred times. The marks on the shelf had been made by the same hand that had written the file and the leaf and the ruled sheet. The office kept everything. The office did not know which things mattered until it was too late to ask.

I thought about the drawer Vale had kept locked and empty for fifty years. The clerk had told me about it on Saturday. The drawer was for the things the office might ask for. Vale had kept it empty. He had kept it locked. He had never put anything in it. The constable knew. The clerk knew. The office did not know, because the office did not ask about drawers.

I did not know whether the disturbance and the drawer were the same kind of thing. I did not know whether the person who had changed the knot and the fold and the turn had been looking for something specific, or whether he had been touching things to see whether they would respond, or whether he had been doing something else entirely. I did not know whether he had found what he was looking for, or whether the finding had been the touching itself.

But I knew this: the disturbance had been selective. Three things changed. Everything else left. Whatever had been done had been done with that difference in mind. I could not say whether that meant anything. I could only say that it was the case.

I stood up. I went to the press. I opened the door. The box was there. The three bundles were on the lid. The uncertain paper lay on top of everything. I lifted the bundles aside and set them on the table, and I reached into the box and took out its contents, and I laid them out on the table beside the papers, because the laying of them out was the only way to see what was there and what was not there and what had been changed and what had not.

Nothing had been added. Nothing had been taken. The box was the same box. The press was the same press. The room was the same room.

I put everything back. I shut the box. I set it back in the press. I closed the press door.

I returned to the table. I looked at the bundle. I looked at the ruled sheet. I looked at the service file. I looked at the notebook.

The arrangement was restored.

The undoing had happened.

The disturbance had been selective.

I did not know what that meant. But I knew that if I wanted to understand what had happened, I would need more than what was in the room. I would need the entry-book itself, not the leaf that described it. I would need the shelf, not the marks that described the shelf. I would need the bench records, not the file that pointed to them. I would need the constable's knowledge, not the clerk's marks.

And the constable had offered me a position that would give me access to those things.

I had refused it on Friday. I had said I was not ready to make the investigation the office's before I knew what the investigation was. I had said the office's question would become mine, and the office's answer would become mine, and if the office was wrong, I would be wrong with it.

I still believed that.

But I also believed this: the office had records I could not obtain alone. The office had questions I could not answer alone. The office kept things in ways that did not always serve the finding of them. The office was not the whole of the investigation. But it was part of it.

And the part that was not mine was the part that had been disturbed while I was away, and the disturbing of it had been selective, and the selectivity was the only question I had that I could not answer from where I was standing.

I sat in the chair. I took out my notebook. I opened it to a fresh page. I wrote what I had seen, in order. What I had been told, in order. What I had concluded, in order. I kept them apart, the way Vale had taught me, because that was the only part of the method that was mine.

What I saw: the bundle tied with a different knot. The ruled sheet folded beside the leaf. The service file turned the wrong way. Nothing missing. Nothing added. The box unchanged. The room unchanged except for the three things.

What I was told: the office keeps everything. The office does not know which things matter until it is too late to ask. The marks are the way the office remembers. The marks are not the way the office knows. Vale kept a drawer locked and empty for fifty years. The constable knows. The clerk knows. The office does not know.

What I concluded: nothing yet. The disturbance had been selective. Three things changed. Everything else left. I could not say who had done it. I could not say why. I could only say what I had seen.

I closed the notebook.

I sat in the chair. The fire was warm. The kettle was cold. The lane was quiet. The house was still. The box was shut. The papers were on the table. The bundle was tied. The ruled sheet was folded. The service file was on the left. The notebook was closed.

The arrangement was restored.

The undoing had happened.

The disturbance had been selective.

I did not know what to do with that knowledge.

But I knew what I would do on Monday.

I would go to the office. I would ask Calloway for the entry-book itself. I would ask him whether I might read it in the room, under the clerk's eye, without removing it. I would ask him whether the office had made any further inquiry through proper channels. I would ask him whether the offer he had made on Friday still stood.

I would not accept the offer on Monday.

I would ask the questions on Monday.

The answering would wait.

The kettle boiled. I poured the water. I drank it standing. I set the cup down. I looked at the table. The bundle. The ruled sheet. The service file. The notebook. The arrangement. The undoing. The selectivity.

I did not know what the selectivity meant.

But I knew that the meaning was not mine to find alone.

The lane was quiet. The house was still. The week was not over. For now.
