# Chapter 69 — The Connection

I sat in the chair until the fire went out, and I did not light it again. The notebook was on the desk, and the list was written, and the work was the same and it was not the same. In the morning I lit the fire again, and I opened the notebook, and I began to write what I actually knew, as distinct from what I assumed.

It was Vale's method, and it was the only one that worked in the room now.

*The facts as I know them:*

*Thomas Vale was a constable in this office before Calloway. He was transferred to Alderwick at some point after that, and he kept a journal of his time there. The journal contains a single line about the transfer: "To Alderwick. The reason offered is establishment. I have read the order twice. It is correctly worded. That troubles me." He did not explain what troubled him, and I did not ask.*

*I found a second copy of the Agreement in the parish register, in the vestry, and it differed from the one Vale gave me. There were passages in one that were not in the other. Vale went to the cottage on the afternoon he disappeared, and the cottage was empty when I arrived. The leather folder and the cabinet key were gone. Vale had taken the disk with the folder and key when he left. The private box was already in my possession, had been since October.*

*The map in his box is a constabulary print, but it is not of Alderwick. It is marked in three inks over thirty years. It has a stamp from an office I cannot identify.*

*One of the letters in his box is in a hand that is not his. The envelope is thinner, the address to a place I do not recognise.*

I wrote all of this down, and I wrote it carefully, and I did not draw a conclusion. I wrote what I knew, and I left the rest blank. Vale had taught me that a man who fills the blank space with assumption fills it with his own desires, not with the facts.

The facts did not explain themselves. But they did suggest a shape.

Vale had been inside the office. He had worked here, before Calloway, before me. He had kept records that were not in the office's books. He had marked a map that the office might have printed. He had corresponded with someone whose handwriting was not his own. He had been moved to Alderwick on an order that was correctly worded and that troubled him. He had left on an afternoon when he went to the vestry, and he had not returned.

I did not know what he had been doing. But I knew that the Constable's office was the place where the records were kept, and the witnesses were found, and the questions were asked. Vale had walked into the office as a worker, not as a visitor. He had known the clerks by their proper names, and he had known which books to trust and which to keep separate. He had had access, and then he had not had it, and the transfer to Alderwick had taken it away.

That was the path. Not because I believed the office had done anything wrong, or because I believed it had answers. But because it was the only place that had both the records and the people who had known Vale before he became the man who asked me whether I had eaten on Thursdays.

Calloway had offered me the place on the register. The register with the gap — the page that had never been written on, the names that were missing because the records had been removed or had never existed. Calloway had said he had looked for them for years and had not found them. I had not asked what he had been looking for, and I had not asked why.

Now I asked.

I went to the office the next afternoon. Calloway was there, as he always was, and the clerk was at his desk. I did not go through the front room. I went to the back, past the counter, and into the small room where the papers were arranged in piles.

"You wanted to see me?" I said.

He looked up from his writing. He did not close the book. He did not put down his pen. He simply waited.

"I have been thinking about the register," I said. "And about what passed through this office before I came to it."

He set the book down. "Vale's records?"

"That is one possibility. Is there anyone else who might know where they went?"

He considered that. "Ingle," he said. "He handles the correspondence. He would know if anything arrived or was sent. But Ingle has been here thirty years, and his memory is not what it was."

I wrote that in my notebook. *Ingle — correspondence. Memory. Uncertainty.* I did not write that I intended to question him. I did not write my motive. I wrote what Calloway had said, and I left the rest for later.

"I have been thinking about the offer," I said. "The one you made before."

"I have not withdrawn it."

"It has a different shape now."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he said, "It does when you know more than you did."

"I want to understand the work," I said. "Not Vale's work. The work. As he understood it. If I am going to continue what he started —"

"He did not ask you to continue anything."

I considered that. "He did not. But I am here, and I have the box, and the Agreement, and the list of items that belongs to the office. I am living in the space between what he left and what has not been found."

"That does not make you a constable."

"No," I said. "But it makes me someone who needs to know what he knew. And the only place that knowledge exists is in this office."

He was quiet for a long time. I did not think he would speak again, and then he said, "There is a selection process. It is not the same as the parish register. It is not a test of knowledge. It is a test of observation."

"Observation."

"Copying, recording, noticing what is wrong and what is merely different. Learning the difference between a report and a record. Understanding that some things must be seen twice before they are true."

I wrote that down too. *Selection. Observation. Report vs. record. Look twice before believing.*

"There is a ranking," he said. "Not pass or fail. Everyone who meets the standard has a place. The ranking decides where. The top ten threshold gives you access to the deeper work. Below that, the work is narrower. I am not promising you a placement yet."

I thought of Vale's journal: *The reason offered is establishment. I have read the order twice. It is correctly worded. That troubles me.* I thought of the stamp on the map, and the different handwriting on the envelope. I thought of the register with its blank page.

"It is not a trial," he said. "It is an assessment. If you are willing, you will report on Monday. You will be given a task, and you will record what you see. How you record it, and what you notice that is not asked for, determines the ranking."

I wrote: *Monday. Task. Record what I see. Ranking decides placement, not acceptance.*

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you stay outside. You keep the box. You keep the Agreement. You do not ask questions of the office, and the office does not ask questions of you. That is a clean boundary."

I thought about the box under the bed, and the Agreement on the desk, and the notebook full of facts that did not yet explain themselves.

"What if I ask too many questions?"

"Then you get the usual. The office answers what it can, and it does not answer what it cannot. That is a constable's job."

I closed my notebook. "I will come on Monday."

He nodded. "Then you will begin as Vale began. Learning what a constable looks for, and how he learns to see it."

That was the first time he had spoken Vale's name directly, and it was the first time I understood that the offer had changed. It was no longer about the future. It was about the past. About Vale. About what had been left behind.

I walked home through the town. The market was full, and the women were at their stalls, and they spoke of flour and coal as if nothing had changed. Nothing had changed, and everything had. In the evening I opened the notebook and wrote:

*I have agreed to the selection. I will report on Monday. I do not know what I will be asked to see. I do not know whether I will see what is asked, or what is not. I am beginning where Vale began. I do not yet know how far either of us will go.*

I closed the notebook and did not write again. Outside, the lane was quiet, and the cottage at the end of it was empty, and the work would go on without him. This time, I would be the one to see what he had seen.
