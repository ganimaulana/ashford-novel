CHAPTER 81 — THE NAMES

I did not hear from the office for three days. I had expected a summons, or a note, or some sign that the examination was continuing. But nothing came, and I found myself waiting in the way a man waits for something he does not understand.

On Saturday morning I went to the market because I needed salt and because the house was too quiet. The stalls were open, the lanes were full, and the morning was cold enough that the breath of the men and women who passed me rose in white clouds. I had not been to the market in a fortnight. I had not wanted to see anyone. I had not wanted to answer questions about why I had been seen at the office so often.

But I had no questions to answer. No one in the market looked at me differently. No one stopped me. I bought my salt, I walked the length of the stalls, and I was turning toward the lane when I saw the man in the clean coat.

He was standing by the chandler’s, the same chandler’s whose wall I had leaned against on Tuesday. He was not looking at me. He was looking at the crowd, and he was writing something in a small book, the same way the man with the notebook wrote, but slower, with more attention to the page.

I did not speak to him. I did not approach him. I went home, and I did not tell anyone what I had seen.

On Monday morning a note came. It was not the same hand as before. It said: The office. Ten o’clock. Same as last time.

I put on my coat and went.

The lane was quiet, but the common was not empty. There were men there already—eight or nine of them, standing in the same place, with the same hats and the same coats and the same low voices. I had thought I might not recognise them. I had thought three days might make them strangers again. But I did recognise them. I knew the shape of the man who had stood beside me on Tuesday. I knew the older man who had shaken his head by the pump.

They knew me too, or at least they remembered me.

The man with the clean coat was there, standing apart from the others, his book closed but still in his hand. When he saw me, he nodded. I nodded back.

The door opened. The man in the grey coat appeared again, the same cap under his arm, the same sheet of paper in his hand. He looked at the crowd and did not seem surprised by anything he saw.

“You will go to the vestry at St. Bartholomew’s,” he said. “You will wait there until you are told what to do. You will not speak to the verger. You will not leave the vestry until you are told. That is all.”

He turned and went back inside, and the door closed behind him.

We stood for a moment. Then we began to move. I was not the first to go, and I was not the last. I walked with the others, not saying anything, not looking at anyone. The man in the clean coat walked beside me, and I did not know whether he had chosen to walk beside me or whether it was only that we were both walking the same way.

The vestry of St. Bartholomew’s was a cold room with a high ceiling and a table in the centre. There were not enough chairs for all of us. Some of us stood. I was one of them. I leaned against the wall and watched the others come in.

There were more than the eleven from the second group. I counted twenty-three, then twenty-four, then twenty-six. Some I recognised from the second group, some I did not. The man who had remembered me was there. The older heavier man was there. The man with the clean coat was there.

And there were others.

A man with a grey beard, older than the rest, who sat in the corner and did not look at anyone. A young man who kept adjusting his coat as though he were not used to wearing it. A tall man who stood by the door, watching the room as I had watched the room on Tuesday.

They did not look at me. They did not need to. I was nothing in that room, and I did not mind it.

The door opened again. This time it was not the man in the grey coat. It was the clerk, the same clerk who had been in the office from the beginning, who had written my statement and told me to sign it. He carried a ledger under his arm and a pencil in his hand.

He did not look at us. He set the ledger on the table, opened it, and began to write. He wrote for a long time, and none of us spoke.

Then he looked up.

“The constable will see you one at a time,” he said. “You will wait until your name is called. You will not speak to one another while you wait. You will not leave the room until you are called. If you are not called today, you will be called tomorrow. That is all.”

He went back to the ledger, and we waited.

The first name he called was Elton. A man stood, a man I had not noticed before, and left the room. The clerk wrote something in the ledger, and we waited.

The second name he called was Thorne. Another man left.

The third name he called was Merritt. I had not heard that name before. I did not know who it belonged to. But a man in the corner, the one who had not looked at anyone, stood and left without a word.

We waited.

I did not count the names. I did not try to remember them. I only stood against the wall and listened, and I began to notice things I had not noticed before. A man named Archer was called, and he walked to the door as though he had been expecting it. A man named Finch was called, and he walked more slowly. A man named Salter was called, and he looked at the door as though he did not want to go through it.

And then the clerk said, “Peale.”

The man with the clean coat stood. He did not look at me. He did not look at anyone. He walked to the door, and the door closed behind him, and he was gone.

I did not know how long we waited. The light from the high window moved across the floor, and I watched it move, and I watched the men who remained. Some of them had not been called yet. Some of them were called and did not return. Some of them were called and returned, and I could not tell whether they had passed or failed or whether there was anything to pass or fail.

The clerk did not read the names aloud after a while. He stopped, wrote something in his ledger, and looked up.

“The rest of you will wait. You will be called as they are ready. You will not leave.”

I was one of the rest.

I stood against the wall for another hour, and then another. The man who had remembered me was called and returned, his face the same as before. The older heavier man was called and did not return. I did not know whether that meant he had passed or failed or been sent home.

I waited.

And then the clerk said, “Ashcroft.”

I walked to the door. I did not look back. I opened the door and went through.

The room beyond was small, with a narrow desk and a window that looked out onto the churchyard. Calloway was sitting at the desk. He was not writing. He was not reading. He was looking at the window, and he did not turn when I came in.

“Sit,” he said.

I sat.

He turned from the window. He looked at me the way he had looked at me before, without surprise, without interest, without anything that I could read.

“You have done the tests,” he said.

“I have,” I said.

“You have done them with others.”

“Yes.”

“And you have done them alone.”

“Yes.”

He did not say anything for a long time. He looked at the window again, and I looked at the window too, because there was nothing else to look at.

“The office has a record of your work,” he said. “The clerk has written it. The record is not the work itself. It is only what the clerk wrote. The work is something else.”

He turned back to me.

“Do you know why you are here?” he said.

I did not know how to answer. I said, “I am here because you sent for me.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No,” I said.

He did not ask again.

“There will be another task,” he said. “You will be told when and where. That is all.”

He looked at me, and I understood that I was dismissed.

I stood, I walked to the door, I opened it, and I went through. I did not know whether I had passed anything, or failed anything, or whether there was anything to pass or fail. I only knew that Calloway had asked me a question I could not answer, and that he had not expected me to answer it.

The clerk was still at the table. He did not look up. I walked past him, out of the vestry, into the lane.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open, but no one was standing on the step.

I walked home slowly. I did not know what the next task would be. I did not know whether I would be grouped with the same men or with others. I did not know what the office was looking for, or whether I was giving it to them.

But I knew the names of some of the others now. Elton, Thorne, Merritt, Archer, Finch, Salter, Peale. They were not just men in a crowd. They were men with names, and I did not know whether that made them more or less real to me.

I set the key in the lock and turned it. The door opened. The room was the same.

I sat down and looked at the window, and I thought about Calloway’s question.

_Do you know why you are here?_

I did not. But I thought about it longer than I had thought about anything in weeks, and I still did not know.

The examination was no longer anonymous. The men had names now. And soon they would have to do something together.

I waited.
