CHAPTER 81 — THE YEAR TURNS

The spring came again to Ashford. I had been keeper for a year. I had kept the forgetting. I had kept the remembering. I had written the account. I had kept the houses, the bell, the medallions, the sign, the passage, the chamber, the place beneath. I had kept everything.

And the spring came, and the snow melted, and the town woke from its winter sleep, and I was still the keeper. I could not say what I had expected. I had expected the keeping to end, perhaps. I had expected the bell to ring again, or the house to appear, or the keeper before Vale to return. None of it had happened. The spring came, and the town went about its business, and I kept the account.

I had written the red book full. I had written everything I had learned. And I had begun to understand that the account was not finished. It would never be finished. The keeping would continue. And the account would continue with it.

That spring, for the first time, I did not merely walk the boundary. I walked it as a surveyor walks a line he intends to map — slowly, deliberately, reading every stone as if it were a word in a sentence I had not yet learned to parse. The eastern marker had not moved. The fourth stone's hairline mark had not grown. The wall held. And yet I could not shake the sense that the boundary was not a thing that held the town in; it was a thing that held something else out, and that the two acts were not the same.

I began to write in the red book not just what I had learned, but what I suspected. I wrote: The boundary holds. But I do not know what it holds against. The stones do not change. But I have begun to wonder whether that is because they are strong, or because they are waiting.

I did not know the answer. I only knew that the question had become part of the keeping.

I went to the vestry, on a day in early spring, and I spoke to the clerk. "The account," I said. "I have written it. I have written everything I have learned."

The clerk looked at me. "You have written the account of the keeping."

"I have."

"And you will keep it."

"I will keep it," I said. "And I will pass it on."

He was silent. "To whom?"

"I do not know," I said. "To the keeper who comes after me."

"The keeper who comes after you." He looked at me. "You do not know who that will be."

"I do not know. But I know that the account will be kept."

He nodded. "Then the keeping is whole," he said. "The forgetting is kept. The remembering is kept. The account is written."

I stood in the vestry. The clerk had said the same words as the keeper before Vale. The keeping is whole. I had not known, until that moment, that I had been waiting for someone to say that to me. But I was not certain I believed it. The account was whole, yes — but only because I had written what I knew. What I suspected, what I feared, what I had begun to guess — those were not in the book. They could not be, not yet. To write a suspicion as if it were a fact would be to make the account a lie.

I left the vestry. I walked through the streets of Ashford, in the spring light, and I understood that the keeping was not a thing I had to do. It was a thing I was. I was the keeper. And the keeping was the whole of it.

I did not put the red book in the drawer that night. I left it on the desk, open to the last page. I had written about the snow, and the spring, and the keeper before Vale, and the account. I had written about the keeping. I had not written about the passing. I had not written about what I would do when I was no longer the keeper, because I had not yet learned to imagine that day.

I sat at the desk, and I took up my pen, and I wrote:

I am the keeper. I have kept the forgetting. I have kept the remembering. I have written the account. I will pass it on. The keeping is whole.

But as I set down the pen, I understood that the sentence was not entirely true. I would pass on the account, yes. But the keeping — the keeping was not a thing that could be passed. It was a thing that had to be taken up. And I did not know whether the keeper who came after me would take it up, or whether it would take him.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer, with the agreement, and the map, and my notebook. I did not put the medallions away. I left them on the desk. I was the keeper. I had written the account. I would pass it on. The keeping would continue.

And I sat in the dark, and I was content. The keeping had found its rhythm. The account would outlast me. I set the medallions beside the book and left them there. That was all I needed to do — except that the door still waited, and 00:00 still meant nothing to me — though I was beginning to see it as the key to the count of thirteen.

The year had turned. I had turned with it. And I was still the keeper. That, at least, I could say with certainty — even if the certainty was all I had.
