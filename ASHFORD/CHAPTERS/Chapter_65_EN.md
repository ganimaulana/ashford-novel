# Chapter 65 — The Thursday That Remained

The Thursday after Vale's death came cold and grey, and I woke with the knowledge that I would not walk up the lane. The habit of the week had become a hollow thing, and I lay in bed for a long time, listening to the town wake, and I did not get up until the fire had gone out and the room had grown cold.

When I finally dressed and went to the pump, the women were there with their talk of flour and coal, and they did not look at me differently. They did not know what I had lost. The town had not noticed that a man had died at the end of Bellweather Lane, because the town did not notice much that did not enter its own door. I filled my bucket and went home, and I sat at the desk with the notebook open, and I did not write.

It was Calloway who came to me, late in the afternoon. He stood at the door with his hat in his hand and his coat buttoned to the neck, and he did not come in. He had not come to offer comfort; he had come to talk about the finding.

"The doctor has made his report," he said. "The parish has been informed. There is no reason to suppose anything other than natural causes. The heart, given his age. He had been sitting in his chair as if he had simply stopped."

I did not ask for details. I knew what the chair looked like. I knew the room.

"There will be no inquest," Calloway continued. "The law requires one only when there is reason to suspect violence or neglect. There is neither. The property will be secured, the effects inventoried, and if no will is found, the estate passes to the Crown. You are not to enter the cottage again without the office's say-so."

"I understand."

He looked at me for a moment, and I thought he might say something about the Thursdays, or the bread, or the way Vale had asked after my eating every time I arrived. But he did not. Calloway's register was employer and predecessor's successor, and the whole of the intimacy was that he told me about Vale. He never asked whether I had eaten.

"The clerk has made the entry," he said. "In the book, and in the second copy. I will have the key to the cottage myself. If there is anything you need from the house, you will ask me."

He left, and I stood in the doorway and watched him walk down the lane. The smoke from the chimneys rose straight up in the still air, and the frost was white on the rooftops, and the lane was empty. I turned back into the room and looked at the chair by the hearth. It was the same chair Vale had sat in, but it was not the same. The absence of the man made the room larger and colder, and the silence had a weight to it that it had not had before.

I went to the constable's office the next morning. I had a statement to make, and I had questions about the inventory. The office was the same low room off the market square, the same wooden counter, the same shelves of books and papers. The clerk was at his desk, his pen moving across the page, and he looked up when I came in.

"Ashcroft," he said. "Yes. The constable told me you would come. Sit."

I sat. The clerk was a man I had seen many times, though I did not know his name. He had been at the desk when I first enrolled, and he had written my name into the book. He never looked up from his writing, and he never volunteered anything.

"Your statement," he said, pushing a sheet of paper toward me. "Write what happened. Not what you think happened."

It was Vale's maxim, turned into the office's habit. I wrote the facts: the time I arrived, the state of the room, the body in the chair, the doctor's examination, Calloway's securing of the house. I did not write about the chipped cup or the half-cut loaf or the silence. I did not write that I had stood with my hand on Vale's shoulder and felt the cold.

The clerk read the statement and nodded. "The constable will sign it. You will keep a copy yourself, and the office keeps the second. That is the rule."

He reached for a book on the shelf behind him — the daily register, I thought — and opened it to a page marked with a ribbon. But he did not write in it. Instead he leafed to another book, further along the shelf, and wrote something there. Then he closed it and put it back.

"The entry is made," he said. "Both copies."

"Is there a record of the time of death?" I asked.

"Estimated," he said. "Sometime in the night. The doctor's report gives a window of several hours. That is all we can say."

I nodded. But as he spoke, I noticed something. The book he had opened first — the one with the ribbon — was the daily register. He had not written Vale's death there. He had written it in the second book, the ledger kept for correspondence and upward reports. That was not strictly unusual, but the daily register was the book Vale had insisted be kept in the order things came. I had seen him turn to it every Thursday, his finger following the line of a name.

I said nothing. The clerk was old, and he had his habits, and Calloway trusted him. I told myself it was nothing.

"Will there be an inquiry into his effects?" I asked.

"Not from the office," Calloway said, appearing in the doorway. He had heard me from the street. "The parish will handle the inventory. The box of the constables passes to me. Whatever Vale kept privately is a matter for the parish solicitor, unless there is a will."

"Was there any indication of a will?"

"None found in the house. The solicitor will advertise. If no one claims, the Crown takes what the Crown wants, and the rest is sold."

I did not say that Vale had a box. I did not say that it was in my possession, had been since October, when he had handed it to me in the cottage with the instruction to look at it twice. I had carried it home and hidden it under the bed, and I had not told a soul. It was mine, he had said. Not the parish's. Not anyone else's. Mine.

"The Agreement," I said quietly.

Calloway looked at me. "What of it?"

"He showed it to me. In February. It is signed by Aurelius Ashcroft. It passes to the keeper."

Calloway's face did not change. "Then the keeping passes to you. That will need to be recorded properly — the solicitor, the parish, possibly London. I will make a note."

He made a note. The clerk did not look up.

I went home through the cold afternoon. The market square was empty, the stalls taken down, the cobbles white with rime. I passed the baker's shop where Vale had bought his bread on Thursdays, and I did not stop. I passed the lane that led to the cottage, and I did not turn. I walked to my own door and let myself in, and the room was cold, and the chair by the window was empty.

I sat in that chair and looked out at the street. Vale had asked me, every Thursday, whether I had eaten. He had kept soup for me, and bread, and he had complained when I was late and denied that he had noticed. He had sent me away and expected me back, and I had gone, and I had come back, and now there was no one to ask. The Thursday had come, and he had not been there, and the habit of the week was broken.

I took Vale's box from under the bed and set it on the table. It was heavier than it looked, packed rather than filled, and the lock was simple. I turned the key and lifted the lid. Inside were the things he had shown me in August and October: the journal with the broken spine, the map marked in ink, the letters tied with browned tape, the bottles labelled in an unknown hand, the brass button worn smooth, the ring too small for his finger, and the folded cloth with the shape still pressed into the weave.

I lifted the journal out and opened it to the first page. The handwriting was steady, the ink brown with age. The first entries were from a garrison town — price of bread, a disliked sergeant, a horse destroyed, a name that appeared four times and then stopped. Then the entry that said, simply, To Alderwick. The reason offered is establishment. I have read the order twice. It is correctly worded. That is what troubles me.

I closed the journal and sat with the box open on the table. The room was cold, and the fire would not light, and I did not try. I had accepted the doctor's finding and Calloway's assessment. I had no reason to suppose otherwise. The death was natural. The investigation was closed. The parish would handle the rest.

But I thought about the register. I thought about the entry made in the correspondence ledger and not the daily book. I thought about Vale's maxim: "If there is only one copy, there is no copy." I thought about his warning that reports sent upward came back altered.

I put the detail in my own book, not the town's. I wrote: Vale's death entered in the correspondence ledger, not the daily register.

I closed the book and sat in the cold room. The fire would not light, and I did not try. Outside, the lane was quiet, and the frost was still white on the rooftops. The Thursday had not come, and the room was empty, and I was alone with the box and the note I had made. I did not know what to do with either of them. I only knew that the detail was there, and that I had written it down, and that for now that was enough.
