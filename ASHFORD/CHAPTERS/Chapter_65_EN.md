CHAPTER 65 — THE DOOR

I went to the mill on Thursday afternoon, because the afternoon was for going to places, and the going was the part of the work that asked nothing of me.

The road was the same road I had taken before. The turn past the common. The last house with its windows dark. The rise toward the hills. The wall that had been older than the mill. The gap where the mill-yard corner had been. The collapsed wall. The stones.

I had walked this road in October, when I was still new to Alderwick, when I did not know what the stones were or what the door was or what the gear in my coat was for. I had walked it in January, when I was learning the names of things. I had walked it in the dark, when I had sat beside the door and kept vigil and written three sentences in my notebook and closed it again.

Now I walked it in the cold afternoon light, with the notebook in my pocket and the gear in my coat and the knowledge of Vale's other life in my head.

The mill stood where it had stood. The wall stood where it had stood. The gap stood where it had stood. Nothing had changed.

I knelt beside the collapsed wall. I cleared the loose stones away from the frame. The stone door was there. The same faint mark on its face — a circle crossed by a short line. The same narrow depression near its centre, long and irregular, exactly the shape of the gear.

I did not take the gear out.

I sat back on my heels. I looked at the door. I looked at the mark. I looked at the depression. I looked at the wall above it, where the mill had been built around something that was already there.

Vale had sat where I was sitting. He had looked at the same door. He had kept the same vigil. He had checked the stones. He had written what he observed and kept what he concluded separate, because that was the method, and the method was the only thing that was his own. He had done it for fifty years.

I thought about the map. The district that was not Alderwick. The marks in several shades over many years. The intelligence work that had ended badly. The reassignment that was not a promotion. The town that had no need of him. The door he would guard for fifty years without understanding what was behind it.

I thought about the gear in my coat. The one I had found under the clock. The one that fit the depression. The one that, when turned under pressure, made the door open an inch. Vale had kept it for fifty years in his box, in a folded cloth, and then he had set it beneath the clock where a thing must be found and not given, and I had found it, and I had carried it in my coat for months, and I had placed it in the depression and turned it and watched the door open and close again, and I had chosen to leave it closed.

Why had he never done that?

I did not know. I could not know. The question was not mine to answer.

But I could ask it.

A man who had worked in intelligence — whatever that had meant, whatever he had done, whatever he had seen — a man who had kept originals of altered reports because he did not trust the road between the desk and the shelf, a man who had asked the same question twice, twenty minutes apart, to test whether the answer changed, a man who had said "Guarding is not the same as knowing" — such a man would not have left a thing unexplored because he was afraid of it. He would have explored it because he was not afraid of not knowing.

Unless the not-knowing was the point.

Unless the keeping of the door closed was the thing he had been keeping all along.

I looked at the door again. It was stone. It was closed. It was older than the mill. It was the same door it had been when I first found it. It had not moved. It had not spoken. It had not opened. It had not given any sign that it knew I was there.

I stood up. I brushed the dirt from my knees. I looked at the wall. I looked at the gap. I looked at the road leading back to town. I thought about the box in my room. I thought about the file in Calloway's coat. I thought about the leaf on my table. I thought about the ninth line with its blank margin. I thought about the map with its marks. I thought about the journal with its garrison years and its blank pages. I thought about the man who had sat in the chair at the end of the lane and asked me whether I had eaten and told me to keep things apart and left me a box.

I did not reach a conclusion. I did not decide that Vale's intelligence career had prepared him for the Door. I did not decide that the Door was part of his earlier work. I did not decide that he had never opened it because he had understood something I did not. I simply stood there and looked at it, the way a man looks at a thing he has seen a hundred times and is seeing again for the first time.

The door was closed. The gear was in my coat. The map was in the box. The file was in Calloway's coat. The journal was in the box. The stone was in the wall. The mark was on the stone. The track was north. The road was south. The lane was quiet. The week was not over. For now.
