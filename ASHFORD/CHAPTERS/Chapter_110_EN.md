CHAPTER 110 — THE OLDER KEEPING

I spent the early spring trying to understand the tally.

I read the book of the first keeper again, and again, and again. I copied the page of marks into my notebook. I counted them by ones, by fives, by rows. I counted them forward and backward. The count was always the same.

The tally was longer than the keeping as I knew it.

I could not yet determine how much longer. I could not yet decide whether the tally was a count of years, seasons, or something else entirely. I could not yet tell whether the marks were even meant to be counted individually. I had counted the marks correctly, or whether the marks meant something other than what the first keeper had said they meant.

But I knew that the keeping was older than the keepers I knew.

And I knew that I had to find out how old.

---

I went to the vestry.

The clerk was there, as he always was, at his table, with the books and the keys. He looked up when I came in.

"You have come about something," he said.

"I have come about the records," I said. "The oldest records you have. The records of the boundary, and the sign, and the house."

The clerk looked at me a long moment.

"The oldest records," he said slowly. "The survey records the wall. The book of the wall records the stones. The vestry book records the parish. But the oldest records — the records that came before the survey, before the book of the wall, before the vestry — those are not in the vestry."

"Where are they?"

The clerk was silent.

"There is a record," he said, "that is older than the vestry. It is kept in the Academy, in the room at the top of the stair, where the papers are kept that no one is shown unless they ask for the oldest things."

"The Academy copy," I said.

"The Academy keeps copies," the clerk said. "It keeps the schedule of the year four, and the letter of T. Wren, and the records of the trust. But the oldest thing it keeps is older than all of those."

I waited.

"There is a book," the clerk said, "in the Academy's keeping, that has no date and no author. It is the record of the boundary as it was first set. It is older than the survey, and older than the book of the wall, and older than the vestry book."

I stood in the vestry.

A book older than the survey, and the book of the wall, and the vestry book. The record of the boundary as it was first set.

"Who has seen it?" I said.

"Very few," the clerk said. "I have seen it once, in my father's time. It is kept in the Academy, and it is not shown, because it is the oldest thing the Academy keeps, and the Academy keeps the oldest things the way a man keeps the oldest things — without showing them."

I thanked the clerk. I left the vestry.

The Academy had a book older than the survey, and the book of the wall, and the vestry book. The record of the boundary as it was first set.

And I had the tally — the count of the keeping's years.

I did not know whether the book and the tally were connected.

But I knew that I had to see the book.

---

I went to the Academy in the afternoon.

Daniel was there, in the room at the top of the stair, with his papers and his ledgers, and he looked up when I came in.

"You have come about the oldest things," he said.

"You know why I have come."

"I have heard," Daniel said. "The clerk has spoken of it. There is a book in the Academy's keeping that has no date and no author — the record of the boundary as it was first set."

"And you will show it to me?"

Daniel was silent a moment.

"I have never shown it to anyone," he said. "I have looked at it, once. It is the oldest thing the Academy keeps."

"And you will show it to me?"

He looked at me a long moment. I had seen Daniel before, in the years of my keeping — a quiet young man at the Academy who kept the ledgers and the papers and never asked for anything. He had the look of a man who had been waiting for something without knowing what it was.

"You have asked for the oldest things," he said. "And you have kept what you have been given. I will show it to you."

He went to the shelf at the back of the room, where the oldest books were kept, and he took down a book — a book bound in leather so old that it had gone the colour of the dust, its spine held together with thread.

He set it on the table.

"My father was clerk before me in his own way," he said. "He kept the vestry for forty years. He told me, once, that if a keeper ever came to the Academy asking for the oldest things, I was to show him this book and ask no questions. I did not believe a keeper would ever come. I have kept the Academy for six years, and no one has asked."

He looked at me.

"I did not think you would be the one to ask."

"The record of the boundary," he said. "As it was first set."

I opened it.

The first page was not a page of words. It was a page of marks.

The same marks as the tally.

Rows of marks, in the same hand, running across the page.

I stood very still.

The tally in the book of the first keeper.

And the same marks, in the same hand, in the oldest book in the Academy.

"The marks," Daniel said. "You know them."

"I know them," I said. "There is a page of the same marks in the book of the first keeper. He wrote that they are the count of the years — one mark for each year, kept since the first year."

"And this book," Daniel said, "records the boundary as it was first set. If the marks are the same, in the same hand..."

I did not let him finish.

I counted the marks in the Academy book.

The count was the same as the tally.

The same number of marks. The same rows. The same groups.

Two books. Two pages of the same marks. The same hand.

I stood in the room at the top of the stair, and I understood.

The tally in the book of the first keeper was not a count of the keeping's years.

It was a count that had been made before the boundary was set.

The same hand that had set the boundary had made the tally.

And the count was older than the boundary itself.

---

I did not tell Daniel what I had concluded.

I thanked him. I left the Academy. I walked home through the streets of Ashford, with the two counts in my mind — the tally in the book of the first keeper, and the marks in the oldest book of the Academy, the same marks, in the same hand, the same count.

The boundary had been set by the first keeper.

The tally had been made by the first keeper.

And the marks in the oldest book of the Academy were the same marks, in the same hand.

The count was older than the boundary.

I did not know what that meant. I could not say what the count was counting. I could not tell for certain why the first keeper had made the same tally in two books.

But I knew that the keeping was older than the boundary.

And I knew that the boundary had been set to mark something that had been counted before.

I sat at my desk that evening, with the two counts before me, and I opened the red book, and I wrote:

*The tally in the book of the first keeper is the same as the marks in the oldest book of the Academy. The same hand. The same count.*

*The boundary was set by the first keeper. The tally was made by the first keeper. And the same tally stands in the oldest record of the boundary.*

*The count is older than the boundary.*

*I do not know what the count is counting.*

*But I know that it was counted before the boundary was set.*

*And I know that the boundary was set to mark something that had been counted before.*

I closed the red book.

I sat in the dark.

I was the keeper. I was a hunter. I was a man who worked with a society that had no name.

And I had found a count that was older than the boundary.

I had no way of knowing what it was counting.

But I knew that I would find out.

The hunt had led me to the tally.

And the tally was leading me somewhere older—older than the keepers, older than the boundary, older than the name of Ashford itself. I did not yet know what lay at the end of that trail, but I knew the trail was not new. It had been marked before I was born. The same hand had set the boundary and made the tally, and the same hand had written its count into the Academy's oldest book, and I did not know why a single man would make the same count in two books — unless he was leaving a record of something that would need to be checked, by the keepers who came after him, against a thing that must not be allowed to change.
