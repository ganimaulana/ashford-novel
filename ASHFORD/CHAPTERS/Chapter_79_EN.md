CHAPTER 79 — THE VISITOR

The winter came, and the town settled into its old stillness. The snow came early that year, a thin first dusting that lay on the fields and melted, and then the cold settled in and would not lift, and the town drew its doors close and its lamps early. I kept the agreement. I kept the account. I walked the boundary as the frost formed on the grass and the first snow fell on the fields beyond the common. The house did not appear. I had not expected it to. The house appeared when the door needed to be opened, and I had not needed to open the door. I had needed to keep.

I wrote in the red book each day, and I read what I had written, and I added the account of the autumn — the bell, the houses, the chamber, the choice. I had not chosen. I had understood that the choice was not mine. I had kept the forgetting, and I had kept the remembering, and I had waited. The winter was the waiting made visible — the long dark, the closed doors, the town holding its breath under the cold.

And then, in the darkest month of the winter, the visitor came.

I was at my desk when I heard the knock. It was not a knock I knew. It was not Crowe's knock, or the clerk's, or Margaret's. It was a knock I had never heard before — and yet I knew at once what it meant. I had been waiting, I understood, for a long time, without knowing I was waiting. I set down my pen. I went to the door.

I opened the door. A man stood in the doorway. He was old — older than Vale had been, older than anyone I had seen in Ashford. His face was lined with years, cut deep as the first sign, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had seen a great deal and had not spoken of most of it. He wore no coat against the cold, and I understood at once that he had not come from the town — that the cold of the passage had been his weather for fifty years, and that the cold of a winter night was no colder.

He stood in the doorway, in the winter cold, and he looked at me. His clothes were the clothes of the passage — dark, dusty with stone, worn at the knees where he had knelt. He had come from the chamber, and he had come to my door.

"You are the keeper," he said.

"I am."

"I know." He looked past me, into the room, at the desk where the Tokens lay. "I have come to see the account."

"Who are you?"

"I am the keeper before Vale," he said.

I stared at him. The keeper before Vale. The keeper who had opened the door. The keeper who had not come back.

"You did not come back," I said.

"I did not come back to the town," he said. "I came back to the passage. I have been in the passage for fifty years."

I stood in the doorway. The keeper before Vale. He had not died. He had been in the passage. The cold came in past him, and I did not feel it. I understood that the keeper who had opened the door had been alive all this time, in the passage, beneath the town, and that I had been keeping the account of a keeping that he had lived.

"I have been in the passage," he said, "since the night the bell rang. I have been in the chamber. I have been with the place beneath."

"And you have come back."

"I have come back," he said, "because the account needs to be written. I have been in the passage for fifty years, and I have not written the account. I have kept the place beneath, but I have not kept the account."

"Vale kept the account."

"Vale kept the forgetting," he said. "He kept the agreement. He did not keep the account. He kept the door closed, and he kept the forgetting, but he did not keep the remembering."

I stood in the doorway. The keeper before Vale had come back to write the account.

"I have been waiting," he said, "for the keeper who would write the account. I have been waiting for you."

I looked at him. "You have been waiting for me?"

"I have been waiting for the keeper who would carry the first sign. I have been waiting for the keeper who would find the second Token. I have been waiting for the keeper who would enter the chamber and set the Token in the first sign and feel the place beneath."

I was silent. I had done all of those things.

"You have done all of it," the keeper said. "And you have written the account. I have come to see it."

I stepped back. I let him in.

He crossed the room, slowly, the way a man crosses a room when he has not crossed a room in fifty years. He did not look at the walls, or the window, or the lamp — only at the desk, at the red book. He sat in my chair as if the chair had been waiting for him, and he did not touch the book.

I opened it. I showed him what I had written. He read.

He read for a long time, in silence, turning the pages slowly, and I stood in the room, and I watched him. His face was not a face that gave anything away — it was a face that had been kept, like the keeping, for fifty years, and had learned to show nothing. But his hands were not the hands of a man reading a stranger's work. They were the hands of a man who had been looking for something for fifty years and had found it, and was holding it carefully, the way one holds a thing that could be broken.

He did not speak. He read to the end, and then he closed the book.

"It is well," he said. "The account is kept. You have written what I did not write."

"The keeper before Vale," I said. "Why did you open the door?"

He was silent a long moment. I did not hurry him. I had been the keeper long enough to know that the long silences of the keeping were not empty; they were where the true things waited.

"Because I did not understand," he said. "I had kept the door closed for years, and I had not understood it. I thought the door was the mystery. I thought opening it would show me what I was keeping."

"And it did not?"

"It did not." He looked at me. "The door opened, and I found the passage, and I walked to the chamber, and I set the Token in the first sign, and I felt the place beneath. I understood what I was keeping. I understood that the door was not the mystery. The place beneath was."

"Then why did you not come back?"

He was silent for a long time. I watched him, and I did not know what a man did in a passage for fifty years, or whether the passage had held him, or whether he had held himself.

"Because I was ashamed," he said. "I had opened the door because I wanted to know. I had wanted to know, and I had opened the door, and I had found what I was keeping — and I did not know what to do with it. I stayed in the passage because I was ashamed that I had opened the door without understanding."

I stood in the room. The keeper before Vale had opened the door, and he had been ashamed, and he had stayed in the passage for fifty years. And I understood, standing in the lamplight, that I had nearly done the same thing — I had set the Token in the first sign, felt the place beneath, and taken it away, and I had gone back to my rooms and written it down, because the writing was my way of not kneeling there until I understood.

"I have come back," he said, "because I have read the account. You have written what I did not write. You have kept the remembering. And I have understood that the forgetting was not the whole of the keeping."

He stood. He looked at me.

"The passage is still open," he said. "The bell has rung. The door is not a door. The place beneath is not a thing to be opened or closed. It is a thing to be kept."

"I know."

"And you have written the account."

"I have."

"Then the keeping is whole," he said. "The forgetting is kept. The remembering is kept. The account is written."

He moved toward the door. I moved with him, and I did not know what to say, and I understood that there was nothing to say — the old man had come to see the account, and he had seen it, and the account had told him what he needed to know, and the words between us had done their work and were finished.

"I will not come back," he said. "I have been in the passage for fifty years, and I have come back to see the account, and I have seen it, and I will not come back."

"Where will you go?"

He was silent a moment. "Back to the passage," he said. "Or beyond. I do not know. But I have done what I came to do."

He opened the door. The winter cold came in.

"The account is yours," he said. "The forgetting is the town's. The remembering is the keeper's. You have kept both. That is the whole of it."

He went out.

I stood in the doorway, in the cold, and I watched him walk down the street, into the dark. He walked the way he had walked into the room — slowly, deliberately, a man who had not been in a hurry for fifty years and was not in a hurry now. He did not look back. The snow was falling, and he walked into it, and the dark took him, and I could not see him anymore.

I closed the door. I went to my desk. I sat down. I opened the red book. I wrote:

The keeper before Vale came back tonight. He was in the passage for fifty years. He came back to see the account. He read it. He said it was well. He said the forgetting is the town's. The remembering is the keeper's. He said I have kept both. He went back to the passage. I do not know whether I will see him again. But I have written the account. And I have understood that the keeping is the whole of it.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer. I left the Tokens on the desk. And I sat in the dark, and I thought about the keeper before Vale, and about the passage, and about the fifty years, and about the account.

But I could not stop thinking of what he had said — that he had opened the door because he wanted to know, and that he had been ashamed of wanting to know, and that he had stayed in the passage because of the shame. I had opened no door. The bell had rung for me, and the passage had opened, and I had gone down. I had not chosen to want to know; the wanting had chosen me. And yet I could not tell whether that made my keeping safer than his, or only luckier. The shame was not in the opening. The shame was in the not understanding — and I did not understand, and I had only the account to put between myself and the passage, the way he had put fifty years.

The snow fell beyond the window. The old man was out in it, walking back to the passage, or beyond. I did not know whether I would ever understand what the place beneath was, or whether I would carry the not-understanding all my life, as he had carried it. The account was mine now in a way it had not been before — the old man had given it to me, and taken nothing. The keeping would not be whole for me as it had been for him, because I had not earned it with fifty years. I would have to earn it with my own time, whatever the time held, whatever the passage gave back or kept.
