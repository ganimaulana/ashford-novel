CHAPTER 79 — THE VISITOR

The winter came, and the town settled into its old stillness. I kept the agreement. I kept the account. I walked the boundary as the frost formed on the grass and the first snow fell on the fields beyond the common. The house did not appear. I had not expected it to. The house appeared when the door needed to be opened, and I had not needed to open the door. I had needed to keep. I kept.

I wrote in the red book each day, and I read what I had written, and I added the account of the autumn — the bell, the houses, the chamber, the choice. I had not chosen. I had understood that the choice was not mine. I had kept the forgetting, and I had kept the remembering, and I had waited.

And then, in the darkest month of the winter, the visitor came.

---

I was at my desk when I heard the knock. It was not a knock I knew. It was not Crowe's knock, or the clerk's, or Margaret's. It was a knock I had never heard before.

I opened the door. A man stood in the doorway. He was old — older than Vale had been, older than anyone I had seen in Ashford. His face was lined with years, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had seen a great deal and had not spoken of most of it.

He stood in the doorway, in the winter cold, and he looked at me. "You are the keeper," he said. "I am." "I know." He looked past me, into the room, at the desk where the medallions lay. "I have come to see the account." I stood in the doorway. "Who are you?" "I am the keeper before Vale," he said.

I stared at him. The keeper before Vale. The keeper who had opened the door. The keeper who had not come back. "You did not come back," I said. "I did not come back to the town," he said. "I came back to the passage. I have been in the passage for fifty years."

I stood in the doorway. The keeper before Vale. He had not died. He had been in the passage. "I have been in the passage," he said, "since the night the bell rang. I have been in the chamber. I have been with the place beneath." "And you have come back." "I have come back," he said, "because the account needs to be written. I have been in the passage for fifty years, and I have not written the account. I have kept the place beneath, but I have not kept the account." "Vale kept the account." "Vale kept the forgetting," he said. "He kept the agreement. He did not keep the account. He kept the door closed, and he kept the forgetting, but he did not keep the remembering." I stood in the doorway. The keeper before Vale had come back to write the account. "I have been waiting," he said, "for the keeper who would write the account. I have been waiting for you."

I looked at him. "You have been waiting for me?" "I have been waiting for the keeper who would carry the first sign. I have been waiting for the keeper who would find the second medallion. I have been waiting for the keeper who would enter the chamber and set the medallion in the first sign and feel the place beneath." I was silent. I had done all of those things. I had carried the first sign. I had found the second medallion. I had entered the chamber. I had set the medallion in the first sign. I had felt the place beneath. "You have done all of it," the keeper said. "And you have written the account. I have come to see it." I stepped back. I let him in.

He crossed the room, slowly, and he sat at my desk, and he looked at the red book. I opened it. I showed him what I had written. He read. He read for a long time, in silence, turning the pages slowly, and I stood in the room, and I watched him. He did not speak. He read to the end, and then he closed the book. "It is well," he said. "The account is kept. You have written what I did not write." I looked at him. "The keeper before Vale," I said. "Why did you open the door?" He was silent a long moment. "Because I did not understand," he said. "I had kept the door closed for years, and I had not understood it. I thought the door was the mystery. I thought opening it would show me what I was keeping." "And it did not?" "It did not." He looked at me. "The door opened, and I found the passage, and I walked to the chamber, and I set the medallion in the first sign, and I felt the place beneath. I understood what I was keeping. I understood that the door was not the mystery. The place beneath was." "Then why did you not come back?" He was silent for a long time. "Because I was ashamed," he said. "I had opened the door because I wanted to know. I had wanted to know, and I had opened the door, and I had found what I was keeping — and I did not know what to do with it. I stayed in the passage because I was ashamed that I had opened the door without understanding."

I stood in the room. The keeper before Vale had opened the door, and he had been ashamed, and he had stayed in the passage for fifty years. "I have come back," he said, "because I have read the account. You have written what I did not write. You have kept the remembering. And I have understood that the forgetting was not the whole of the keeping." He stood. He looked at me. "The passage is still open," he said. "The bell has rung. The door is not a door. The place beneath is not a thing to be opened or closed. It is a thing to be kept." I looked at him. "I know." "And you have written the account." "I have." "Then the keeping is whole," he said. "The forgetting is kept. The remembering is kept. The account is written." He moved toward the door. "I will not come back," he said. "I have been in the passage for fifty years, and I have come back to see the account, and I have seen it, and I will not come back." "Where will you go?" He was silent a moment. "Back to the passage," he said. "Or beyond. I do not know. But I have done what I came to do." He opened the door. The winter cold came in. "The account is yours," he said. "The forgetting is the town's. The remembering is the keeper's. You have kept both. That is the whole of it." He went out. I stood in the doorway, in the cold, and I watched him walk down the street, into the dark, until I could not see him anymore.

I closed the door. I went to my desk. I sat down. I opened the red book. I wrote:

The keeper before Vale came back tonight. He was in the passage for fifty years. He came back to see the account. He read it. He said it was well. He said the forgetting is the town's. The remembering is the keeper's. He said I have kept both. He went back to the passage. I do not know whether I will see him again. But I have written the account. And I have understood that the keeping is the whole of it.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer. I left the medallions on the desk. And I sat in the dark, and I thought about the keeper before Vale, and about the passage, and about the fifty years, and about the account. I had written the account. The keeper before Vale had read it. He had said it was well. And he had gone.

I sat in the dark, and I understood that the keeping was the whole of it. I was the keeper. I kept the forgetting. I kept the remembering. I kept the account. And that was the whole of my duty.
