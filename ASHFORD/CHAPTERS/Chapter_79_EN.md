CHAPTER 79 — THE DECISION

A boy came to the lane on Thursday morning with a note and did not wait for an answer. The note said only: The office. Before noon.

I put on my coat and went. The lane was quiet. The common was empty. At the office door the other three were already waiting — the man with the notebook, the older man with the frayed cuffs, the younger man. We went in together.

Calloway was at the desk. Orme stood by the window with his hat on. The clerk sat with the ledger closed in front of him.

"Sit," Calloway said.

We sat. He did not sit himself. He took a sheet of paper from the desk and read from it, slowly.

"Yesterday evening a man named Garland did not come home. He is a sawyer at the timber yard by the mill gate. Thirty-one years old. Married, no children. He finished his work at dusk, took his wages, and was seen walking toward the river path by the man at the coal wharf, at about half-past six. Since then nobody has seen him.

"His wife raised the matter this morning. His lodgings are a room above the chandler's in Cross Lane. The landlady says his bed was not slept in. On the table were his coat, his purse with the wages still in it, and a paper with writing on it. She will not say what the writing was, only that it was short and that she did not like it.

"There is one more thing. The coal wharf man says Garland had been out of sorts for a fortnight. He says Garland told him on Tuesday that he meant to settle a matter he had left too long."

He set the paper down.

"The rain came in the night from the west and the river is up. There is a wind this morning and more cloud coming behind it. That is the account."

He looked at each of us.

"You have the account. You have the town. You have the day. Decide what you will do with it, and do it. Report to the clerk when you come back."

He put on his hat and went out. Orme followed him without a word.

The clerk did not look up. The room was ours.

The younger man spoke first, because he always did.

"He was seen at the river path at half-past six. We go to the river path."

"It rained in the night," the older man said.

"It rained. Footprints do not last through rain. What lasts is where he went afterward, and that is in his room — the paper on his table would tell us more than any path."

"The landlady will not say what the writing was," the man with the notebook said.

"She will say it to four men standing in her passage."

"She would not say it to the constable's man this morning," the older man said.

The man with the notebook had the account sheet in front of him.

"There are two places and one morning," he said. "The room above the chandler's, and the river path. Whichever we go to first, the other waits."

"The room keeps," the older man said. "The coat and the purse will be there tonight and tomorrow. What will not keep is the path. Half the town will walk it by noon. The rain has already had one night at it. And if a man went down to the water in the dark, the water does not wait for anyone."

I had been thinking of the room. A man who leaves his wages behind is not going anywhere for wages' sake, and the paper might tell us whether he had gone somewhere on purpose. But the older man had seen the cost of waiting: the path was being spent whether we walked it or not.

"The room will keep until this evening," I said. "The path will not keep until this afternoon. If we cannot do both, the one we put off should be the one that does not change."

The younger man did not like it.

"If he went to the water," he said, "then the river has him, and a paper on a table will not tell us anything we do not know."

"If the river has him," the older man said, "we will know it by the path. If the river does not have him, the path will tell us which way he went, and the paper will still be on the table at supper."

The man with the notebook wrote two lines and looked at them.

"One thing more," he said. "The landlady has already let the constable's man go away with nothing. By this evening the room may not be as it is now either — a woman with a room standing empty over her shop wants it let again. But an evening is not a morning. The path is losing more by the hour than the room is."

The younger man sat back. He was not satisfied, and he did not pretend to be.

"The path first, then," he said. "But I say the room would have told us more."

"That may be so," the older man said. "We will find out tonight, or we will not."

We went out. The clerk wrote something as we passed.

The river path began behind the coal wharf. The river was brown and fast against the pilings.

We split the path three ways, the older man on the bank side, the younger man down by the water, the man with the notebook and I along the middle. For half a mile there was nothing but the wind and the coal dust blown off the wharf.

The younger man found it. A gap in the hedge where the dead thorn had been broken back, the break pale and new. On the mud below it, the print of a nailed boot, half gone with the seep of water through the bank, and the mark of something set down heavy in front of it.

"He came off the path here," the younger man said.

We looked. The mud was cut with the one print and a second beside it, deeper, as though a man had stood a while in the soft ground. Past the hedge the field ran up toward the lane behind the chandler's.

"Or he stood here," the man with the notebook said, "and went back the way he came. One print does not say which way a man was facing."

"Two prints," the older man said. "He stood. The break in the thorn says he went through or meant to. The field goes up to Cross Lane."

I looked back along the path, downstream, and up at the field. I had nothing to add that the mud had not already said.

"He went through the hedge," I said. "Which way after that is guessing."

We followed the line of the field up. At the stile there was nothing. At the lane behind the chandler's, the mud gave out onto gravel and the gravel said nothing to anybody. By then it was past noon.

We went to Cross Lane.

The chandler's shop was shut. The landlady came to the door with her shawl over her head and her keys in her hand.

"We have not let the room," she said, before anyone had asked her anything. "And I have told the constable's man all I mean to tell anyone."

She began to shut the door. The older man did not put his foot in it or his hand on it.

"His things are still on the table," he said. "The coat and the purse. We are not asking about the writing. We are asking whether the room is as it was this morning."

"It is as it was," she said. "Nobody has been up."

"Then we will not trouble you for it today," he said. "But we will come back with the constable's man, and the room will want to be as it is now when we do."

She looked at him for a while. Then she shut the door, and we heard the key.

We walked back in silence. It began to rain again before we reached the office.

The clerk was at the ledger. Calloway was not in the room. Orme was there, standing where he had stood in the morning. He looked at our wet coats and said nothing.

The man with the notebook gave the account, and he gave it plainly: the hedge gap, the two prints, the break in the thorn, the gravel that said nothing, the landlady's door. The older man added that the room had been confirmed untouched and would need to be taken with the constable's man. The younger man said, without being asked, that he still thought the room would have told us more.

The clerk wrote it all down. He did not read it back to us. He did not ask us to put our names to it.

"Mr. Garland's wife has been sent for to her sister at Wick Hill," he said, when he had finished. "The constable will walk the river path himself tomorrow, if the water lets him."

We went out. The older man went down toward the market. The younger man went with him. The man with the notebook stood a moment under the office eaves with me.

"The prints might have been there since Tuesday," he said. "The wharf man said Garland talked of settling a matter on Tuesday. We spent the morning on mud that may be two days old, because it was the mud that was going."

"Yes," I said.

"And the paper may have said he had a brother in Wick Hill and meant to walk there tonight."

"Yes."

He looked out at the rain.

"Then we chose the thing we could not afford to lose," he said, "and the thing we could not afford to lose may not have been worth having. That is not the same as being wrong."

He put up his collar and went.

I walked home through the rain. At my door I stood a moment out of the wet before I set the key in the lock.

Garland's coat and purse were still on a table above the chandler's shop, and nobody had read what the paper said. The prints by the broken thorn would be gone by tomorrow, if they had not gone already.

I had wanted the room all morning, and I had said the path, because the older man's arithmetic was better than my wanting. I did not know whether that was judgment or only the weight of his voice. I did not know whether the mud had been Garland's or a fortnight of other men's. I did not know what the landlady's paper said.

But I knew this much: we had spent the morning on the thing that does not wait, and the thing that waits had waited, and tomorrow the room would still be there — unless it was not, because we had shown the landlady that we could be put off with a shut door and had gone away. Whatever the paper said, we had chosen not to know it today. That choice had been ours, and we had made it together, and the cost of it was not anything we would be able to see.
