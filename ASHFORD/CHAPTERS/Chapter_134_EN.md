CHAPTER 134 — THE HOUSE ON THE HILL

I walked toward the house on the hill.

The road led up from the town, past the wall of old stone, to a gate that stood open. Beyond the gate, a drive of packed earth led to the house.

I did not know what the house was. I did not know why the count had brought me here.

But the medallion was warm.

I passed through the gate. I walked up the drive.

The house was old. It was large, built of stone, with a roof of slate and windows that looked out over the town. It had been a house of some importance, once, and had fallen into a quieter age.

I came to the door.

It was closed.

I did not knock. I stood at the door, with the medallion warm against my chest, and I waited.

The door opened.

A woman stood in the doorway. She was older, in a plain dress, with a face that had seen many years. She looked at me for a long moment.

"You are the keeper," she said.

I did not deny it.

"You have come for the medallion," she said.

I stood very still.

"The medallion," I said. "The count has been calling it. It led me here."

She nodded.

"It has been waiting," she said. "It has been waiting for you."

She stepped back.

"Come in."

---

I entered the house.

It was quiet. The rooms were furnished, but plainly. The walls were lined with books, and papers, and the things of a long life.

The woman led me to a room at the back of the house, where a fire burned low.

There was a table in the middle of the room.

On the table, there was a box.

A wooden box, small, dark, plain.

The same size as the box that had held my medallion, all my life.

I looked at the box.

"Open it," the woman said.

I crossed the room. I opened the box.

Inside, on a bed of cloth, there was a medallion.

It was like mine. The same size. The same tarnished metal. The same cord.

And on its face, the same mark.

A circle.

Three lines through it.

A single dot.

The first sign.

I took it out.

It was cold.

I held it in my hand, and I looked at it, and I understood that I had found a third medallion.

The medallion I had carried all my life — the first sign, the mark of the maker, the medallion of my childhood. The medallion I had found in the kept house at the end of Bellweather Lane, that had warmed only for that house. And this medallion, in the box, in the house on the hill.

Three medallions. Three first signs.

"The count called you to it," the woman said. "The medallion has been waiting for you."

"Whose was it?" I said.

She was silent a moment.

"It belonged to the keeper before the first keeper," she said. "The keeper who came before the one who set the boundary. The keeper who made the first sign."

I stood in the room, with the cold medallion in my hand.

The keeper before the first keeper.

The one who had made the first sign.

"His medallion," I said.

"His medallion," she said. "It has been kept here, in this house, since his time. It has been kept until the keeper came for it."

I looked at the medallion in my hand.

The medallion of the keeper before the first keeper.

It was the third. I had carried one all my life. I had found the second in the kept house at the end of Bellweather Lane. And this was the third — the one that had been kept for the keeper who would come.

It was cold. My own medallion was warm. The second was warm, in my coat.

One warm from my own keeping. One warm from the kept house. And this one — cold.

But I knew that I had been led to it.

The count had called me north. The count had led me through the marsh, and away from the marsh, and to this town, and to this house.

And the count had led me to the medallion.

The medallion of the keeper before the first keeper.

---

I did not leave the house that night.

The woman gave me a room, and I stayed, and I looked at the medallion.

It lay on the table before me, cold.

My own medallion lay beside it, warm.

Three medallions. The first sign on each.

One warm. One cold.

I did not know what the difference meant. I did not know why my medallion was warm and the other was cold.

But I knew that they were connected.

I set the three medallions on the table. The first — the one I had carried all my life. The second — the one from the kept house. The third — the one from the house on the hill.

Three medallions. The first sign on each.

I looked at them.

The first was warm. The second was warm. The third was cold.

And I waited.

I did not know what would happen.

But I knew that something would.

I sat in the room, in the lamplight, and I watched the three medallions.

And in the middle of the night, the cold medallion warmed.

It warmed slowly, without touch, without cause — as if the other two, lying beside it, had reached out to it.

I watched it warm.

And I understood that the three medallions were meant to be together.

The keeper's medallion. The kept house medallion. The medallion of the keeper before the first keeper.

Three medallions. Three first signs.

And I was the keeper of all three.

I sat in the room, in the lamplight, and I looked at the three medallions, all warm now, side by side.

The count had called me to them.

The count had brought them together.

And I was the keeper of both.
