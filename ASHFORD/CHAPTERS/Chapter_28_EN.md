# Chapter 28 — The Counter and the Question

The Friday came in dry and cold, with the sky gone the colour of pewter, and the town under it going about its week's end, and I went about mine, and carried the telling of the day before in my pocket the way a man carries what he had not yet made up his mind to.

The lamp had burned at nine on the Thursday, and been carried away along the rooms, and no sound came. I set it down as the fourteenth reading and let it stand.

I did not know whether the absence meant anything.

The house had kept its pattern before, and patterns became dangerous only when a man mistook them for rules.

I had done enough of that already.

It was Arthur that broke the morning.

I had gone to his shop with the book I had carried out the week before, because a book is the thing a man carries when he wants a counter to stand at, and the standing at the counter is the way of speech in this town. I had come for the speech of it without knowing I had come.

Arthur was not behind the counter when I entered.

The shop stood with its smell of old paper and its silence. I put the book down and waited. After a little while I heard him come down the stair at the back, slower than his way, and he came to the counter and looked at me without looking at the book.

"Put it down," he said. "There is a thing I will say to you before the book, because the book can wait, and the thing cannot. Shut the door, if you please."

I shut the door and came back.

Arthur stood behind the counter with both hands upon it, which was the way he stood when he wanted the keeping of himself.

"The man came here yesterday," he said.

"The man from the inn?"

Arthur nodded.

"In the afternoon. The shop was empty. He came in, stood where you are standing, and asked about books."

"What books?"

"The histories first. Town histories. Parish matters. The things that are printed and sold."

"You showed him?"

"I did."

Arthur's fingers moved once against the wood.

"He bought the history of the town."

"The old one?"

"The one printed twenty years ago. It has stood on that shelf for ten."

"Did he ask for anything else?"

"No."

He said it without hesitation.

That was what made me look at him.

"No other book?"

"No other book."

He paused.

"That is what troubles me."

"Why?"

"Because a man who wants a town's history usually wants to know something the history has failed to put in it."

"And he did not ask?"

"He did not."

Arthur looked toward the shelves.

"He bought the book and paid for it properly. I wrapped it. He took it. If that had been all, I would have thought nothing of it."

"But it was not all."

"No."

He looked at me then.

"He stood here for some time."

"How long?"

"I don't know."

"You noticed?"

"I noticed him noticing."

The shop seemed quieter after he said it.

"He looked about," Arthur continued. "Not like a man choosing another book. His eye went to the stair. Then the press in the corner. Then the parish shelves. He did not touch anything."

"Did he ask about the press?"

"No."

Arthur's hand moved beneath the counter and returned.

"The press was locked."

I waited.

"The key was in my pocket."

He tapped the pocket of his coat once.

"I kept it there."

"Was he trying to see it?"

"I cannot say."

"Then why tell me?"

"Because I was glad the key was in my pocket."

He said it quietly.

"I did not know I was glad until he had gone."

I looked toward the press.

It was an ordinary piece of furniture, dark with age, its lock no different from the locks on half the cupboards in Alderwick. There was nothing about it to make a man think twice.

That was often the difficulty.

"Did he see you put the key away?" I asked.

"No."

"Did he know it was locked?"

"I think so."

"You think?"

Arthur gave me a thin look.

"I sell books, Mr. Ashcroft. I do not sell certainty."

I nearly smiled.

He did not.

There was a silence between us.

Then Arthur said, "At the door, he asked one more thing."

I waited.

"He asked whether I kept papers."

"What sort?"

"He did not say."

"Family papers?"

Arthur nodded.

"He asked whether the shop kept papers belonging to the old families of the town."

"And what did you say?"

"That the shop keeps what is sold."

"And?"

"That papers belonging to families are not generally sold in a bookseller's shop."

Arthur's mouth tightened.

"That was enough for him."

"Did he ask where they were?"

"No."

"Did you tell him?"

"No."

"Did he ask whose papers?"

"No."

Arthur looked down at the counter.

"That is the part I cannot make sense of. If a man wants something, he usually wants to know where to find it."

"Unless he already knows."

Arthur looked up.

I had not meant to say it.

Neither of us spoke for a moment.

Then Arthur shook his head.

"I would not put that much upon it."

"Neither would I."

It was not entirely true.

He reached beneath the counter and brought out the town history.

"He left this behind in his mind, I think."

"The book?"

"The question."

I looked at the volume.

Arthur pushed it toward me.

"He bought the history. That is nothing. But if he was looking for something in the history, he will find very little. If he was looking for a name, perhaps there is one. If he was looking for a house, there are houses. If he was looking for a family, there are families."

"And if he was looking for papers?"

Arthur's eyes went to the locked press.

"Then he did not find them here."

He said nothing more.

I took up my book.

"One thing," he said.

I stopped.

"I have heard that man asking questions."

"At the post office?"

Arthur nodded.

"And elsewhere?"

"I did not say that."

"You did not need to."

His expression hardened slightly.

"You have a way of making a man say what he has not said."

"I am learning."

"Then learn this instead."

He leaned forward.

"A question does not become an answer merely because three people have heard it."

I looked at him.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean that if he asks me where something is, that tells me only that he wants to know where something is."

"And not what it is?"

"Not what it is."

"And not why?"

"Certainly not why."

Arthur straightened.

"That is all I know."

I believed him.

Or, at least, I believed that was all he was willing to put into words.

I went out into the cold.

The street was the street, and the town was going about its Friday, and nothing in it looked changed.

Yet I carried three things with me.

The post office.

Helen's door.

Arthur's counter.

The three were not the same telling.

At the post office, the man had been associated with letters that belonged to the end house.

At Helen's door, he had asked about houses and their keeping.

At Arthur's counter, he had asked whether old family papers were kept there.

I stopped beneath the eave of a closed shop and considered the three.

There was a resemblance.

That was all.

I had learned enough by then to distrust resemblances.

Still, the resemblance remained.

The man was asking questions about things that were kept.

But I could not yet say whether he was looking for one thing, three things, or merely trying to learn the shape of the town.

That last possibility troubled me more than the others.

A man looking for an object could be followed.

A man learning a town was harder to follow, because every door could become part of the work.

I walked on.

The certified page was in my desk.

The Academy copy had E. Hodge's name in it.

The notebook and key were in my keeping.

My watch-roll carried my name.

Daniel knew what I had found.

Arthur knew I had found it.

Helen knew I had been watching.

And the man at the inn had now begun to ask.

I did not know whether he knew about me.

That was the thought I disliked most.

---

The evening brought the lamp at nine.

It burned at its old height and was carried away along the rooms in the old fashion. No sound came.

I watched until the last movement was gone.

Then I opened the book.

*The lamp burned at nine. No sound followed.*

I stopped.

For once, I did not add a theory.

After a while I wrote:

*The man from the inn has asked at the post office, at Helen's door, and at Arthur's counter, though the questions were not the same. I do not know whether they concern one thing. Arthur says a question is not an answer merely because three people have heard it.*

I read the sentence twice.

Then I added:

*The press was locked. Its key remained in Arthur's pocket. The man did not ask for the key.*

I left that line beneath the others.

There was something about it I could not settle.

A man who wanted the contents of a locked press might ask for the press.

A man who wanted only to know whether it was guarded might not.

I closed the book.

Tomorrow the watch would make its round.

I had thought of that round as a duty when I first came to Alderwick. Then I had thought of it as a habit. Later, I had wondered whether the town used it to keep certain things where they belonged.

Now I wondered whether someone else was watching the watch.

That was an unpleasant thought.

I did not know if it was true.

I did not write it down.

The bell had been in my pocket for several days.

I took it out and held it over the firelight.

It looked no different from the last time.

The iron was dark. The surface was worn. It gave no warmth.

I had carried it because carrying a thing felt better than leaving it behind.

That was not a reason.

It was fear dressed as preparation.

I opened the drawer.

For a moment I held the bell above it.

Then I put it back.

I shut the drawer and turned the key.

If the bell had rules, I did not know them.

Until I did, I would not carry it as though it were protection.

The town had enough things that men kept without understanding.

I had no wish to become another of them.

---

The next morning, the constable came to my door.

I had not expected him. He stood on the step, his coat buttoned against the cold, and he looked at me the way he had looked at me at the station house—as a fact to be measured.

"Mr. Ashcroft," he said.

"Sir."

"I have a small matter. Not a case. A question."

He did not wait for me to invite him in. He stepped past me into the room, and I shut the door behind him. He looked at the fire, at the table, at the book on the desk. He did not sit.

"The ledger," he said. "The girl's version is written up. But I have been thinking about what you said about the window—that it was opened once, in the morning, and not again. I have a statement from a man who says he saw the window open at noon. That would be two openings."

"Or the same opening, left open."

"Exactly. He says he saw it open, not that he saw it opened. The difference is not small."

He looked at me.

"Would you read his statement? Tell me whether it says what he saw, or what he assumed."

I took the paper. It was a single page, written in a clerk's hand. The man's name was Thomas Hemp, a carrier who had passed the shop at noon. He stated that he saw the window "standing open." He did not say it was opened at that hour. He said he remembered it because the frost had melted on the sill, and he thought it odd that anyone would leave a window open in such weather.

I read it twice.

"He saw the window open," I said. "He did not see it opened. The frost had melted because it had been open since morning. That is consistent with what I told you."

The constable nodded.

"That is what I thought. But I wanted to see whether you would find the same distinction."

He took the paper back and folded it.

"You see things that are not stated. That is useful."

He did not praise me. He stated it as a fact.

He turned to the door.

"One more thing," he said. "The man from the inn. He has been asking about the watch. He asked me who kept the list. I told him it was the parish book. He asked whether anyone had been added recently. I told him the roll changes with the season."

"And what did he say?"

"He said nothing. He wrote it down."

He opened the door.

"Good morning, Mr. Ashcroft."

He left.

I stood in the room with the cold coming in from the open door, and I understood that I had been tested again, and that I had passed without knowing it was a test.

I wrote nothing.

I went to the fire and warmed my hands.

The constable had not recruited me.

He had simply asked me to read a statement.

But I knew, now, that he was watching me the way I was watching the gate: for a pattern that might not be there, and might be there when I least expected it.

The lamp would come at nine.

The gate would remain shut.

And the man from the inn would keep writing names.

I did not know whether the three things were one thing.

I did not know whether I was meant to find out.

I only knew that I would keep watching, because that was the only thing I knew how to do that was not guessing.
