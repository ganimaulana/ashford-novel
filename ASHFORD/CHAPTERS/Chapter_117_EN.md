CHAPTER 117 — THE CONSTABLE BEFORE VALE

I looked for Marcus Ferrand again.

I had looked for him once before, in the third test, and I had found nothing — no trace, no grave, no record after the year of his retirement. I had concluded that he had been deliberately removed from the records, and the winter had passed, and the finding of the first sign on the floorboard of the reading room had given me a reason to look again.

I had been right, the first time. He had been removed. But now I knew that he had used the reading room. I knew that he had been a member of the society. I knew that he had been the constable before Vale. And I knew that the first sign was on the floor of the room he had used — the same first sign that stood at the base of every boundary stone, the same first sign that was the language of the count.

I went to see Calloway.

He was in his office, at his desk, and he looked up when I came in. The lamp was burning, and the office was warm against the cold, and he was not surprised to see me. I had the sense that he had been waiting for this visit, the way the keeper of a door waits for the man who will finally knock.

"You have been asking about Ferrand again," he said.

"I have."

"I thought you had finished with that name."

"I had," I said. "Then I found something. He used a room above the chandler's shop. A room with a mark on the floorboard. A room the society took after his death."

Calloway was silent a long moment. I watched his hands, and I saw them still on the desk, the way a man's hands still when he is deciding something.

"The room," he said slowly. "The room above the chandler's shop. I have known it was there for years. No record ever said who used it. I asked once, and the answer was that it was not the town's business — and I let it be, because the town keeps better when its secrets are kept."

"He was a constable," I said. "He was also a member of a society. And he used that room."

Calloway looked at me.

"How do you know he was a member of a society?"

"Because the society took the room after his death," I said. "And because I was given his name, in a test, by the society."

Calloway stood.

He went to the cabinet at the back of the office. He took out a key I had not seen him use before, and he unlocked a drawer I had not seen opened. The drawer was at the very bottom of the cabinet, and it moved heavily, as if the weight in it was not the weight of paper.

He took out a box.

"This is the box of the constables," he said. "It was passed to me by Vale, and to Vale by Ferrand, and to Ferrand by the man before him. It holds the things that the constable keeps because they cannot be written down."

He set the box on the desk. It was old, bound in dark leather, worn soft at the corners, the kind of box that has been handled by many hands over many years and shows it. I looked at it, and I did not reach for it.

"I have not opened it," Calloway said. "Vale told me not to open it, unless there was a reason. He said a constable who opens the box for curiosity will find only the curiosity, and one who opens it for a reason will find the reason. I have kept it closed for all the years I have had it. I think you have given me a reason."

He opened the box.

Inside, on a bed of dark cloth, lay a notebook.

Not the red book. Not the book of the first keeper. A different notebook — old, bound in dark leather, its pages yellow with age, its corners worn the way the corners of a thing are worn that has been carried in a coat for years. It lay in the box the way a thing lies that has been waiting.

Calloway did not touch it.

"It is Ferrand's," he said. "It was in the box when Vale passed it to me. I have never opened it."

I looked at the notebook.

"Did Vale tell you it was in there?"

Calloway thought about it.

"He told me the box held what could not be written down. He did not itemise it." He paused. "He was particular about that. I remember thinking it was an old man's fussiness. He would not say a thing in a room with a door open. He would not put a name in a letter. When he handed me this box he did it here, with the shutters closed, and he watched me put it in the drawer, and then he asked me for the key and gave it back to me himself."

"Why?"

"I assumed he wanted to be sure I knew where it was." Calloway looked at the box. "I have since wondered whether he wanted to be sure no one else was watching me learn it."

I reached out. I took it.

It was heavy in my hands — heavier than it should have been, the way a notebook is heavy when it holds more than a man's writing. I stood with it in my hands, in the lamp-lit office, and I understood that I was holding the last record of a man who had been removed from every other record in the town. The box of the constables had kept him the way the boundary stones kept the sign — by being a place where the ordinary records did not reach.

I opened it.

The first page was not a page of words. It was a page of marks.

The same marks as the tally.

Rows of marks, in the same hand, running across the page. I knew the hand. I had read it in the book of the first keeper. I had read it in the oldest book of the Academy. And now the same hand, the same rows, the same count, on the first page of a constable's notebook, hidden for years in a box that was not opened.

I stood very still.

The tally from the book of the first keeper.

The marks from the oldest book of the Academy.

And now the same marks, in Ferrand's notebook.

"The marks," Calloway said. "You know them."

"I know them," I said. "There is a page of the same marks in the book of the first keeper. The first keeper wrote that they are the count of the years."

"And Ferrand kept them too," Calloway said.

I looked at the notebook.

Ferrand had kept the count. The constable before Vale had kept the same count the first keeper had kept — in his own hand, in a notebook no record mentioned, in a box no one opened. The constable had been a keeper of the count the way I was, and the town had not known it. The count passed through hands, from the first keeper to the Academy to a constable of Ashford, and the hands were not the thing. The count was the thing. And the keeper was not.

---

I read Ferrand's notebook that night.

I sat at my desk, with the lamp burning low, and I read it through from the first page to the last, and I did not hurry. It was not the account of the first keeper. It was not the record of the boundary. It was the notebook of a constable — a man who had watched the town for fifteen years, and who had kept the things that could not be written down. The pages were the pages of a working life: notes on the streets, on the houses, on the weather of a town through fifteen years. And through it all, page after page, ran the marks — the same rows, the same hand, the same count, one mark for each year, kept since the first year.

He had kept the count.

And among the marks, in the margins, in a hand that grew steadier with years, he had written notes.

*The count stands at the same number. It has not changed in my time.*

*The stones do not move. The count does not change. The boundary holds.*

*I do not know what the count is counting. But I know that it is the same count that the keeper before me kept, and the keeper before him.*

I read the notes. Ferrand had kept the count. He had watched it. He had written that it had not changed in his time — the same thing I had found standing in the snow through the winter, the same stillness the stones had held for as long as I had walked them. And he had written that he did not know what it was counting.

I turned the pages. The marks ran on. The notes grew fewer, and the hand grew older, and the town he wrote about grew smaller in the writing, the way a man's world narrows. There were notes about the poor-house, and notes about the church, and notes about a daughter married and gone north. And on the last page of the notebook there was a single line, in a hand that had grown tired with years:

*I have kept the count. I have not understood it. But I have kept it, the way the keepers before me kept it, and the way the keeper who comes after will keep it.*

*The count is the thing. And the keeper is not.*

I closed the notebook.

I sat in the dark.

The count was the thing. And the keeper was not. The same words the first keeper had written. The same words I had written in the red book. I had thought, when I read them in the book of the first keeper, that they were a sentence about humility. Reading them again, in a constable's hand, in a box that was not opened, I understood them differently. They were a warning, and a comfort, and a command: the count is the thing, and the keeper is not, so keep the count, and do not ask what you are for.

Ferrand had kept the count. He had not understood it. And he had kept it, the way the keepers before him had kept it, and he had written that the keeper who comes after would keep it too.

I was the keeper who came after.

I sat in the stillness, with Ferrand's notebook before me, and I understood that I was not the first keeper to wonder what the count was counting. And I would not be the last. But I was the one who had the notebook — the one who had been given the marks in three books, and the first sign on the floor of the room he had used, and the warning, in his own hand, that the count was the thing and the keeper was not.

I opened the red book, and I wrote:

*Ferrand kept the count. He kept it for fifteen years. He did not understand it, and he kept it, the way the keepers before him kept it.*

*He wrote: "The count is the thing. And the keeper is not."*

*I am the keeper who came after.*

*I have kept the count. I have not understood it.*

*But I will find out what it is counting.*

*I have Ferrand's notebook. I have the tally. I have the marks of the Academy. I have the first sign.*

*And I will learn the count.*

I closed the red book. I sat in the dark, with Ferrand's notebook before me.

I had read the last line of a man who had kept the count for fifteen years without understanding it — a man who had written that the count was the thing and the keeper was not, and who had meant it as a comfort, and had not been comforted by it, because he had gone on asking the question to the end of his life. The notebook ended there. It did not say whether Ferrand ever found the answer. It did not say why he had been removed from the records, or who had removed him, or whether the two things were the same question.

The box of the constables had kept him. The room above the chandler's shop had kept the sign he stood over. The count had kept his hand, page after page, long after the records had let him go. I understood that a man can be removed from every record and still be kept — kept by the things he kept, kept by the count that would not let him go.

I had the notebook, the tally, the marks of the Academy, the first sign. I understood that I was not the first keeper to wonder what the count was counting — and that I would not be the last. But I was the one who had Ferrand's notebook. And I would find out what the count was counting — and what Ferrand had found, at the end, that had made him stop keeping the records of his own life.

I sat with the notebook a long time, in the dark, with the lamp burning low.

And I asked the question the notebook had left me: whether Ferrand had stopped keeping the records of his own life because he had learned something, or because he had given up on learning it — and which of those two I would be, when the count came at last to its end.
