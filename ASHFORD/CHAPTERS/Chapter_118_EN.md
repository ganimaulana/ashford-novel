CHAPTER 118 — THE SEASON RETURNS

The season came again to Ashford.

I had known it would come. I had known it for years — since the first winter, when I had stood at the end of the lane and watched the lamp burn at nine and heard the bell at the corner. The season came every year, from the thirteenth to the twenty-first of November, and it ceased on the twenty-second, and in all those years it had never once failed to come, and never once failed to go.

I had kept the account through four seasons now. I had watched the lamp burn, and the bell come, and the house at the end of the lane keep itself through the season. I had walked the boundary, and the wall, and the passage. I had kept the door closed, and the bell silent, and the houses kept. I had written it all in the red book, year after year, the same entries in the same order, as if the season were a chapter that the town could not stop writing.

The season came again.

And this year I understood it differently.

I cannot say what changed, exactly. It was not the season itself — the season was the same as it had always been, the same dates, the same darkness, the same cold coming down over the lane. It was the understanding that had been growing in me since the tally, since the book of the first keeper, since I had read the sentence that the count was the thing and the keeper was not. I had watched the season four times. This year I watched it knowing that I was not watching a haunting, or a memory, or a thing the town had simply agreed to keep. I was watching the count turn.

The town drew in toward the season the way it always did. The shops closed earlier in the middle of the month, and the lane emptied by dark, and the windows were shuttered against the evenings, and no one spoke of why, because in Ashford the town does not speak of the season; it merely observes it, the way a man observes the turning of a clock without asking what keeps the time. I walked out on the thirteenth with the lamps being lit along the High Street, and the town settling into its evening, and the windows taking light one by one, and I went to the end of the lane and stood where I had stood in every season since the first.

The end house stood in its place, dark, with the rosemary in its pot at the sill. I had stood before it in other winters, in the rain and the frost, and I had never once seen a hand at the rosemary, and never once seen it wilt or die or grow beyond its pot. It was kept the way the house was kept, without a keeper's body.

I waited.

At nine, the lamp came.

It burned in the upper window, steady, at its old height, and then it was carried away along the rooms, the way it had been carried every year, without a servant's voice, without footsteps, without the opening of any door I could hear. I watched it go from window to window — the upper room, the middle room, the narrow window at the turn of the stair — and I did not move until the last light had passed out of sight and the house stood dark again, the way it always stood.

I had watched it for years. I had noted it. I had written it in the red book. I had not understood it.

Now I understood it differently.

The lamp was not a ghost. It was not a haunting. It was a keeping — a keeping that had stood for longer than the town had a name, performed every year by a hand that was not a hand, in a house that was not a house in the way the town understood houses. And the keeping was part of the count.

I could not yet tell what the count was counting. But I knew that the lamp was part of it. The lamp burned at nine. The bell came when the lamp did not. The house kept itself through the season. All of it, part of the count, turning the same way it had turned before the town had a name for the turning.

I stood at the end of the lane, with the object warm against my chest, and I understood that I was watching the count, and that the count was watching itself through me, and I could not tell which of those was the truer thing to say. In other years I had stood here as a witness, noting what the season did. This year I stood here as part of it — the keeper of the account, the one who was in the count, and I understood that the difference was not in the season. It was in me.

---

I went to the common on the night of the fifteenth.

The house was there.

It stood at the edge of the common, where the grass met the lower pasture, its windows dark, its front door open. It had appeared, and disappeared, for as long as anyone could remember; it left only the flattened grass, and the keeper's sign pressed into the earth. I had entered it once, in the first year. I had found the clock stopped at 00:00. I had heard the three knocks from beneath the floor. I had not entered it again, and I had not been certain, all the years between, whether the not-entering was fear or prudence.

I stood at the edge of the common now, in the dark, and I looked at the house. The cold came off the ground in a low mist. The windows gave back nothing. The open door held the dark the way a held breath holds air.

I did not approach it.

I stood at the edge of the common, with the object warm against my chest, and I looked at the house, and I remembered the first year — carrying the object across the grass to the keeper's sign in the earth, feeling it warm against my palm, understanding that the first sign responded to the keeper's sign, understanding that I had walked into the middle of something that had been waiting for me.

The object was warm now.

It warmed at the edge of the common, where the house appeared, and it had not warmed at the common for a long time — not since the first year. I stood in the dark and looked at the house and understood that the house was part of the count. It appeared. It disappeared. It kept something — the first sign, carved into its wall, hidden where only the object could find it. The house was part of the count.

I did not enter it. I had not entered it in three seasons, and I did not enter it now. I stood at the edge of the common and watched it, and the object burned gently against my chest, and I understood that whatever the house was keeping, it was not yet mine to take. It was keeping it for the count, the way the lamp kept the end house, the way I kept the account — each of us holding our small share of a thing none of us could name.

---

I went to the tower on the night of the twentieth.

I had the key. I had always had the key — the keeper's key, to the tower, to the bell. I climbed the stairs in the dark, by the feel of the walls, the way I had climbed them a hundred times, and I stood in the bell chamber.

The bell hung in the dark, silent, the first sign on its rim.

I had not rung it. I had not needed to ring it. The bell was the keeper's bell, and it was kept, and it was not rung — that was the whole of the keeping, that the bell stayed silent, that the count stayed unspoken. I stood in the bell chamber and looked at the bell, and the silence in the chamber was not the absence of sound. It was a sound in itself, the sound of something that had been holding its breath for a very long time.

The first sign was on its rim.

The bell was part of the count.

I did not ring it. I stood in the bell chamber, in the dark, with the key cold in my pocket, and I understood that I was watching the count, and that the count had its own watchmen, and that I was one of them, whether the bell ever called on me or not. There had been nights, in the first winters, when I had come to this chamber and stood close to the bell and listened, half-expecting it to ring of itself, to call the end of the count it had been keeping for so long. It had never rung. And this year I no longer expected it to. I understood that the bell was not waiting for the count to end. It was part of the count, and the count was not over, and I could not say when it would be.

---

The season ended on the twenty-second of November.

The lamp came, and the bell came, and the house kept itself through the season, and on the twenty-second it ceased, the way it had ceased every year, the way it would cease next year and the year after, for as long as the keeping held. I stood at the end of the lane on the morning of the twenty-second, in the grey light, and I looked at the end house, and I understood that the season had passed.

The count had turned.

I walked home, and I sat at my desk, and I opened the red book, and I wrote:

*The season has passed. The lamp burned, and the bell came, and the house kept itself through the season.*

*I watched it differently this year.*

*The lamp is part of the count. The bell is part of the count. The house is part of the count. The object is part of the count. The stones are part of the count.*

*I do not know what the count is counting.*

*But I know that I am part of it.*

*And I know that the count is older than the keeping.*

*I will learn what it is counting.*

*The season has passed.*

*And the count has turned.*

I closed the red book. I sat in the dark.

Four seasons had passed under my keeping, and I had watched each one, and I had written each one, and I had understood each one a little less than the season before. The lamp came, and the bell came, and the house kept itself, and I was the keeper, and I did not know what any of it was for — only that it was, and that I was in it, and that the count had turned without asking my leave, and would turn again.

The winter would come now, and the town would settle, and I would carry the question through it, the way I had carried it every winter since the first.

And when the season came again, I would stand at the end of the lane again, and I would watch the lamp come again, and I would ask the same question.

And I did not know — I still do not know — whether I was asking it as the keeper of the count, or as a man the count was counting.
