CHAPTER 118 — THE NUMBER

I carried the three tallies with me — the tally from the book of the first keeper, the marks from the oldest book of the Academy, and the marks from Ferrand's notebook. They were the same marks, in the same hand, the same count. I had counted them many times. I had written the number in the red book. I had not understood it.

I set the three books on the desk, side by side, and I looked at them. The same marks. The same hand. The same count. Three books, three tallies, one number — likely the number of the thirteen signs that governed the count. I could not tell what the number was counting. But I knew that it was important — and that the bell would ring when the count reached its end at 00:00.

I went to see Ysabel. She was at her house near the river, and I set the three books on her table. "Three tallies," I said. "The book of the first keeper. The oldest book of the Academy. Ferrand's notebook. The same marks. The same hand. The same count."

Ysabel looked at them for a long time. "The first keeper made the tally," she said. "The boundary was set by the first keeper. The Academy book records the boundary as it was first set. And Ferrand kept the same count, fifteen years, without understanding it."

"Yes."

"There is a number," Ysabel said. "A number that has been kept by every keeper, from the first to the present. A number that has not changed in Ferrand's time. A number that the boundary is set to mark."

"Yes."

Ysabel was silent a moment. "Then the number is not a count of years," she said. "A count of years would grow. Ferrand wrote that the count had not changed in his time. A count that does not change is not a count of years."

I stood in her house. "A count that does not change," I said. "A count of what?"

"I do not know," she said. "But if the count does not change, then it is not counting time. It is counting something that is fixed — something that has always been the same number, and will always be the same number."

I looked at the three tallies. A count that did not change. A count of something fixed. I did not know what it was counting. But I knew that it was not counting years.

I went back to the boundary. I walked the line of stones, from the eastern marker to the corner where the wall turned. I counted the stones. The number of stones was not the tally. I stood at the fourth stone, where the hairline mark ran across its face. The stones did not change. The count did not change. The boundary held. I knelt at the fourth stone. I looked at the first sign at its base. The first sign. The mark of the maker. I looked at the hairline mark across the face. Two marks on the same stone. I had watched the hairline mark appear, in the first year. It had appeared, and it had not grown, and I had not known what it meant. I looked at the hairline mark now. It had not grown. It had not changed. It was the same mark it had been in the first year. A mark that did not change.

I stood. I looked at the line of stones. The stones did not change. The count did not change. The boundary held. And the hairline mark did not change. I did not know what that meant. But I understood that the count and the boundary and the stones were all the same thing — a thing that did not change. A thing that was fixed. And I was part of it.

I went home. I sat at my desk. I opened the red book. I wrote: The tally does not change. Ferrand wrote that the count had not changed in his time. The stones do not change. The boundary holds. The hairline mark does not change. The count is not a count of years. It is a count of something fixed — something that has always been the same number, and will always be the same number. I do not know what it is counting. But I know that it is fixed. And I know that I am part of it. The count is fixed. The boundary is fixed. The stones are fixed. And I am part of it. I do not know what the count is counting. But I would learn. The hunt had led me to the number. And the number was leading me somewhere.

But as I set down the pen, I understood that the number was not telling me what the count was counting. It was telling me that the count was not a count of anything that had happened. It was a count of something that was going to happen. The number was fixed because the thing it was counting was not yet complete. And when the count reached its end — when the number was finally reached — the bell would ring at 00:00, and the thirteen signs would be revealed. The number was not a record of the past. It was a promise of the future. And I was part of that promise.
