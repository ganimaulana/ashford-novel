# Chapter 27 — The Man at the Table

The day after Helen's door, the cold held the lane in the old way, and I went about my morning with the quiet that comes after a thing has been said, and the knowing that the saying of it had not ended the thing. I had the name in my book, and the lamp in my mind, and the gate in my memory, and the three of them sat with me at the desk, and I did not take them out to look at them, because I had looked at them enough. The frost had settled on the window, and the fire was low, and the town outside moved at its usual pace, but I felt that I had become part of something that did not move at the town's pace.

The room was cold, and my breath hung in the air as I sat at the desk. I had not slept well. Helen's words remained with me, but I could do nothing with them yet. I closed the book and went out for bread.

I went out at noon for bread, because the loaf was gone, and the cold had made the walk necessary, and the walk was the thing that cleared the head. I took the long way, by the church, because the church was the way I had come to know, and I passed the tower and did not stop, and passed the gate and did not look, and came to Margaret's at the hour when the room was full of the midday men. The heat of the room hit me as I opened the door, and the smell of bread and stew and wet wool filled the space. The windows were steamed, and the talk was low and steady, the sound of men who had known each other for years.

I stood for a moment at the threshold, letting the warmth settle on my face. The room was fuller than usual, the tables crowded with men in work coats, their voices rising and falling like a tide I did not yet know how to read. Margaret was at the counter, her hands moving with the practiced ease of a woman who had served the same faces for decades. She looked up when I came in, and I saw that she had something to say. But before she could speak, I saw the man at the table by the window.

He was there, reading the paper he had been reading the week before. He did not look up when I came in, and I did not look at him, and I stood at the counter and bought the bread, and Margaret wrapped it, and she said: "He is still here. He has been here every day this week, and he has not said what he is waiting for."

Her voice was low, meant only for me. The man at the table did not move. He turned a page of his newspaper with the slow care of a man who had all the time in the world. His coat was dark, and his hat sat on the empty chair beside him, and his hands were steady and still, as if he had been sitting there for hours and intended to sit for many more.

I paid for the bread. As I turned away, the man looked up and gave me a small nod before returning to his paper. I did not return it. I could not tell whether it meant recognition or ordinary courtesy. I went out into the cold, and the bread was warm in my hand, and I walked home the short way, and I did not look back, because looking back is the thing a man does when he is being watched, and I did not want to be a man that looked back.

I went in at my own door, and I set the bread on the table, and I sat with the cold still in my coat, and I thought of the man at the table, and of the nod he had given me, and of the week he had been there, and of the paper he had been reading, and I did not know whether the nod was the nod of a man that had been waiting for me to come, or the nod of a man that had not noticed me at all. The paper he held was folded in a way that hid the front page, and I had not seen what he was reading. I did not know whether he was reading the news, or something else. The bread on the table grew cold as I sat, and I did not eat it.

In the afternoon, I went to the pump, because the water was wanted, and the pump was the place where the town spoke, and I had not heard the town speak since the rate. I stood in the line with my bucket, and the women were at the pump, and they spoke of the season and the price and the bench, and I listened, and I did not ask, and the listening was the asking. The pump handle was cold, and the water was clear, and the women's voices carried in the still air. The frost had thickened on the cobbles, and the shadows were long and thin, and the town seemed to hold its breath.

"The man at Margaret's," said one of them, "the one that comes every year. He has been longer this year than he has been before. He has been here a fortnight, and he has not gone, and he has not said what he is waiting for."

"He is waiting for the snow," said another. "He waits for the snow to clear the roads, and the roads have cleared, and he is still here."

"He is waiting for something else," said the first. "I have seen him at the church and at the town hall and at the end of the lane, and I have seen him looking at the house, and I do not know what he is looking for, but I know he has not found it."

I filled my bucket and lifted it, and the cold bit at my fingers. I walked home through the lane, past the end house with its rosemary, past Helen's door, past the gate that had opened in the night. The man at Margaret's was not the only one watching. I had felt eyes on me more than once, and I did not know whether they were his, or someone else's, or whether the town itself was simply the kind of place that watched its inhabitants as a matter of course.

I took my water and went home, and the words stood in my mind the way the nod had stood, and I put them together with what I had, and the sum came out as sums come out in this town: that Foster had been at the end of the lane, and had been looking at the house, and had been at the church and the town hall, and had been longer than he had been before, and had not gone, and had not said what he was waiting for. I did not know whether the house at the end of the lane was the house he had been looking at, or whether the looking was the looking of a man that had been looking at houses all his life, and I did not know whether the being longer was the being longer of a man that had found something, or the being longer of a man that had not.

I sat at the desk, and I did not take out the book, because I had written enough that week, and the writing of it was the keeping of it, and the keeping of it was the thing I had been doing since I came to the town. I sat with the water on the table, and the bread on the plate, and the cold at the window, and I did not write, and the not-writing was the thing I had not done before. I thought of the man at the table, and of the way he had nodded, and of the women's words. I thought of the house at the end of the lane, and of Helen's door, and of the lamp that burned at nine. I thought of the name Hodge, and the rate books, and the margins that did not match. I thought of the agreement, and the boundary, and the door beneath the mill. And I thought of the man at the table, and I did not know whether he was part of any of those things, or whether he was simply a traveller who had decided to stay longer than usual.

By evening I still had no answer to the nod. It might have meant nothing. That possibility bothered me more than a clear question would have.

The evening came, and the lamp came up at the end of the lane at nine, and I watched it from my window, and I thought of Helen's lamp, and of the man at the table, and of the nod he had given me, and of the week he had been there, and of the day he had been at the end of the lane, and I understood that the town had three things it kept: the lamp in the end house, and the lamp in Helen's window, and the man at Margaret's table, and the three of them were kept by three different hands, and I did not know whether the three hands were one hand, or three hands that did not know each other.

I did not write it down. I let the thought stand, and I let the night stand, and I went to bed with the cold at the pane, and the knowledge that I had seen the man at the table, and that he had seen me, and that the seeing of me was the thing I had been carrying since the morning. I did not know what he had seen, and I did not know what he had made of it, and I did not know whether he had made anything of it at all.

I slept the sleep of a man that has been seen, and does not know what the seeing means. The cold held through the night, and the lane was quiet, and when I woke, the man at the table was still there, in my mind, waiting for me to decide what to do about him. I did not know yet. But I knew that I would not be able to ignore him. The questions he carried were not his alone; they had become mine, and I would have to find the answers, or learn to live with the silence.
