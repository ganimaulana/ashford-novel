# Chapter 35 — Goding’s Farm

The Thursday came with the frost lying hard again, and the lanes white with it, and the breath of the town standing in the air over its own chimneys, and I went out by ten of the morning with the entry copied fair in my book, because the entry is the thing Goding asked for, and a thing asked for is a thing a man brings fair or not at all.

Goding's farm stands out of the town by the north road, at the end of the lane that goes past the mill-yard, and I had not gone that way since the watch round went past the mill-yard corner, because the round goes the other direction of the gate, and my business with the corner has been the round's business and not my own, and the Thursday was my own business, and the two businesses are not the same thing though they go by the same gate.

I came by the mill-yard at the quarter past ten, and the yard was in its winter work, and the gate was shut with the padlock to it, and I went by it as a man goes by a thing he has noted, and did not stop, because the stopping is the thing that makes a thing seen, and I did not want my going to the farm to be a thing seen by the yard, and the going was kept.

The farm stands a half-mile past the yard, on the rising ground where the town's fields begin, and the house is of the stone the country is made of, and the yard of it has the look of a place that has stood two hundred years in the same family's keeping, and the standing is the keeping, and the keeping stood. Goding was at the barn door with a man of his, and the two of them were about a cart that wanted a wheel seen to, and the seeing to it was the morning's work, and I stood at the yard gate till Goding's eye came to me, and the coming of it was the welcome, because a farmer's welcome is the eye and not the hand.

"You're come," said Goding. "Come in to the house, and the entry with you, and the cold out of you. The wheel can wait, and the entry cannot, because the entry has waited these seven years already."

I went in with him to the house, and the house was the inside of such a house, with the fire and the table and the walls that hold their own smoke, and a woman of the house that set bread and cheese on the table without being asked, because the setting is the woman's way of saying a man has business, and the business wants the table, and the table wants the bread, and the bread is set.

Goding sat at the table with his coat still on, because a farmer keeps his coat in his own house in January, and put his hands flat on the table the way a man puts his hands when he is ready for a thing, and said, "The entry."

I opened the book at the place and set it before him. The entry stood there in my fair copy: *Bell sold by agreement of the wardens, Michaelmas quarter, to Mr. Josiah Crane of High Street, ironmonger. Sum received: four pounds ten shillings. Entered by T. Wren, clerk.* And beneath it the note: *Money carried to general account; bell removed from tower inventory.*

Goding read it twice, and the second reading was slower than the first, and the slowness was the thinking, and the thinking stood in his hands flat on the table, and then he said, "This is as I remember it. The selling was in the year nine, and the Wren that was clerk entered it, and the money went to the general account. I was at the market the day the bell came down, because the coming down of it was a thing the town came out for, and I saw it come down, and I saw Crane's cart take it away. That is my own knowledge, and I give it you as my own, because my own is mine to give."

"The cart took it to the shop," I said.

"The cart took it up the High Street," said Goding. "What the shop did with it after is not in my knowledge. But I will say this of the entry, and it is the thing the looking has brought. The entry says the money was carried to the general account. The general account is the account the wardens keep, and the wardens' account is entered in the old books, and the old books are what you asked for at the vestry, and the clerk would not show them, and the clerk was right not to show them, because the showing of them wants the wardens' word, and the word is not given for the asking. But—" He stopped, and took his hands off the table, and put them together, and the putting together was the weighing, and the weighing took the time of a man that weighs. "But I am a warden. And the entry you have brought is an entry of the wardens' own act, entered by the clerk in the wardens' name, and I will not have the wardens' name standing over an entry that cannot be looked at. That is my part of it. So I will give you the word."

"You will show me the old books," I said.

"I will show you the general account for the year nine," said Goding. "That is what the entry points to, and the pointing is the bound of it. The old books are more than the account, and the more is not mine to give, because the more is the parish's and not the warden's, and a warden stands over the parish's goods and does not own them. But the account is the account, and the account will say whether the four pounds ten stood in it in the year nine, and whether it stood in it after, and the whether is what you came for."

He rose from the table and went to the press at the wall, which was the press such houses keep their papers in, and took from it a book that was not the church's book but a book of the farm's, and set it beside my own, and said, "The vestry keeps the parish's books, and I keep my word in my own press till the giving of it. I will go to the vestry on the Saturday, because the Saturday is when the vestry is open for the accounts, and I will give the clerk the word, and the clerk will show you the account for the year nine, and the looking is yours to do. That is Goding's word, and it stands."

I thanked him, and the woman of the house set more bread on the table, because the business was done and the bread is the thing that comes after business, and Goding ate with me, and spoke of the turnips and the frost and the price of the flour, and did not speak again of the bell, because a farmer gives a thing the time it wants and not more, and the time was given, and I went out of the farm with the Saturday before me, and the going of it was the going of a man that has got what he came for, and the getting is the thing, and the thing stood. The price of the flour he mentioned was higher than the last time I had asked; even farmers were feeling the pinch of the season.

---

I came back by the north road, and came by the mill-yard again at noon, and the yard was at its noon, and the gate was shut with the padlock to it, and I went by it again as a man goes by a thing he has noted, and noted it again, because the noting is the keeping, and the keeping was kept.

And coming by the corner of the yard wall, where the footpath goes past to the lane, I saw a thing I had not seen before, or had not seen so plain, which was this: that the stones of the wall at that corner were not all of one age. The stones of the wall were the stones of the country, and the country's stones are grey, and the grey of them is the grey of the weather, and the weather is the thing that makes the grey, and makes it in the years. But two of the stones at the corner were not so grey as the others, and the not-so-grey is the thing a man sees when he has seen the rest, and I had seen the rest these two months, and the two stones stood at the corner with their newer grey, and I stood and looked at them, and understood what I looked at.

I had seen the same thing before. The wall of the garden at the end of the lane, by Marlowe's wall, has a piece of the top stone newer than the rest, and the newness was set there for air to the shut upper rooms, and I had seen it and noted it in the November. And here was the same doing at the mill-yard corner, in the same wall that goes past the same house, and the two newnesses stood in the one line of the wall, and the line was the line that goes from the mill-yard corner to the garden of the house at the end of the lane, and the going of it was the going of the thing I had been asking after.

I did not touch the stones. The touching is the thing that makes a thing seen, and I did not want the thing seen, and I went on by the path to the lane, and came home by the lane, and the lane stood in its cold, and the house stood at the end of it, and the house kept itself, and I went in at my own door and wrote the day down:

*9 Jan. Goding's farm. Goding's word given for the Saturday: the clerk will show the general account of the year nine at the vestry. The bell came down in the year nine and went up the High Street in Crane's cart — Goding's own sight. Coming home by the mill-yard corner, the two stones at the corner of the yard wall newer than the rest, as the top stones of the garden wall by Marlowe's are newer, and the two newnesses stand in the one line of the wall that goes past the house. The wall has been opened in two places for the air of the house, or for the going of something to the house. The Saturday is before me. The stones are behind me. The keeping stands.*

I shut the book, and let the fire burn, and the lamp came at nine, and was carried away along the rooms, and no sound came, and I set it down as the thirty-second reading, and let it stand, and slept with the stones in my head and the Saturday before me, and the two things stood together as the things of this town stand, and the standing was the keeping, and the keeping was kept.
