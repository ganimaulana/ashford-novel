I returned to the chamber the following night.

I did not go through Bellweather House. The trapdoor was there, and I knew the passage was there, but I did not go that way. I went to the common.

The house was there. I had not expected it to be, but it was there, standing at the edge of the grass, its windows dark, its front door open. I crossed the common. I knelt, lifted the mat, opened the trapdoor. I went down into the passage beneath the common.

The keeper's signs marked the way. I did not stop at the nineteenth sign. I did not stop at the door that was not a door. I walked the passage, past the keeper's signs, past the door, to the place where the passage turned. I had not walked past the door before. I had always stopped there. I had always turned back. I walked past it now.

The passage continued beyond the door â€” the passage beneath the common continued, past the door that was not a door, deeper into the earth. I walked. The keeper's signs ended. The first signs began. The circle, the three lines, the dot, carved into the stone at intervals, marking the way. The passage beneath the common and the passage beneath Bellweather House were the same passage. They met here, past the door, in the earth beneath Ashford.

I walked on. The passage ended at a chamber. The same chamber. The first sign on the floor. I stood in the chamber, in the candlelight, and I looked at the first sign on the floor. The place beneath.

I knelt. I took out my object. I had set it here before. I had felt the warmth. I had taken it away. I set it again. It lay in the first sign, as it had before. The stone warmed. I put my hand to the floor, beside the object. The warmth rose through the stone, into my hand.

I knelt there, with the object set in the first sign, and my hand on the warm floor, and I did not take the object away. I stayed. I could not yet tell how long I stayed. The warmth was constant â€” not growing, not fading, the warmth of something deep and patient and waiting. I stayed until I understood.

I had thought the door was the mystery. I had thought the signs were the mystery. I had thought the medallions were the mystery. I had been wrong. The mystery was beneath. The door, the signs, the medallions, the houses, the bell â€” they were not the mystery. They were the keeping. The keeping of the place beneath.

The place beneath was not behind the door. The place beneath was beneath the chamber, beneath the first sign, beneath the object, beneath the houses, beneath the town. And I was the keeper of it.

I took the object from the floor. The warmth faded. I stood. I looked at the first sign on the floor. I understood, at last, what I was keeper of. Not the door. Not the gear. Not the signs. Not the bell. The place beneath. The place that had been kept for longer than the town had a name. And I was the keeper of it.

I walked back along the passage. I passed the door that was not a door. I passed the nineteenth sign. I climbed the ladder. I closed the trapdoor. I crossed the common. The house was still there. I did not look back.

I walked home through the dark streets, and I sat at my desk, and I opened the red book, and I wrote:

The passages are the same. They meet beneath the door, in the earth beneath Ashford. The chamber is beneath both houses. The first sign is on the floor of the chamber. The object fits it. The stone warms.

The place beneath is not behind the door. It is beneath the chamber. I am the keeper of it. I have touched it. I have felt its warmth. I do not know what it is. But I know that it is not a thing to be opened, or closed, or sealed. It is a thing to be kept. I am the keeper of the place beneath.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer, with the agreement, and the map, and my notebook. I did not put the medallions away. I left them on the desk.

And I sat in the dark, and I thought about the place beneath, and the warmth, and the keeping. I had been a keeper for two months. I had been keeping the door closed. I had been keeping the sign. I had been keeping the account. I had not known that the thing I was keeping was the place beneath.

I understood now. The door was not a door. The signs were not a path. The medallions were not the first sign. They were the keeping. And the keeping was the place beneath.

I sat in the dark until the lamp burned down. The medallions lay on the desk. The first sign, twice. I was the keeper of the place beneath—or so I believed. I did not know what it was. I had touched it, or imagined touching it. I would keep it, if there was anything to keep. The keeping might be the thing, or it might be the name I gave to my own indecision. I would need to decide before the bell rang again—and when it rang at 00:00, the count would be complete, and all thirteen signs would be revealed.
