CHAPTER 55 — THE CLERK'S HAND

I did not sleep well Wednesday night. The room was cold, and the fire had gone out before I went to bed, and I had not troubled to light it again, and the cold was part of it, but not all of it. The rest was the paper that was not there. The ninth line. The blank margin. The account that Vale had written and sent and that had never come to the shelf.

I lay awake and listened to the lane, because the lane was the only thing in Alderwick that kept the same hours it had kept before Thursday, and the keeping of the hours was the one thing I could still count on. The pump at the common. The cart on the High Street. The dog somewhere up the lane that barked once and then was quiet. Ordinary sounds. The town continued.

I rose early. I lit the fire. I put the kettle on. I counted the eight again, because the morning was for counting, and the counting was the part of the work that asked nothing of me.

There were eight. The arithmetic held.

The Hartley girl's bowl was gone. I had returned it the evening before, and she had taken it without saying anything, and the lane had been quiet, and the house at the end of it had been shut, and I had walked back through the dark with my hands in my pockets and the cold in my face, and I had understood that the week had not ended and would not end soon.

I sat in the chair. I did not open the notebook. The kettle boiled. I poured the water and sat with the cup in my hands, and I did not drink it, because the holding of it was enough.

The office came again at half past nine.

It was not Calloway. It was a younger man, broad through the chest, with the look of a man who had been told to stand at the end of the lane and wait, and who had waited, and who had been told to come up when the door opened, and who had come up, and who stood now in my room with his hat in his hands and his eyes on the table where the papers lay.

I had seen him before. He had been with Calloway on Saturday. He had been with Calloway again on Monday, standing at the end of the lane with his face turned the other way. I had noticed him then. I noticed him now. He was the sort of man whose presence was a sentence without words, and the sentence was that he had been sent to do a thing that Calloway had decided not to do himself.

"I am the clerk," he said.

It was not an introduction. It was a statement of function, the way a man says "I am the smith" or "I am the carrier." His name was not offered. I did not ask for it.

"The constable asked me to look at the marks," he said.

He spoke carefully, the way a man speaks when he is not sure the words are his own. His eyes moved once around the room — the press, the fire, the chair, the table — and then settled on the papers. He did not sit. He did not look at me. He looked at the papers the way a man looks at a thing he has been permitted to touch but not to own.

"The constable told me you had agreed to inspection."

"I did," I said.

He nodded. He took a step toward the table. He did not reach for the papers. He stood with his hands at his sides and his hat in them, and he looked at the marks on the backs of the eight tied bundles, and then at the leaf Calloway had left, and then at the marks in the margin of the leaf, and then back at the papers again.

"The hand is the same," he said.

I did not answer.

He looked at me then, briefly, the way a man looks to see whether he has been understood. His face was plain. It was not a face that gave anything away. It was the face of a man who had spent twenty years with a pen in his hand and had learned that the safest thing to say was what was required and no more.

"The constable told me the marks were mine," he said. "He told me you had asked which hand wrote them. I wrote them all. Two constables. Forty years. One hand."

"And the ninth line," I said.

He looked at the leaf. He looked at the bottom line, the one with the blank margin. He looked at it for a long time. His face did not change. But his shoulders moved, once, the way a man's shoulders move when he has reached a conclusion he does not wish to have reached.

"That is not my hand," he said.

I heard him out. I let the statement settle, because it was the first new thing anyone had said since Monday, and new things were the things that needed the most room around them.

"The date is late February," I said. "The account is of a visit. You have no mark for it."

"No."

"Did the paper come to the office?"

"No."

"How do you know."

"Because I would have marked it." He said it without heat. It was not a boast. It was a statement of fact, the way a man says "I would have known if the door had opened" or "I would have known if the light had gone out." "The constable asked me the same question on Tuesday. I told him then what I tell you now. The paper did not come. If it had come, it would have been marked. It was not marked. Therefore it did not come."

"And yet the entry is there."

"The entry is there," he said. "The paper is not."

He looked around the room again. His eyes moved to the box in the press, and then to the bundles on the lid, and then to the papers on the table. He did not ask to open the box. He did not ask to see the journals or the letters or the map. His business was the marks, and the marks were on the table, and the table was all he needed.

"The constable told me you would permit comparison," he said.

"Comparison of what."

"The dates on the marks with the dates on the backs. To confirm that they agree. The constable said you had seen the papers. He said you would know whether the dates were as he had written them."

I stood up. I took the eight tied papers from the box lid and set them on the table beside the leaf. I did not hand them to him. I set them down, the way a man sets down a thing he is not yet sure he wants to let go of, and I stood behind my chair.

"Compare," I said.

He came to the table. He did not sit. He bent over the papers, one hand resting lightly on the wood, and he read the marks on the backs, date by date, and he read the dates on the leaf, and he read the marks in the margin of the leaf, and he did it slowly, the way a man does a thing he has done a hundred times and does not wish to hurry now.

The first was June 1803. The second, September 1804. The third, March 1805. The fourth, November 1807. The fifth, January 1811. The sixth, May 1812. The seventh, August 1814. The eighth, February 1816.

Eight dates. Eight marks. Eight lines in the book.

He looked at each one twice. He looked at the ninth line not at all.

When he finished, he straightened. He took a pencil from his pocket — a short, stubby pencil, the kind a clerk keeps for a day's work — and he wrote the eight dates on the clean sheet that Calloway had left, one by one, beside each the subject as the leaf had it, and beside each the mark as it appeared on the back of the paper. He wrote without haste. His hand was the same hand that had written the marks: small, precise, unhurried. The hand of a man who had written the same words forty years and had not grown careless with them.

When he finished, he set the pencil down and folded the sheet and put it in his coat, beside the one Calloway had left.

"The constable has his copy," he said. "I have mine. The book has its lines. The shelf has nothing. The paper from February is not in the book, not on the shelf, and not here. That is what I know."

He looked at me. It was the first time he had looked at me directly, and when he did, I saw that his eyes were grey and very still, and that there was nothing in them that I could read.

"The constable told me to tell you that the office will make inquiry through proper channels," he said. "He told me to say that the paper from February is the one that concerns the office most. He told me to ask whether you have any account of what was said at your door that day."

"He told you to ask that."

"Yes."

"And you have asked it."

"Yes."

I thought about the man at the door in February. I thought about the questions he had asked, in the right order, and Vale's answers, and the silence that had followed, and the account that Vale had written and sent, and the account that had never come, and the account that was now a line in a book with no mark beside it and no paper behind it.

"I have no account," I said. "I was not present."

"The constable told me you might say that."

He said it without disappointment. He said it the way a man says "I expected this" when he has been expecting it and has prepared for it.

"The constable told me to tell you that if any such account comes to light, you are to give it to the office without reference to the other papers. He told me to say that the February inquiry is separate from the eight. That the office's interest in the February paper is not an interest in the eight. That the two matters are distinct."

I heard him out.

"The constable told me to ask whether you have seen the clerk's book since the constable last looked at it."

"The leaf is on the table," I said. "The book is at the office."

"The constable knows where the book is," he said. "He asked me to ask whether anyone else has seen it. Whether anyone else has had it in their hands. Whether anyone else has made marks in it."

I looked at him. I thought about the shelf. I thought about the room. I thought about the two keys. I thought about the clerk's hand, which had written every mark for forty years, and the constable's hand, which had held one of the two keys, and the third hand that had never been named, and the fourth hand that might have existed and might not, and the paper that was not there, and the account that was not there, and the mark that was not there, and the knowledge that the not-there was the only thing that was certain.

"No one else has had it," I said.

He nodded. He did not write it down. He did not ask me to repeat it. He accepted it the way a man accepts a thing he has been told by a person he does not yet know whether to trust.

"The constable told me to tell you one more thing," he said.

I waited.

"He told me to say that the office's question is not your question. He told me to say that you are not required to answer the office's question. He told me to say that the office's question is the office's own, and that you may keep your own."

He paused. Then he added, in the same careful voice:

"He told me to say that he has found it useful, in his own experience, to keep the two apart."

I heard him out. I did not answer. The statement was not a question. It was not an instruction. It was a thing placed on the table between us, the way Calloway had placed the leaf, the way the old constable had placed subjects between us and left them, to be returned to when he chose.

The clerk waited for a moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men in that room had learned to nod at the end of a sentence. He turned. He walked to the door. He opened it. He stood in the doorway for a second, looking out at the lane, and then he went down the step, and I shut the door.

I stood in the room. The fire was low. The kettle was cold. The papers were on the table. The leaf was beside them. The clerk's ruled sheet was folded on top of the leaf. The three bundles were on the box lid. The box was in the press.

I sat down. I took out my notebook. I wrote what I had seen, in order. What I had been told, in order. What I had concluded, in order. I kept them apart, the way he had taught me, because keeping them apart was the only part of the method that was mine.

What I saw: a young man with a plain coat and a stubby pencil. The same hand that wrote the marks. The dates agreed. The ninth line was not his. The clerk said the paper had not come. He said he would have marked it. He said he had been asked by Calloway to say three things: that the February paper was separate, that I was not required to answer the office's question, and that Calloway had found it useful to keep the two apart.

What I was told: the clerk's name was not given. The constable had sent him. The constable's interest in February was distinct from his interest in the eight. The office would make inquiry through proper channels. No one else had seen the book.

What I concluded: nothing yet. The clerk's testimony was consistent with what Calloway had said. The dates agreed. The ninth line remained unexplained. The constable's message was consistent with the arrangement he had proposed the day before. The pressure had increased by one step — a second man, a second sheet, a second request — but the boundary remained.

I closed the notebook.

I carried the box to the table and opened it. I looked at the three bundles on the lid. I looked at the uncertain paper that had no name, lying on top of everything. I thought about the clerk's hand, and the marks it had made, and the blank margin it had left, and the question that the blank margin asked, and the question that the office was asking, and the question that I was asking, and the questions that remained.

The office kept a book. The book kept a mark. The mark kept a promise. The promise had not been kept. The book said the eight papers were in the office's hands. The shelf said they were not. The clerk said the ninth paper had never come. The constable said the February inquiry was separate. The man who had taken the account knew the account was not there. The knowing of it was the not-knowing of it. The not-knowing was the paper that was not there.

I lit the fire. I sat in the chair. I did not open the notebook again. The kettle was cold. The bowl was gone. The box was shut. The book was at the office. The marks were on the papers. The paper that was not there was not there. The office had its leaf. I had its copy. The lane was quiet. The week was not over. For now.
