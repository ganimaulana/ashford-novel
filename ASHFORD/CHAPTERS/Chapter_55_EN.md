# Chapter 55 — The Day-Book

The note from the office came on a Thursday and asked for a Monday. It said the Greyhound fee wanted closing before the Sessions, and that my signature would be wanted beside it, and it was signed with the constable's initials, three letters in a small hard hand that leaned neither way. I had been in the office a dozen times for other people's business and never once for the books themselves. I took my ledger with me that morning, because a line in my own book wanted the date the office gave it, and because I found, on reading the note a second time, that I wanted to see the room where the town's memory was kept more than I wanted to be paid.

The office stood on the square with its steps wet and its door low. Inside, the day-room smelled of sooty coal and linseed. There were two clerks and a collier's boy delivering a warrant, and nobody looked up when I came in, which is the politeness of rooms where the work is copying. By the window stood a wide folio with the pages grown soft at the turning corners, and behind it sat the elder clerk with his wrists inside his cuffs and a strip of clean paper on his shoulder. He was thin, careful, sixty at the least, and his eyes went from your face to whatever you were carrying and back to the page. The younger clerk mended a pen and heard nothing but our words.

I gave my name and said I had come about the Greyhound line.

"You'll want the day-book," the elder clerk said. It was not a question. It was the first thing said to me all week that I did not have to answer, and I noticed that I liked it.

He found the page without hunting, the way a man finds a thing he has not hidden. Under his finger the town's weeks turned over: the turnpike, a lost mare, a poaching charge, a drunken smith bound over and entered twice because at first he had gone off not guilty. Three hands or four kept that book across the year, the same letters differently made, the clerk said without looking up — and the oldest stretch of it, he said, was in the hand of the constable before Calloway. He made his figure for a seven his own way, and you could follow that man's whole month of Tuesdays by the seven alone. I let that pass through me without stopping, because my own line stood under the clerk's finger at last, and I had come to read what the office had made of the month I spent at the Greyhound.

*GREYHOUND, INQUIRY — NOTHING FOUND.*

I looked at that line for a long time, trying not to blame the clerk for the shape of his work. The fee was there. The date was there. That an inquiry had been made and delivered was there, and it was true. The particulars, the clerk told me when I asked if the book was all the office carried of the business, were filed with the report at the back; that was the rule of the place. The minute was for the man who must know what came next, not for the man who must know what had happened; he said it once, plainly, as a wheelwright describes a wheel, without any wish that the wheel were another thing. A day-book you could not read standing was of no use, and a day-book that could not turn a whole year over at a leaf was of no use, and so the town's weight of happenings came to rest in two words, three at the outside, and no one of the three was a lie.

What the two words were not was everything else. Not my *supports.* Not my *not proven.* Not the held interval, nor the two pairs of boots in the one room, nor the manner of knowing, unknown. All the three kinds of knowing I keep by rule apart, the seen and the told and the concluded, stood entered there in one hand and one ink, and that one ink was all the office would go by the next time the town came round that way. Nobody in the room had done anything wrong. The loss came from the shape of the work: a short entry made for the next man, while the fuller account belonged somewhere else.

Then, almost before I had decided to ask for it, I laid my hand on the folio and turned it back, and I asked the clerk to find out for me a small matter of this same April. The last thing the office had sent me to do before the Greyhound. A name: Pell.

The elder clerk looked up from the book then, the first time all morning. He looked at me the length of a word he did not say. "You made that report," he said. "I copied that minute." Then he turned the leaves back to the spring, and his thin finger came down, and there it stood, this April, in the same hand as the Greyhound line: *PELL, INQUIRY — NOTHING FOUND.*

The original of that report was still in my bag. My line had been four words, and they were: *he was not there.* That was a proved negative, drawn out of a booking-book and a memory, the road named, the dates tied to it. The day-book had lost none of the facts: Pell's name was there, April was there, an inquiry was there. It had taken out the shape of the finding, and set my hard little negative down among the turnpike and the lost mare in the same two words that meant the office was done with the business. The two entries stood on the page in the same ink, indistinguishable to a hurried reader. One had been proved. The other had only been closed. I laid the book back straight on its shelf, which I did because it was there to be done, and all the while I was aware that the elder clerk was watching my hands with a patience, a man waiting to see which sort I should turn out, and having seen several sorts.

Calloway came in while I stood at the window with that still in my mind, and the fee-book was in his hand before I had spoken to him. He listened standing. I told him the whole of it in the order it had come to me: the two words, my four words, the shape of the trade, the fair use of a minute. I said I wanted no entry altered. The minute was true of the office, and I would sooner ask a mason to take a stone out of a finished wall than ask a clerk to take a word out of a balanced book. What I wanted to know was a plainer thing: whose hand made the two words into one kind when they covered three kinds altogether: the thing proved absent, the thing searched and missed, and the thing never looked after at all.

"The next man reads the minute," Calloway said. "Not the report." He said it without offence, and he looked at me afterwards like a man handed a fact he was not certain he had sent for. "If you're telling me my books want three sorts of nothing found, sir, I'll not say you're wrong. And I'll say this fair: I don't know how many entries stand here where my own officers' words have done the office's meaning no clear service. Which of them means what you mean when you say *I proved it*, I could not tell you." I said nobody could tell him, that the counting wanted doing book by book, year by year, by somebody with no fee for it. He came and stood by the folio, read the Greyhound line twice, and took up a pen. In the wide blank foot of the page, where the office keeps its short words and a stranger's eye is trained to pass them by, he wrote: *As reported. Not proven.* He did not sign it, turned the page, and went back to the fee, as if the matter were finished; and it was, for the day-book, the only way it ever could be. I stood there and understood, by that small addition which was neither minute nor report, that a man in that trade had been given the one question he could not answer standing, and had set down what he could answer in the small and safe place at the foot of the page, where his clerks would see it every day, as plain, in its place, as anything else in the book.

The fee-line was signed then, the date tied, the Sessions answered for; and then he had given me the reason he had sent for me. The second shelf, at the back of the day-room, held the settled business that had gone back there under his predecessor and had stayed ever since, a shelf of small boxes the office owed nothing to and did not dispute. I had looked at it once the whole visit, and he had seen the look. "I am not offering you a place," he said, and he said it early enough in the morning for it to be plain true. What he offered was the shelf itself. The books were clear on Mondays. A man could sit down at the window and read the office's memory and put the books back. He said it the way you lend a spade. I said I would be there Monday morning, and no more than that. He asked nothing of me beyond Monday.

I will tell you what the elder clerk said, and then not one thing more that passed in the day-room that morning. As I put on my coat, he stood his pen in the ink, and it ran, and he did not go back to his folio, and he did not look at me. "It has not been opened since the old constable's time, that shelf," he said. "Not since I began here. What stands written in those boxes is true as my own day-book. What that is worth to you, I will not say." He spoke no faster than all his other talk. I thanked him. He took up his pen again, and the book was the same after me as before.

The rain began as I crossed the square, a small rain that soaks a man without seeming to begin. I carried no paper out of the office; nobody had offered me any, and I asked for none. But the two words went home with me, and by the time I was in the lane, above the wall where the testimony of the Greyhound had stopped, I saw plainly why the day-book had not shaken me and the little shelf had. The *nothing found* of the day-book would outlive the report filed behind it, and outlive the three hands that wrote it. And behind that door lay years of the same two words, set over matters that nobody had been in a position to ask about again, and no page in the room could tell a searcher which of them had been proved and which had only been put away.

At the house I made three lines in my ledger under the fee. The first was the day-book's line copied in my own hand: *Nothing found.* The second was what Calloway had written small at the foot of the page, set in my ledger now where no clerk would ever see it: *As reported. Not proven.* And the third, under both, was a date: *Monday.*

I read the three lines and thought, beside them, of the box on the shelf at home: the room, the columns, the finding, all shut up together in their proper order. The new lines did not belong in that box, and I did not force them in. The box was for finished work. What these three lines were for was a week of work not begun, so they wanted a clean sheet, and the sheet was there, and the week stood before it with nothing in it that I had not seen with my own eyes. I made the fire up. Monday was four days off. That was enough for one evening.
