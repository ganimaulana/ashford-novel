CHAPTER 133 — THE CALLING

I stayed in the marsh for another season. I had kept the house. I had kept the ring of stones. I had kept the first sign on the floor. I had not moved the stone. I did not know what I was waiting for. The count had called me north. The count had led me to the house. The count had shown me the beginning of the boundary. I had kept it. And I had waited. I did not know whether the count would call me further. I did not know whether I had come to the end of the calling, or whether there was more. But I waited.

On a night in late summer, I felt it. I was at the house, at the first sign on the floor, when I felt it — the warmth. Not the warmth of the medallions. Not the warmth of the summer night. A different warmth — the warmth I had felt at the boundary in Ashford, at the door, at the chamber. The warmth of the medallions. I took out the medallions. They were warm. They had been warm many times. They had warmed at the stones, and at the door, and at the chamber. They had warmed at the first sign on the wall of the common house. They were warm now. I held them up. I did not know why they were warm. I was in the house in the marsh, at the first sign on the floor. The medallions had warmed there before. But they were warmer now. I looked at them. They were warm — warmer than they had been, warmer than they had ever been in the marsh. And I understood. The count was calling. Not me. The medallions. The count was calling the medallions — the second of thirteen signs, perhaps, or the next marker in the count. And I would follow until all thirteen were found.

I stood in the house, in the dark, with the warm medallions in my hand, and I understood that I had not come to the end of the calling. The count had led me to the house. The count had kept me at the house. And now the count was calling the medallions. Calling them somewhere. I did not know where. But I knew that I would follow.

I left the marsh in the morning. I went to the village first. I told the marsh people I was leaving. "The count is calling," I said. "It is calling the medallions. I must follow." The woman looked at me a long moment. "Then you must go," she said. "The keeper follows the calling. That is the keeping." I looked at the house, and the ring of stones, and the marsh. "You will keep the house?" I said. "We will keep it," she said. "We have always kept it. We will keep it until the keeper returns." I stood in the village, in the grey light. "I do not know when I will return," I said. "I do not know whether I will return." "Then we will keep it," she said. "The keeping is the thing. The keeper is not." I nodded. I left the marsh. I walked north, along the causeway, away from the water, and I did not look back. The medallions were warm against my chest. The count was calling. And I would follow.

I walked north for a week. The marsh fell behind me. The land rose. The fields returned, and the lanes, and the small towns. I did not know where I was going. I did not know what the count was calling me to. But the medallions were warm. They had never stopped being warm since I left the marsh. They warmed against my chest, steady, constant. I walked north, and I watched the medallions, and I waited for them to change. On the seventh day, they changed. I was walking through a small town, at midday, when the medallions grew warmer. I stopped. I looked at them. They were warm — warmer than they had been. I looked at the town. It was a small town, like many I had passed. Streets, houses, a market square, a church. And at the end of the town, on a hill, there was a house. I looked at the house. It was large, old, set back from the town, behind a wall of old stone. And the medallions were warm. I stood in the street, in the midday light, and I looked at the house on the hill. The count had called me north. The count had led me through the marsh, and away from the marsh, and to this town. And the medallions were warm. I did not know what the house was. I did not know why the count had brought me here. But I knew that I would go to it. The count was calling. And I would answer.
