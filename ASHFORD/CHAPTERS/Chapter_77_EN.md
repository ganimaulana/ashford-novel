CHAPTER 77 — THE COMMON ROOM

I went to the office on Friday morning, because Thursday had ended with the clerk's words and the constable had sent for us.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open. I had been there ten times before. But I had never been there as one of four men who were to be examined together, and I had never been there as a man who was not answerable to the office at all.

I stepped inside.

The room was not the same. The chairs had been moved back to their usual places. The desk was clear except for a sheet of paper and a pencil. The clerk was there, copying into the ledger. Calloway was standing by the fireplace. Orme was there, sitting in the chair opposite the door.

The three other men were there. The man with the notebook. The older man with the hat. The younger man with the eager look.

Calloway looked up. He set the paper down. He took off his hat and set it on the desk.

"You are four," he said. "The office will give you one set of papers. The papers are statements from witnesses. They concern the same incident. They do not agree. You will prepare one statement for the entry-book. You may write together or separately. The clerk will take what you produce. You will not ask the clerk questions. You will not ask the constable questions. You will do the work."

He looked at the clerk. The clerk opened the ledger. He turned the page. He placed six sheets of paper on the desk.

"Read them," he said. "Write one statement. Sign it. The record will be the office's record."

He closed the ledger. He put on his hat. He went out.

We sat at the desk. The man with the notebook leaned forward. He took the first paper.

The first statement said:

A farmer states that he found his gate open at dawn on the tenth of January. The post was not split. The hinge was not pulled. The gate hung straight. There was a scratch on the lower rail, new and pale, as though something had brushed against it. The farmer says he heard no sound during the night. The farmer says he locked the gate himself at dusk.

The second statement said:

A carter states that he passed through the lane at half-past nine on the evening of the tenth. He saw the gate standing open. He saw no one nearby. He says the wind was strong from the east. He says he did not stop. He says he did not touch the gate.

The third statement said:

A woman states that she was woken by a sound between dusk and midnight. She looked from her window. She saw the gate standing open. She saw no one. She says the sound was like a cart wheel on the road, but there was no cart.

The fourth statement said:

A man states that he left the inn at midnight. He walked down the lane. He saw the gate standing open. He saw no one. He says the night was still. He says the air was cold. He says he would have heard a cart if there had been one.

The fifth statement said:

A child states that he saw a light in the lane at half-past eight. He says it was a lantern. He says it moved toward the gate. He says he did not see who carried it. He says it went out before it reached the gate.

The sixth statement said:

The postman states that he passed through the lane at nine o'clock. He saw the gate standing open. He saw no one. He says his horse was skittish. He says the horse stopped and would not go forward until he spoke to it. He says the gate was open and there was nothing behind it.

The man with the notebook read them twice. He looked at the older man. He looked at the younger man. He looked at me.

"The first two are the ones from before," he said. "The others are new. The woman heard a sound. The man from the inn saw nothing. The child saw a light. The postman's horse stopped."

"The times do not agree," I said. "The farmer locked the gate at dusk. The child saw a light at half-past eight. The carter saw the gate open at half-past nine. The woman heard a sound between dusk and midnight. The postman saw it open at nine. The man from the inn saw it open at midnight. The gate could have been opened at any time between dusk and dawn."

"So the gate was open," the younger man said. "We should say the gate was open."

"We cannot say what happened," I said. "We can only say what each witness said. The clerk will write what we tell him to write."

The older man turned the third statement over. He looked at the back. It was blank.

"The woman says the sound was like a cart wheel," he said. "But there was no cart. The postman says his horse stopped. The child says the light went out before it reached the gate. The farmer says there was a scratch. The carter says nothing about a scratch."

He looked at us.

"The question is what the office wants," he said. "If it wants to know what happened, we must choose. If it wants to know what the witnesses said, we must not choose."

The man with the notebook wrote a heading on a fresh sheet. He wrote:

Statement concerning the gate at the end of the lane, on the night of the tenth of January.

He looked at us.

"What do we put under it," he said.

"I think we should say the gate was open," the younger man said. "That is what the witnesses agree on. The rest is detail."

"The rest is what the office asked for," I said. "If we say only that the gate was open, we leave out everything else. The clerk will write what we say. If we say only that the gate was open, we have chosen. If we say what each witness said, we have not chosen."

The older man turned his hat. He looked at the younger man.

"The boy is right that the gate was open," he said. "But the clerk will write what we say. If we say the gate was open, the record will say the gate was open. If we say the farmer locked it at dusk and the carter saw it open at half-past nine, the record will say both. The office can decide which to credit."

The younger man frowned. He sat back.

The man with the notebook looked at me. He did not say anything. He pushed the paper toward me.

"You write it," he said.

I took the pencil. I wrote:

The gate was found open at dawn. The post was not split. The hinge was not pulled. There was a scratch on the lower rail. The farmer locked the gate at dusk. A carter saw the gate open at half-past nine. A woman heard a sound between dusk and midnight. A man leaving the inn saw the gate open at midnight. A postman saw the gate open at nine. A child saw a light at half-past eight. The postman's horse stopped. The child says the light went out before it reached the gate. The woman says the sound was like a cart wheel. The man from the inn says the night was still. The postman says the gate was open and there was nothing behind it. The carter says nothing about a scratch.

I set the pencil down.

"We write what each witness said," I said. "We do not choose which is true. We do not say what happened. We say what they said. The clerk will write it. The record will be the office's record. The knowing will be ours."

The man with the notebook read it. He nodded. He signed his name at the bottom.

The older man signed. The younger man signed. I signed.

The door opened. Calloway came in. He had his hat in his hands. He looked at the paper. He did not say anything. He looked at us in turn.

"You may go," he said.

We went out.

The man with the notebook fell into step beside me on the lane. The older man went ahead. The younger man came last, looking back at the office door as though he had mislaid something.

"What do you think they will make of it," the man with the notebook said.

"I do not know," I said.

"Do you think we passed."

"I do not know what the test was."

He looked at me. He did not seem surprised. He turned down a side street. The older man was already gone. The younger man looked at me. He looked away.

I set the key in the lock and turned it. The door opened. The room was the same.

The box was in the press. The papers were on the table. The bundle was tied. The ruled sheet was folded. The service file was on the left. The notebook was closed.

I had signed the statement. The statement was in the clerk's hand. The clerk would write it in the entry-book. The record would be the office's record. The knowing would be ours. Whether it was enough, I did not know.
