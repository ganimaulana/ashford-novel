CHAPTER 90 — THE RULES

Dawn came grey and cold. I had not slept well. The bed was narrow, the room was unfamiliar, and the memory of the lamp in the distant window stayed with me as I lay in the dark. I did not know who had been watching, or whether they had been watching me in particular. I only knew that the grounds were not empty when we thought they were.

I rose, washed my face in the basin, and went down to the courtyard.

The candidates were already gathering. Some stood in groups, talking in low voices. Others stood apart, their faces closed. The man with the notebook was there, standing near the well, his notebook open, writing something. The older man with the frayed cuffs was leaning against the wall, his arms crossed. Peale was near the gate, looking at the sky. Ellison was standing by the steps of the main building, his hands in his pockets. Merritt was alone by the cart, not looking at anyone.

The man from the market square was there. He was standing near the fountain, his arms crossed, his head tilted. He did not look at me.

John Weaver was near the dining hall. He nodded when he saw me. I nodded back.

Calloway came out of the main building. He did not speak at first. He walked to the centre of the courtyard and waited until the crowd was silent. He looked at us the way he always looked at us, as though he had seen us before and knew he would see us again.

“You have completed the first task,” he said. “You moved the sacks. Some of you were faster than others. Some of you were stronger. Some of you were more careful. The office observed all of it. The office did not announce a winner, because the task was not designed to produce one.”

He paused.

“The examination you have entered is not a series of separate tasks. It is a single selection process. It will continue over several days, possibly longer. There will be tasks of many kinds—physical, observational, cooperative, decision-based. Some tasks will test you alone. Some will test you in groups. Some will test you against one another.”

He looked at the crowd.

“Not everyone who arrived here will remain until the end. Some of you will be asked to leave the examination. That is not the same as being rejected by the Constable. The Constable has many needs, and not all of them require the same kind of person. Some of you may be assigned to other places, other offices, other duties. Leaving this ground does not mean you have failed. It means the office has determined where you are best placed.”

A murmur passed through the crowd. I saw men exchange glances. Some looked relieved. Some looked uneasy.

Calloway continued.

“The examination will have several stages. At each stage, some candidates will continue, and some will not. The number of candidates will decrease. At the end, a smaller number will remain. Those candidates will be offered positions directly with this office. The others may be offered positions elsewhere, or they may be released from the examination entirely. That decision belongs to the office.”

He paused again.

“There are rules. They are simple. You will obey the instructions of any Constable official. You will not leave the grounds without permission. You will not damage the buildings or equipment. You will not steal from one another. You will not use violence against another candidate. If you do any of these things, you will be removed immediately, and you will not be considered for any Constable position.”

He looked at us.

“You may cooperate with other candidates unless a specific task forbids it. You may choose to work alone if you prefer. You may ask questions, but you are not guaranteed answers. The office will not explain every task in advance. You will be told what you need to know when you need to know it.”

He let that settle.

“The next stage will begin at noon. You will be told where to assemble. You are dismissed until then.”

He turned and walked back into the building.

We stood in silence for a moment. Then the murmuring began.

I walked to the edge of the courtyard and leaned against the wall. I thought about what Calloway had said. The tasks were not ends in themselves; they were part of something larger.

I thought about the sacks. I had completed the task, but I had been slow. I had not been the strongest or the fastest. I did not know whether the office had measured my speed, or my persistence, or my willingness to continue when I was tired. I did not know which of those qualities they valued.

Weaver walked over to me. “What do you think?” he said.

“I think they are not telling us everything,” I said.

“That is always true,” he said. “But I meant, what do you think about the rules? The violence rule. The cooperation rule.”

“They are clear,” I said. “They want us to know what is permitted and what is not.”

“And what is not is fighting,” he said. “That is interesting. If they wanted to test us under pressure, they might allow fighting. But they do not.”

I looked at him. “You think that tells us something.”

“I think it tells us they are not looking for soldiers,” he said. “They are looking for something else.”

He walked away. I stayed where I was.

I saw Ellison standing near the well. He was speaking with a candidate I did not know. I saw Peale walking toward the field, his head down. I saw the man from the market square standing near the gate, his arms crossed, his head tilted. He was not looking at anyone. He was looking at the main building.

I walked over to him.

“You heard the rules,” I said.

He did not look at me. “I heard them.”

“What do you think?”

“I think they are careful,” he said. “They have thought about this. They know what they are looking for.”

“Do you know what they are looking for?”

He turned his head slightly. “They are looking for people who can handle uncertainty. People who can act without knowing everything. People who can cooperate when necessary and compete when necessary. People who can be trusted with something valuable.”

He looked at me. “That is what I think.”

He turned and walked away.

I stood there for a moment, thinking about what he had said. He had not given me a secret. He had given me a description. But it was a description that matched what I had seen—the tasks, the rules, the way the office operated.

I walked back to my room. I sat on the bed and looked out the window. The courtyard was still full of candidates, talking in groups, comparing impressions. Some looked confident. Some looked uncertain. Some looked like they were already calculating their chances.

I did not know whether I would continue. I did not know whether I would be one of the ones who left the grounds early. I did not know whether leaving would mean failure or simply a different assignment.

But I knew that the examination was not about finding the right answer. It was about becoming the kind of person who could be trusted with something valuable.

I waited for noon.

When the bell rang, I went down to the courtyard. The candidates assembled again. Calloway did not come out. The man in the grey coat came out instead. He stood on the steps and looked at us.

“You will assemble at the field,” he said. “The next task will begin there.”

We walked to the field. The posts and ropes were still there, but they had been rearranged. There were markings on the ground, lines and circles that I had not noticed before. The storehouse was closed. The sacks were gone.

I looked at the field. I did not know what the task would be. I did not know whether it would be physical or mental or something else. I only knew that the rules had been given, and that the examination was now truly beginning.

I stood in the field and waited.

I waited for the next instruction.

I waited for whatever would come next.
