# Chapter 47 — The Keeper's Vigil

On the first of May, the old constable died. I went to the house at the end of the lane in the morning, and I found him in his bed, his eyes closed, his face still. He had died in his sleep, as he had said he would.

I sat with him for a long time, and I did not speak. I had known him for only a few months, but he had been the keeper of the agreement for fifty years, and he had passed it on to me. He had trusted me, and I had not let him down. I closed the door of the house behind me, and I walked through the streets of Ashford with the weight of the agreement on my shoulders. I was the keeper now, and I was alone.

I went to Calloway's office, and I told him what had happened. He listened without interrupting, and when I had finished, he did not speak for a long time. "The old constable was a good man," he said at last. "He kept the agreement for fifty years, and he kept it well. You are his heir, and you must carry on his work." I nodded, and I left his office. I went to the house at the end of the lane, and I stood at the gate, and I looked at the door. The house was empty now, and it was my house to keep.

I went home and wrote in my book: *1 May. The old constable is dead. I am the keeper now, and I am alone. I will keep the agreement, and I will keep the town safe.*

The weeks after his death were quiet, the way the weeks after a passing often are. I went about my duties as the keeper, and I kept the agreement, and I watched the boundaries. But I knew that the quiet would not last. On the fifteenth of May, I was called to the old mill by Crowe. He was waiting for me at the door, and he looked at me with the same steady gaze he always had. "You are the keeper now," he said. "And the time has come to decide."

"I have already decided," I said. "I will keep the agreement."

He shook his head. "The agreement is not a document," he said. "It is a choice. And a choice is only as strong as the will of the one who makes it. You have chosen to keep the agreement, but you have not chosen to understand it. You do not know what you are keeping, and you do not know why."

I did not answer, because I knew he was right. I did not know what was inside the space beneath the mill. I did not know why Aurelius Ashcroft had sealed it. I did not know what the power was, or what it could do.

"Come with me," Crowe said. "I will show you what you are keeping."

I followed him down into the space beneath the mill, and he led me to the door that I had sealed. He did not open it. He stood in front of it, and he said, "This is the door. And behind it is the truth. You can keep it sealed, or you can open it. The choice is yours."

I stood at the door, and I felt the weight of the agreement on my shoulders. I was the keeper, and I was the one who would decide. I did not open it. I did not know what was behind it, but I knew that I was not ready to find out. I went back to the house at the end of the lane, and I sat in the back room, where the old constable had sat. I looked at the rosemary on the sill, and the open window, and the cold light of the afternoon.

I was the keeper, and I had kept the agreement. But I had not understood it. I had not understood what I was keeping, or why. I took out my book and wrote: *15 May. I am the keeper of the agreement, but I do not know what the agreement is. I have kept it, but I have not understood it. I must find out what I am keeping. I must find out what is behind the door.*

I shut the book, and I went to the old mill. I stood at the door, and I placed my hand on it. I did not open it. I only stood there, and I waited. I waited for the truth to come to me, because I was not ready to go to it. I went home, and I wrote in my book: *15 May. I have stood at the door, and I have not opened it. I am not ready. But I know that I will be ready when the time comes.*

On the twentieth of May, I was called to a meeting of the Order. Crowe was there, and Eleanor was there, and the others were there. They looked at me the way a group of people look at a man who has made a choice that they did not expect. "You have kept the agreement," Crowe said. "You have kept the door closed. But you have not decided." "I have decided," I said. "I will keep the agreement. I will keep the door closed."

Crowe nodded, and I saw that he was not angry. He was not satisfied, but he was not angry. "The agreement will be kept," he said. "And you will be the one who keeps it." I nodded, and I went out into the lane. I went home and wrote in my book: *20 May. I have kept the agreement, and the Order has accepted my decision. I am the keeper of the agreement, and I will keep it until the time is right.*

The months passed, and I kept the agreement. I did not open the door, and I did not use the power. I watched the boundaries, and I watched the stones, and I watched the Order. I was the keeper, and I was the watcher, and I was the one who would decide when the time was right. I went to Margaret's for bread, and I went to Calloway's office for news, and I went to the house at the end of the lane to sit in the back room and think. I lived in the town, and I kept the agreement, and I waited.

On the first of July, I wrote in my book: *1 Jul. I have kept the agreement for three months. The boundaries are still, and the stones are still, and the space beneath the mill is sealed. I do not know when the time will be right. But I know that I am the one who will decide.* I shut the book, and the lamp came at nine, and was carried away along the rooms, and no sound came. I let it stand. I was the keeper of the agreement, and I would keep it until the time was right. And when the time came, I would decide what to do. But I knew that the time was not yet come. I was still learning, still watching, still waiting. And I would be ready when the time came.

I sat in the silence, and I waited for the next question, because I knew it would come. I had kept the agreement, but the agreement had not kept me. I was bound to it, and it was bound to me. And I knew that one day, I would have to choose whether to keep it or to break it. I did not know what I would choose. But I knew that I would not choose blindly. I would understand what I was keeping, and I would decide with my eyes open.

The lane was quiet that night, and the house at the end of it stood with its rosemary on the sill and its window open to the cold. I was the keeper, and I was the watcher, and I was alone. But I was not afraid. I had been alone before. I had been watching for a long time. And I had learned that the watching was not the end. It was only the beginning.
