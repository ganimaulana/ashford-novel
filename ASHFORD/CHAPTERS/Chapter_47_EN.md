# Chapter 47 — The Understanding

I went to Margaret's on the morning of the twenty-eighth, because a man who has been absent a week owes an explanation, and because I had finally found the words for it. The shop was empty when I entered, and Margaret stood behind the counter with the same cloth she always held, and she looked at me the way a woman looks at a man who has been away too long.

"You've been working for the constable," she said. It was not a question.

"I have," I said. "He asked me to help with some old records—historical boundaries, property lines, things that need a careful eye. It's not much, but it pays, and it keeps me occupied."

She nodded slowly, and I saw that she did not believe all of it, but she did not press. That was the way of Margaret's: she gave you the space to tell the truth, and she gave you the space to keep it.

"I'll still be needing bread," I said.

"The bread will be here," she said.

I took the loaf and paid, and I went home to sit at my desk and write what I had learned.

The winter had settled into Ashford like a thing that had come to stay, and I had come to stay with it. I had been in the town sixty-three days, and I had begun to see the town as a kind of language—a language of patterns and conditions, of triggers and responses, of anomalies that appeared when certain conditions were met.

I sat at my desk and looked at the three cases I had investigated. The room at the Greyhound. The letters on High Street. The clock on Mill Lane. Three cases, three anomalies, and a common thread: they were all responses to conditions.

The room changed when it was listened to. The letters arrived when they were expected. The clock struck when something was present. And the gear—the gear was a key to something I did not yet understand.

I took out my book and wrote:

*28 Jan. I have investigated three cases, and I have learned three things. First: anomalies are not random. They are responses to conditions. Second: the conditions are not always obvious. They must be discovered through observation and trial. Third: the anomalies are not the things themselves—they are the responses to the conditions. The room is not the anomaly. The speech is. The letters are not the anomaly. The expectation is. The clock is not the anomaly. The presence is. I am beginning to understand that the world is not a world of things. It is a world of conditions and responses. And I am beginning to learn how to read them.*

I shut the book and looked out at the lane. The house at the end of it stood with its rosemary on the sill and its glass open to the cold, and the lamp was not yet lit, because the hour was not yet come. I had not solved the three cases, but I had learned something more important: I had learned to see the shape of the questions. And that, I knew, was the beginning of understanding.

I sat with the gear in my hand, and I thought about the pattern. The anomalies were not random. They followed rules. And if they followed rules, they could be understood. I did not yet understand the rules, but I was beginning to see them.

That was enough for now.
