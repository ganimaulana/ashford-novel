CHAPTER 83 — THE DIVISION

I did not hear from the office for two days after I brought the book back. I had expected something—a word, a summons, a sign that the examination was continuing. But nothing came, and I found myself walking the lane each morning, watching the door, waiting for a note that did not arrive.

On Saturday morning I went to the market again. The stalls were full, the lanes were crowded, and the morning was cold enough that the breath of the men and women who passed me rose in white clouds. I bought bread and cheese and a small knife to replace the one I had lost, and I was turning toward the lane when I saw Peale.

He was standing by the chandler’s, the same place he had stood before. He was not writing. He was not looking at anyone. He was looking at the sky, and when he saw me, he nodded once, as though he had been waiting for me.

I nodded back. I did not approach him. I did not know what to say to him, and he did not say anything to me. We stood there for a moment, and then he turned and walked away, and I went home.

On Monday morning a note came. It said only: The office. Nine o’clock. Bring nothing.

I put on my coat and went.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open, and the crowd was already there. There were fewer of them than before—perhaps twenty-five, perhaps fewer. Some of the faces I remembered. Some I did not. The man with the notebook was there, standing apart from the others, his notebook closed but in his hand. The older man with the frayed cuffs was there, leaning against the wall by the pump. Peale was there, standing near the door, and he looked at me when I arrived and did not nod this time.

The door opened. Calloway came out, followed by the man in the grey coat. Calloway looked at the crowd the way he always looked at it, as though he had seen it before and knew he would see it again.

“You have done the first task,” he said. “Some of you succeeded. Some of you did not. The office does not care which. The office cares what you chose to do.”

He paused. No one spoke.

“The second task will be different,” he said. “You will not do it alone. You will do it in groups. The groups have been assigned. The clerk will read the names.”

He stepped back. The clerk stepped forward, carrying the ledger. He opened it and began to read.

I listened. I heard names I recognized—Elton, Thorne, Merritt, Archer, Finch, Salter. I heard names I did not recognize. I heard the man with the notebook’s name called with two men I did not know. I heard the older man’s name called with three men I had never seen before.

I heard my own name called with two men I did not know.

“Leon Ashcroft,” the clerk said, “with Henry Ellison and Thomas Merritt.”

I did not know Henry Ellison. I had never spoken to him. I looked for him in the crowd and saw a man about my age, with a plain coat and a quiet face, standing near the back. He was not looking at me. He was looking at the ground.

Thomas Merritt I knew. He was the man with the grey beard who had sat in the corner of the vestry, the one who had not looked at anyone. He was standing near the door, and when the clerk called his name, he did not move.

I walked over to Ellison. He looked up when I approached, and he did not seem surprised to see me.

“You are Ellison?” I said.

“I am,” he said.

“I am Leon Ashcroft.”

“I know,” he said. “I remember you from the first day. You were quiet.”

“I was,” I said.

He nodded, as though that was the answer he had expected. “Merritt does not speak,” he said. “He may speak later, but he will not speak now. We should not wait for him.”

I looked at Merritt. He was still standing near the door, still not looking at anyone. I did not know whether he was waiting for something or whether he had simply decided not to move.

“What do you think we are supposed to do?” I said.

Ellison shrugged. “The same as before, I suppose. Find something. Bring it back. The office does not tell us what it wants. It only tells us what to do.”

“Do you know where we are supposed to go?”

“No,” he said. “But I know who does.”

He pointed to the man in the grey coat, who was standing on the step with a sheet of paper in his hand. The man in the grey coat was looking at the crowd, and when he saw me looking at him, he did not look away.

“He will tell us,” Ellison said. “When he is ready.”

We waited. The crowd began to move, groups forming and dispersing, men talking in low voices. I watched the man with the notebook, who was standing with his group, his notebook open, writing something as he spoke. I watched the older man with the frayed cuffs, who was standing with his group, his hands in his pockets, not speaking.

I did not know whether I was in a good group or a bad group. I did not know whether Ellison was competent or whether he was merely quiet. I did not know whether Merritt would ever speak.

The man in the grey coat stepped forward.

“Each group will be given a location,” he said. “You will go to that location. You will find something there. You will bring it back to the office. The something will be different for each group. You will not know what it is until you find it.”

He paused.

“You will have until noon.”

He began to walk through the crowd, handing a small piece of paper to each group. When he reached us, he handed the paper to Ellison, who opened it and read it silently.

“The old mill,” Ellison said. “We are to go to the old mill.”

I did not know the old mill. I had heard of it, but I had never seen it. I looked at Merritt, who was still standing near the door, and I wondered whether he knew where it was.

“Do you know where it is?” I said.

“No,” Ellison said. “But I know someone who does.”

He walked to the gate and stopped a man who was passing. The man pointed down the lane and said something I could not hear. Ellison nodded and came back.

“The old mill is on the other side of the river,” he said. “We will need to walk.”

We walked. I did not know whether we were the first group to leave or the last. I did not know whether the others were walking the same direction or different ones. I only knew that Ellison walked quickly and that Merritt followed without speaking.

The old mill was larger than I had expected. It stood on the bank of the river, its wheel broken, its roof sagging, the windows dark and empty. The door was open, and the wind blew through it with a sound like a voice I could not understand.

“We are to find something,” Ellison said. “But we do not know what it is.”

“We will know it when we see it,” I said.

“Will we?”

I did not answer. I did not know.

We went inside. The floor was wooden and rotting, the walls black with damp. The light came through the broken windows in narrow strips, and I walked carefully, watching where I placed my feet.

Merritt did not speak. He did not need to. He walked to the far end of the room and stood still, looking at the wall as though he could see something I could not.

I walked to the centre of the room and looked around. There was nothing here except dust and decay and the sound of the wind. I did not know what we were looking for.

Ellison was examining the door. He was not looking at the door itself. He was looking at the frame, at the wood where the door had been fitted. He ran his hand along it and stopped.

“There is something here,” he said.

I walked over. He pointed to a small mark on the wood, a line and a circle, cut into the surface as though someone had pressed a tool into it and left it there.

“It is a sign,” he said. “I have seen it before.”

“Where?”

“In the office,” he said. “On the ledger. The clerk uses it.”

I looked at the mark. It was small and simple, a line with a circle at the top, the kind of mark a man might make without thinking. I did not know whether it meant anything or whether it was only a scratch.

Merritt walked over. He looked at the mark, and then he looked at me, and then he walked back to the far end of the room.

“He knows something,” Ellison said.

“Does he?”

“I think so. But he will not tell us.”

We searched the room for an hour. We found nothing. The floor was rotten, the walls were damp, the wind was cold. I had begun to think that the mark was the only thing we would find when Merritt spoke.

“It is under the floor,” he said.

His voice was rough, as though he had not used it in days. I looked at him, and he looked at me, and then he pointed to the far end of the room, where the floorboards were loose.

I walked over and knelt. The boards were not nailed down. They were loose, as though someone had lifted them recently. I lifted one, and then another, and then I saw it.

A small box, dark and smooth, tied with a leather cord. It was not large. It was not heavy. It was simply there, waiting to be found.

I lifted it out. The box was warm, as though it had been sitting in the sun, even though the room was cold. I untied the cord and opened the lid.

Inside was a single sheet of paper. It was folded, and it had writing on it, the same brown ink I had seen before, the same hand I had seen on the book in the garden.

I read it.

_You are not the first. You will not be the last. The keeping is the thing._

Ellison read it over my shoulder. He said nothing.

Merritt said nothing.

I folded the paper and put it back in the box. I tied the cord and stood up.

“We should go back,” I said.

We walked back in silence. I did not know what the words meant. I did not know whether they were meant for me or for anyone who found the box. I only knew that they had been written by someone who expected them to be found.

We reached the office just before noon. The man in the grey coat was standing on the step, and he looked at the box in my hand and did not seem surprised by anything he saw.

“You found it,” he said.

“We found it,” I said.

He took the box. He opened it. He read the paper, and then he folded it and put it back, and he wrote something in the small book he carried.

“You may go,” he said.

We went. Ellison walked ahead of me, his hands in his pockets, his face unreadable. Merritt walked behind me, his steps slow, his breath heavy.

I did not know whether we had passed or failed. I did not know whether the box was the right thing or the wrong thing. I only knew that the words were written in the same hand as the words in the garden, and that the voice behind them was someone who had been waiting for someone to find them.

I walked home and sat in the chair by the window. I did not know what the keeping was, or whether I was meant to be part of it. I only knew that the examination was no longer about finding things.

It was about being found.
