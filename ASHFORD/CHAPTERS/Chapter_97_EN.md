CHAPTER 97 — THE HUNT

The note came before dawn. I had not slept well. The lock and the symbol and the clerk's face stayed with me through the dark, turning over like a stone I could not set down. When I opened the door, the note was already on the floor. It said only: The field. Half past six. Bring nothing.

I went down. The field was empty, the grass wet with dew. The other candidates were already gathering. I counted them—nineteen others, including myself. Twenty of us remained. The division had held; I saw Bishop near the back, Ford beside him, Pearce standing apart. I saw Weaver with Merritt, their heads together, speaking low. I saw Hunter near the gate, alone, his hands in his pockets.

Calloway came out of the main building. He did not speak at first. He walked to the centre of the field and waited until we were silent.

"This is the fourth round," he said. "You have been divided into groups. Now you will be tested together. There is a man in the eastern grounds. He is carrying a sealed packet. He will remain within the boundaries of the grounds. He will move. He will not leave the grounds. He will not hide in buildings. He will not stop for more than a few minutes at a time. All groups will pursue the same man. The first four groups to return the packet to this office will be recorded as having passed. The last group will be recorded as not passing."

He paused.

"You may work within your groups. You may not use violence against another candidate. You may not damage the grounds. You may not obstruct another candidate physically. You may not take a packet from another candidate. If you find the packet, you will bring it directly to this office. That is all."

He stepped back. The clerk stepped forward and handed each group a small map of the eastern grounds—a section of the compound we had not yet seen, with paths, walls, open areas, and a stream. I took the map for our group.

Bishop, Ford, and Pearce gathered around me. I spread the map on the ground. The eastern grounds were larger than I had expected—a patchwork of open fields, narrow paths, a line of trees, and a stream that cut across the middle. There was no marked start point, no indication of where the man might be.

"He is moving," Ford said. "We do not know where he started."

"We do not know how fast he moves," Bishop said.

Pearce looked at the map. "We could split up. Cover more ground."

"We could," I said. "But if one of us finds him, we need to be able to reach the others. And we need to be able to bring the packet back quickly."

"Then we stay together until we have a direction," Ford said. "But we cannot afford to be slow. Other groups are already moving."

I looked up. Several groups were already heading into the eastern grounds at a run. Hunter was among them, his group moving quickly, his steps even. Weaver and Merritt were not far behind.

"We will not be the fastest," I said. "But we can be the most careful."

"Careful may not win this," Pearce said.

"Careful may," Bishop said. "Let us see the ground first."

We began to move. The eastern grounds were quiet, the paths empty. The sun was rising, but the light was still grey. I kept the map open, matching the landmarks to what I saw—a gate, a wall, a line of trees. We walked quickly but not at a run. I did not want to exhaust myself before I knew where we were going.

We heard footsteps ahead. I looked up and saw two candidates from another group—a man I did not know and another I had barely noticed. They were running, their eyes fixed on something beyond the next wall. They did not look at us.

"They are chasing something," Bishop said.

"Or they think they are," I said.

We followed. The path turned, and I saw the man in the distance—a figure in a grey coat, moving quickly between the trees. He was not running, but he was not walking slowly. He moved with purpose, his head down, his steps even.

The two candidates were gaining on him. I watched them close the distance. Then the man in the grey coat turned sharply, cut between two walls, and disappeared. The candidates slowed, hesitated, and chose the left passage.

"Left," Ford said, already moving.

"Wait," I said. But they were already going. Bishop followed Ford. Pearce looked at me, then followed the others. I had no choice but to follow.

We ran through the left passage. It was narrow, the walls close on both sides. My breath came short. The passage opened into a small yard, empty. No grey coat. No sign of the man. The two candidates from the other group were standing in the centre, looking around.

"He is not here," Bishop said.

"He was not heading here," I said. "He turned. He wanted them to follow."

"Then where?" Ford asked.

I looked back toward the junction. The right passage led deeper into the grounds, toward the stream. The left passage had been a dead end. The man had known it. He had used it to send the others the wrong way.

"We go back," I said. "Take the right passage."

We turned. My legs already ached from the false run. We had lost time—not much, but enough. When we reached the junction again, the two candidates from the other group were just realizing their mistake. They looked at us, then at the right passage, and ran.

We followed. The right passage opened into a wider path, lined with low walls. I could see the stream ahead, glinting in the growing light. The grey coat was nowhere in sight.

"He is fast," Pearce said.

"He is not fast," I said. "He knows where he is going. We do not."

I stopped. My legs burned. I leaned against the wall, catching my breath. Bishop and Ford waited. Pearce stood apart, looking down the path.

"We cannot keep running after him," I said. "He will always be ahead."

"Then what?" Ford said.

I looked at the map. The stream ran through the middle of the grounds, north to south. The paths formed a grid. The man had been moving north, then east, then south. If he was circling—if the pattern held—he would come back toward the stream from the east.

"If he keeps this pattern," I said, "he will cross the stream from the east side within the next few minutes."

"That is a guess," Pearce said.

"It is a guess," I admitted. "But we have already lost time following the wrong way. We cannot afford to lose more."

Ford looked at the map. "There is a bridge here." He pointed. "If we cut across the open field, we can reach the bridge before he does. We wait there."

"And if he does not come?" Bishop asked.

"Then we have lost more time," I said. "But if we keep chasing, we will lose more time still."

We decided. We cut across the open field. The ground was uneven, the grass wet and slick. My legs burned worse than before. The false run had cost me. Bishop and Ford moved easily; Pearce kept up. I was the slowest. I stumbled once on a rut in the ground and went down on one knee. Ford reached back and pulled me up.

"Can you keep going?" he asked.

"Yes."

But my pace was slower. They had to wait for me twice more before we reached the treeline near the stream.

We stopped. I leaned against a tree, breathing hard. The stream was ahead, shallow, running over stones. The bridge was a narrow wooden crossing, just wide enough for two men.

We waited. A minute passed. Then two. I heard footsteps on the path behind us. Another group—Weaver and Merritt, with two others. They saw us and slowed.

"You are not chasing," Weaver said.

"We are waiting," I said.

"For what?"

"For him to come to us."

Weaver looked at the stream, then at the bridge. "You think he will cross here?"

"I think it is possible."

He considered for a moment. "We will go further south. There is another crossing. If he goes that way, we will catch him before you do."

They moved on. I watched them go. Merritt glanced back at me once, but said nothing.

We waited. The sun rose higher. My legs ached. I began to doubt. Perhaps the pattern had changed. Perhaps the man had already crossed the stream while we were stumbling across the field. Perhaps we had lost the hunt entirely.

Then I saw him. The grey coat appeared on the far side of the stream, moving steadily, his head down. He was heading toward the bridge.

I stepped out of the treeline. Bishop and Ford flanked me. Pearce stayed back, watching the path behind us.

The man in the grey coat saw us. He did not stop. He did not turn. He walked straight toward the bridge.

"We need the packet," I said.

He reached the bridge. He stopped. He looked at me, then at Bishop and Ford. He reached into his coat and pulled out a sealed packet. He held it out.

I took it. He turned and walked back the way he had come, disappearing into the trees.

I stood still for a moment, the packet in my hand. It was sealed. I did not open it.

"Go," Bishop said. "Now."

We turned and ran back toward the office. The packet was in my hand. My legs burned. The open field stretched ahead, uneven and slick. I could not run fast. Bishop and Ford ran beside me, matching my pace. Pearce was ahead, already moving.

Then I heard footsteps behind us. Not one group—two. I looked back. Weaver's group was coming from the south, having found nothing at the other crossing. And behind them, another group I did not recognize, running hard.

"They are coming," Ford said.

"Then we do not stop," Bishop said.

I ran. My legs screamed. The packet was heavy in my hand. The office was at the far end of the grounds, past the open field, past the courtyard. I could see the main building ahead. I could see the clerk standing near the door.

Weaver's group was gaining. I could hear their breathing. They were faster than me. Faster than all of us. But they were behind. The distance was small, but it was enough.

We reached the courtyard. I walked the last few steps. The clerk was standing near the main building, his ledger open, a pencil in his hand. I handed him the packet. He took it, wrote something in the ledger.

Behind me, Weaver's group crossed the courtyard. Merritt was breathing hard. Weaver looked at me, then at the clerk, then at the packet disappearing into the ledger.

Weaver handed nothing. He had not found the man.

The clerk looked at him. "You did not return the packet."

"No," Weaver said.

The clerk wrote something else. Weaver turned and walked away. Merritt followed.

I stood in the courtyard, my legs shaking. Bishop and Ford were beside me. Pearce was leaning against the wall, his hands on his knees.

The group that had been behind Weaver arrived a moment later. They also had no packet. The clerk recorded them. They walked away.

Then another group arrived. They had the packet. The clerk took it, wrote in the ledger. They walked past me without looking.

I counted. Two groups had returned the packet. Weaver's had not. The group behind Weaver had not. That meant at least two more groups still had not returned.

I looked toward the eastern grounds. I did not see Hunter. I did not see his group. I did not know whether they had found the man, or whether they were still searching, or whether they had chosen not to chase at all.

I walked back to my room. The sun was high now, the light strong. I sat on the bed and looked at my hands. They were shaking. Not from cold. From the run. From the false turn. From the stumble in the field.

We had not been the fastest. We had been lucky—the man had come back toward the stream. If he had crossed elsewhere, if the pattern had changed, we would have been standing at the bridge while another group took the packet.

And I had cost us time. The false run. The stumble. Twice they had waited for me. If I had been faster, we would have reached the bridge sooner. If I had not fallen, we might not have needed to wait at all.

I thought about Weaver. He had chosen the southern crossing. It had been a reasonable choice. He had not been wrong to go south. He had simply been wrong about where the man would come.

I thought about the group that had followed us back. They had not tried to take the packet. They had only run. That was allowed. But they had been close. If the field had been longer, if my legs had given out one more time, they would have reached the office before me.

The clerk's face had not changed when I handed him the packet. He had written it down as though he had been waiting for someone to bring it.

I looked out the window. The courtyard was empty. The eastern grounds were quiet. Somewhere out there, the man in the grey coat was still walking. The hunt was not over for everyone.

I sat in the quiet and listened for the bell.
