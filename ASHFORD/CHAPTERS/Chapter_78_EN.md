CHAPTER 78 — THE DISTURBANCE

I went to the office on Monday morning, because Friday had ended with the dismissal and the constable had sent for us.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open. I had been there eleven times before. But I had never been there as one of four men who were to sit a second examination together, and I had never been there as a man who was not answerable to the office at all.

I stepped inside.

The room was not the same. The chairs had been moved back to their usual places. The desk was clear except for a sheet of paper and a pencil. The clerk was there, copying into the ledger. Calloway was standing by the fireplace. Orme was there, sitting in the chair opposite the door.

The three other men were there. The man with the notebook. The older man with the hat. The younger man with the eager look.

Calloway looked up. He set the paper down. He took off his hat and set it on the desk.

"You have done one task together," he said.

"We have," I said.

"There is a room upstairs," he said. "It was left this morning as it stands now. I will not tell you who left it. I will not tell you why. You will go up. You will look. You will speak among yourselves. You will decide what you do."

He turned his hat in his hands.

"You may disturb one thing," he said. "One. Not two. You will agree among yourselves which it is, or you will disturb nothing at all. When the quarter hour is done you will come down, and the clerk will write what you did."

"And what we saw," the man with the notebook said.

"What you did," Calloway said. "The office has no method for a room of that kind. It has never had one. That is why it is being put to four men and not to one."

He looked at the clerk. The clerk closed the ledger. He put on his hat. He went out.

We went. The clerk led us through the door, down the passage, up the stairs. The man with the notebook walked beside me. The older man went ahead. The younger man came last, his boots loud on the wood.

The clerk opened the door at the top of the stairs. He stood aside. He did not come in.

"The quarter hour begins," he said. "I will be in the passage."

He closed the door behind us.

It was the same room as before. The walls were plain. The floor was wooden. The window was high. The light came through it in a rectangle on the floor. There was a long table in the centre. There were chairs around it.

But the room was not the same.

The table had been drawn out from the wall. A chair lay on its side near the door. The window was open a hand's width. There was dust on the floor near the fallen chair, and in the dust there was the print of a boot. There was a mark on the wall below the window, low down, a smear the length of a finger.

On the table were six objects.

The first was a key. It was iron, the bow shaped like a plain ring. The bit was worn. The shaft was straight. It lay with the bow toward the edge of the table.

The second was a folded paper. It was small, the fold worn soft. The outside was blank. It lay beneath the key.

The third was a watch. It was silver, the case scratched. The crystal was cracked. The hands were at half-past ten. It did not tick.

The fourth was a brass disc. It was the size of a coin. It had a mark upon it — a circle with three short lines beneath it and a dot at the centre. It lay beside the watch.

The fifth was a stone. It was rough, the colour of wet gravel. It had a circle cut into one face, and inside the circle were three short lines, and at the centre of the lines a dot. The cut was shallow.

The sixth was a letter. The paper was thin. The ink was brown. The address was in a hand I did not recognise. The seal was unbroken.

The younger man went to the table first. He looked at the key. He looked at the paper beneath it.

"There is writing under the key," he said. "That is the one thing. We lift the key. We read the paper. We put the key back."

"You cannot put it back," the older man said. "Not as it lay."

"It is a key on a table."

"It is a key on a table in a particular way," the older man said. "Once it is moved, nobody in this room can say how it lay. Not the clerk. Not the constable. Not us."

The man with the notebook had not sat down. He was standing over the dust by the fallen chair.

"There is a print here," he said.

I went and looked. The print was near the chair. It was light. The sole was smooth, without nails and without a pattern.

I looked at the boots in the room. The older man's were thick, the welts heavy. The younger man's were scuffed at the toe and nailed. The man with the notebook wore shoes with a pattern cut into the sole.

"The print is not one of ours," I said.

"Are you certain of that," the man with the notebook said.

"I am certain of the soles," I said. "Yours has a pattern. His are nailed. His are heavy. That one is smooth. What follows from it I cannot say."

The older man knelt. He looked at the print, and at his own boot, and at the print again. He stood up.

"Not mine," he said.

The younger man looked at his own boot. He sat down on the edge of the table, and then stood off it again quickly.

"Not mine," he said.

The man with the notebook turned up the sole of his shoe and looked at it, and looked at the print, and wrote something down.

"Then a fifth man has been in this room," he said.

"Or a man of the office set the room out and walked back through his own door," I said. "I cannot say which."

"The window is open," the younger man said.

I looked at the window. It was high in the wall. It opened inward. The dust on the sill was gone from a part of it and not from the rest.

"The sill has been disturbed," I said.

"From outside," the younger man said.

"I cannot say from which side," I said. "I can say the dust is gone from part of it."

The man with the notebook wrote. He looked up.

"Then we have three things and one choice," he said. "The paper under the key. The sill. And the print in the dust."

"The print is not a choice," the older man said. "We can look at the print without moving it."

"We can look at it today," the man with the notebook said. "It is dust on a floor in a room with a door that four men have walked through and will walk through again. It will not be there tomorrow. The key will be there tomorrow. The paper will be there tomorrow. The letter will be there tomorrow."

That was the first thing anybody had said in the room that I had not thought of myself.

"Then we spend nothing on the paper," he said, "and we spend the one thing on the sill, because the sill and the print are the only things here that point out of this room, and they are the only things that will not keep."

"The paper may say who set the room out," the younger man said.

"It may say nothing," the older man said.

"Then we would know it says nothing."

"We would know that, and we would not know how the key lay," the older man said. "I have been forty years about work of this kind and I have never once been sorry for what I left alone."

They looked at me.

I had meant to say that we should disturb nothing, and write down what could be seen, and let the office do what it liked with the room afterwards. That was still what I would have chosen if the choosing had been mine. But the man with the notebook was right that dust does not keep, and I could not answer it.

"The sill," I said. "I would rather have taken nothing. But he is right that the dust will go and the key will not."

"Three," the man with the notebook said.

The older man turned his hat. He looked at the window for a while.

"The sill, then," he said. "Not the paper."

"There is a difficulty," I said. "The window is above a man's head. To reach it, somebody must stand on a chair."

"There is a chair on the floor," the younger man said.

"Not that one," the older man said.

"Then one of the chairs from the table," the man with the notebook said. "They were set here for us to sit on. A chair we were given to use is not a thing in the room."

"That is an argument," the older man said. "It is not a certainty. Say it in the record either way."

"I will say it in the record," the man with the notebook said.

He wrote it down. Then he took a chair from the table and set it below the window and stood on it, and the older man held the back of it, and the younger man watched, and I watched the floor to see that none of us walked through the dust.

The man with the notebook looked at the sill for a long time. He did not touch the window.

"The dust is gone from a hand's width of the outer sill," he said. "Not from the inner. There is a green smear on the outer frame, low, the width of two fingers. Moss, or the wall. That is all I can see."

He came down. He put the chair back where it had been.

"And the window stays open," he said.

"It stays open," the older man said.

We wrote. There were four papers and we did not write one between us, because the clerk had asked what we did, and each of us had done a different part of it. I wrote what I had seen, and what we had agreed, and what I had wanted instead. I wrote that the choice had been the sill, and that I had not been the one to reason it out.

The door opened. The clerk was in the passage.

"The quarter hour is done," he said.

We came out. We went down the stairs. We went through the passage. We went into the room. Calloway was there. Orme was there. The clerk sat in his chair and drew the ledger toward him.

"What did you do," Calloway said.

The man with the notebook told him. He told it plainly, in order, and he did not leave out the argument about the chair, and he did not leave out that we had chosen the sill over the paper and why. The older man added that he had held the chair. The younger man said that he had wanted the paper and had been overruled and that he still thought the paper was the better choice. Nobody contradicted him.

Calloway did not say whether it was so.

The clerk wrote:

Four men entered the upper room and found it disturbed. A chair overturned near the door. A window open a hand's width. A boot print in dust, smooth-soled, matching none of the four. Dust absent from part of the outer sill. Table drawn out from the wall. Six objects upon it, lying in three pairs. The men agreed to disturb one thing and disturbed one thing: a chair from the table was moved to the window and returned. The outer sill was examined and not touched. The folded paper beneath the key was not read. The overturned chair was not righted. The window was not closed. One man dissented as to the choice and is so recorded.

He set the pencil down. He turned the page. He looked at us in turn.

"Sign," he said.

The man with the notebook signed. The older man signed. The younger man signed. I signed.

Calloway took the paper. He read it. He set it on the desk. He looked at the four of us for longer than he needed to.

"The record is made," he said. "There will be a third task. The constable will send for you."

He put on his hat. He walked to the door. He opened it. He looked out at the lane. He went out.

The clerk turned back to the ledger. Orme stood up and put on his hat and looked at us, and went out without saying anything at all.

We went out.

The man with the notebook fell into step beside me on the lane. The older man went ahead. The younger man came last, looking back at the office door as though he had mislaid something.

"You would have taken nothing," he said.

"I would," I said.

"Do you think we were wrong."

"I think the dust would have gone," I said. "That was your reason and it was better than mine. I do not know whether it was right."

"We will not know what the paper said."

"No," I said.

He looked at me. He did not seem surprised. He turned down a side street. The older man was already gone. The younger man looked at me, and looked away, and went on up the lane.

I set the key in the lock and turned it. The door opened. The room was the same.

The box was in the press. The papers were on the table. The bundle was tied. The ruled sheet was folded. The service file was on the left. The notebook was closed.

I did not know whether the print had been left for us to find or left by a man who did not know we were coming. I did not know whether the room had been set out that morning or the night before. I did not know whether Calloway had wanted to see what we would touch, or what we would refuse to touch, or only which of us would say so out loud.

But we had been given one thing and we had spent it, and there was a folded paper under a key in a room above the office that none of us had read.

Whether that was the right way to have spent it, I did not know.
