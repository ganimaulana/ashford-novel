# Chapter 25 — The Rate and the Name

The Monday brought the town to its winter business. The poor-rate was asked at the vestry, because the vestry is the room the parish does its sums in. The sums are done in December, because the winter makes the poor, and the making of them wants a paper. I had heard it at the pump and at Margaret's and at the door of Crane's. I knew it would come. I did not know it would come that day.

It came in the afternoon.

Daniel came to my door a little after three, with his hat in his hand. He stood at the door the length of a breath before he spoke, which is not his way.

"There is a meeting at the vestry this afternoon," he said. "The rate. I have been at the Academy window, and I have seen them go in. One of them is not of the town."

"Who."

"The man from the inn. The man that came in with the season. He has been at the inn a fortnight, and he has been seen at the church and at the town hall and at the bench. He went in with the churchwardens, and he went in as a man who has the right of it. The town went in after him. I thought you would want the telling of it."

I did want it. Daniel stood by the fire and gave me the rest: the clerk went in with his book, the constable with the list, the two farmers from the north end, Michael with his hat off, and the verger last—because the verger keeps the door as well as the tower. The stranger sat where the wardens sit. He was spoken to as a man spoken to at the table, not as a man shown the room.

"The rate is the town's own business," said Daniel, "and the town has let a stranger sit at it. I do not know why. I thought you would want to know, because you keep the roll, and the roll is the other paper of the town."

I thanked him. He went away into the afternoon. I stood at the window with the knowledge of it.

The stranger had appeared wherever parish business was being discussed. He had been seen at the church and the town hall, had asked about the watch, and now sat with the wardens during the rate. His presence was becoming a fact before I understood its purpose. The town had let it come because the town wanted what it brought: the asking of the rate, and the rate was the poor, and the poor were the winter, and the winter was the thing the town could not keep.

I thought about the man from the inn. He had been in Alderwick for a fortnight, but I had not seen him at Margaret's or at the pump. He kept to himself, asked questions, wrote names. The landlord at the inn had said he paid his bill on time and asked for nothing but a quiet room. That made him unusual in a town where most strangers were either watched or ignored.

---

The evening brought the telling of the meeting. A meeting is told in a town the way a thing is told. The telling came to my door at the hour of the lamp, in the person of the verger. He came from the church by the road he always comes. He stood at my door with his lantern low and gave me the meeting as he had kept it.

"The rate is asked," he said, "and it is asked at the figure the bench named. The town has said yes. The saying of yes was the thing that was done, and the doing of it was short. Two spoke against it. Their speaking was heard, and the hearing of it was the end of it. The rate stands at the figure. The paper will be made of it before the year is out."

"The bench named the figure," I said.

"The bench named it," said the verger. "The churchwardens carried it. The stranger sat at the table where the wardens sit and spoke the figure as a man speaks what he has come to speak. The town said yes, because the winter is here, and the poor are here, and the figure was the figure that was wanted. That is the meeting. I have told it you because you keep the roll, and the roll is the paper the watch is kept in, and the watch is the other asking of the season. The stranger did not smile. He did not look at anyone. He spoke the figure once, and then he sat back and waited. The wardens nodded, and the meeting went on as if he had always been there."

"Was Hobbs named," I said.

The verger stood the length of a breath. His lantern stood low with him.

"Hobbs was named," he said. "He was named at the meeting because his name is on the poor list, and the poor list is the list the rate is asked for. The keeper said he has not come in. There was more said, and I will tell you the more, because you have been at the poor-house asking. The keeper said the man went out on the night of the bell, and has not come in since. His bed has stood cold. His place at the window empty. The town has noted it. The noting is in the vestry book, and the noting is all the town will do of it. A man who goes out of the poor-house is the poor-house's. The poor-house has not asked for him. The town will not ask where the poor-house does not ask."

"Who said he went out on the night of the bell," I said.

"The keeper said it," said the verger. "The keeper of the house said it at the meeting. The saying of it was kept in the book. I stood at the door, and I heard it. I tell it you because you asked at the house, and because the night was the night of the bell, and you know what the night was, and the town does not know what the night was. That is the difference of the two of us. I keep it so."

He went away with the lantern. I stood at the door with the night coming down.

The lamp came up at the end of the lane at the corner hour of nine. Steady. Its old height. I watched it.

I watched the lamp for a long moment, thinking about the rate and Hobbs and the stranger. They were three separate facts, but they had all come to the town in the same season. I did not know whether that meant anything. I knew only that I did not have enough to see the shape of it.

The town had done two ordinary things that afternoon: it had set the rate and recorded Hobbs's absence. The same book contained both entries. The man was gone from the house where the poor are kept. He had gone on the night of the bell. The town had noted it. The noting was the end of the town's part.

I went in. I shut the door. I stood in the dark a moment, letting the cold settle on my coat. Then I took the book and wrote the day down. The pages were cold, and the ink was thick, and I wrote slowly, as if the weight of the words might pull them out of the paper.

*The rate is asked at the figure the bench named. The town has said yes. Hobbs is named at the meeting: gone out on the night of the bell, not come in; bed cold; place at the window empty; noted in the vestry book. The town asks the rate for the poor and notes the going of one of the poor, and does both in one afternoon. The stranger sits at the table where the wardens sit. The poor-house does not ask for the man. The town does not ask where the poor-house does not. I asked at the house on the Monday of the snow, and I asked again on the day of the register. The two askings are in my book. The town's noting is in the vestry book. The two books stand about one man, and do not say the same thing of him. I do not put them together. I leave the space between.*

I remained after the verger had gone and asked to see the rate book. The clerk brought it from the vestry table and opened it to the page where the winter figures had been entered. There was nothing remarkable in the handwriting. Names, amounts, objections, and a final mark beside the agreed sum. Hobbs's name appeared among the poor, followed by a short note concerning his absence.

I read the entry twice.

The stranger's name was not there.

That mattered less than I expected. If he had spoken at the meeting without his name entering the book, then his presence and his authority were two different questions. I wrote neither conclusion. I wrote only that he had been present, that he had spoken, and that the record did not preserve the reason.

The clerk closed the book.

"You are fond of records," he said.

"I am fond of knowing what they actually say."

He smiled without amusement. "That is not always the same thing."

I thanked him and left the vestry.

I shut the book. The lamp burned at the end of the lane. The gate was shut. The frost was settling.

I went to bed with the cold at the pane and the two facts in my mind, and I left them where they were, side by side, not touching.
