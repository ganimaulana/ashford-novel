# Chapter 36 — The Locked Room

The Saturday came in with a light frost that melted by midday, and I went about my morning with Goding's words still in my mind. I had not abandoned the question of Hodge; I had simply learned that it would not yield an answer by being stared at. I had spent the night turning over the question of the field and the house, and I had come to no conclusion except that the records did not agree and that I did not know which of them was wrong. I went to the pump for water, and I stood in the line and listened to the talk of the town, and I heard nothing of Hodge, nothing of the stranger, nothing of Foster. The town had moved on, and I had not.

I was at the door of my house when I saw the constable coming down the lane. He was a square man of fifty-one, with a face that had spent thirty years watching other men lie, and he walked the way he did everything, with a deliberate steadiness that made no apology for itself.

"Mr. Ashcroft," he said.

"Constable."

"I have a thing to ask you. It is not a thing I would ask lightly. But the verger told me you noticed the frost marks at the tower door, and that you wrote down two comings and one going. That is a rare way of seeing."

I did not answer. I waited.

"Come with me," he said. "I will tell you on the way."

---

He led me to the Greyhound, the inn at the end of the market square. It was a building of grey stone, with a sign that creaked in the wind, and it had the look of a place that had been standing for a long time and had learned to keep its own counsel. The constable did not stop at the door. He led me through the common room, past the fire and the tables, and up the stairs to a corridor at the back of the building.

"There is a room here," he said, "at the end of the corridor. It has been used as a store room for the last three years, because the landlord cannot rent it. He says the room moves things."

"Moves things?"

"He says that when he locks it at night and opens it in the morning, the furniture is not where he left it. A chair moved to the window. A book on the floor. A picture turned to the wall. Small things, all of them. Nothing taken. Nothing broken. But not where they were put."

"And you believe him?"

The constable looked at me. "I believe that a man who has kept an inn for twenty years does not invent stories about his own rooms. It costs him money to keep a room empty. He would not do it for a story."

We reached the door. It was a plain door, of the same grey wood as the rest of the building, with a lock that was old but solid. The constable took a key from his pocket and turned it.

"I have the key," he said. "The landlord gave it to me this morning. I have not been in since. I thought you should see it as it is, before anyone has touched it."

He opened the door.

The room was small, the kind of room that a traveller would rent for a night and leave without memory. There was a bed against the far wall, with a cover that had faded from its original colour. There was a table by the window, and a chair beside it, and a washbasin on a stand, and a picture on the wall of a landscape that I did not recognise. The floor was bare wood, clean, with no dust that I could see. It looked like a room that had been swept and left.

"What do you see?" the constable asked.

"I see a room that has been cleaned," I said. "The landlord has not left it untouched. He has swept it, or someone has, since it was last used."

The constable nodded. "He sweeps it once a week, he says, because a store room that is not swept is a room that begins to smell. He does not move the furniture. He sweeps around it."

I looked at the furniture. The chair was pushed in to the table, the way a chair is pushed in when a room is being kept tidy. The picture hung straight on its hook. The washbasin stood on its stand, empty, the pitcher beside it. Nothing was out of place.

"When was the room last locked?"

"Last night. The landlord says he locked it at ten, after the last of the travellers had gone. He came up this morning at seven, and the chair was at the window, facing out. He did not leave it there. He had moved it back before he came to me."

"The chair at the window," I said. "Facing out, toward the street?"

"Toward the square, he said. He did not say why he noticed it. He said only that it was not where he had left it, and that it was the third time this month."

I walked to the window and looked out. It gave onto the square, and from that height I could see the church tower and the roofs of the houses and the road that led out of town. I could see anyone who walked across the square. I could see anyone who stood still and looked up.

"The chair was turned toward the window," I said. "That was the detail the landlord noticed."

"I cannot say," said the constable. "I can say only that the landlord is a man of plain habits, and that he does not move furniture in the night. I have asked him whether he has had any trouble with the room—travellers who have used it, strangers who have asked about it, anyone who might have a key. He says no. He says the room has been locked for three years, and that he and his wife are the only ones who have keys, and that neither of them has moved a chair to the window."

I turned from the window and looked at the room again. The floor was clean. The furniture was in order. The lock was old but solid, and I had seen the constable turn the key, and I had heard the bolt move in the door.

"Do you believe that something moved the chair?" I said.

The constable looked at me, and I saw him measure the question.

"I believe that the landlord believes it," he said. "I believe that he has told the same story to his wife and to his help, and that they have told him he is mistaken. I believe that he is not the kind of man who enjoys being mistaken, and that he has taken the trouble to lock the room and count the key, and that he is still certain the chair was at the window when he opened the door. I do not know whether that is proof. I know only that I cannot explain it, and that I would rather have someone else look at it than pretend I have not been asked."

He handed me the key. The new matter had nothing to do, so far as I knew, with Hodge or Bellweather Lane; it was simply a room in an inn that had acquired a reputation, and the constable wanted another pair of eyes on it.

"Keep it for a day," he said. "Come back tonight, or tomorrow morning, and look at the room as it is. If the chair moves again, you will have seen it. If it does not, you will have seen that. Either way, you will have seen something, and you will have written it down, and I will have a second account of the same door."

I took the key, and I gave him my word. Before I left, I knelt by the chair and drew a small chalk mark on the floor, just beside the back left leg, and another where the front right leg met a crack in the board. I checked the window latch—it was fastened from the inside—and the lock on the door. I closed the door, turned the key, and counted the turns. I would know if the lock had been opened.

---

I locked the door and went out into the square, and the cold had come back with the afternoon, and the town was settling into its Saturday quiet. I walked to my house and sat at the desk, and I took out the book and wrote what I had seen:

*The Greyhound: room at the end of the corridor, locked for three years. The landlord reports that furniture has been moved on three occasions this month: a chair at the window, a book on the floor, a picture turned to the wall. The constable has the key. I have examined the room. It appears ordinary. The floor is clean; the furniture is in order; no sign of forced entry or recent disturbance. The lock is old but solid. The landlord and his wife have keys; the help may have access. The room overlooks the square. If the chair moved, it was placed to face the window. I cannot determine who moved it, or whether it moved at all. The landlord is not the kind of man who invents stories. The constable trusts him. I will return tonight and see whether anything has changed.*

I shut the book and sat with the fire low, and I thought of the room, and the chair, and the square below the window, and I did not know whether I was looking at a mystery or a misunderstanding. I knew only that the constable had asked me to look, and that I had said yes, and that I would go back at night with the key in my pocket, and I would open the door, and I would see what I would see.

---

I went back at eight, when the inn was full and the common room was warm and the landlord was behind the bar. He was a man of perhaps fifty, with a face that had been worn smooth by years of dealing with travellers, and he looked at me the way he looked at everyone, with the practiced neutrality of a man who had learned not to judge.

"The constable told me," he said. "You are the one who keeps the book."

"I am the one who keeps the book."

"Then you will not tell me what you think of the room until you have seen it. That is what he told me. He said you would look first, and you would write later, and you would not write what you had not seen. That is a good way to work, if you can do it."

I took the key and went up the stairs. The corridor was dark, and the door was at the end, and I turned the key and opened it. The room was not the same. The chair was at the window, facing out. I stopped in the doorway and looked at the floor. The chalk marks I had made that morning were still there, undisturbed—but the chair's legs no longer rested on them. The chair had been lifted and placed elsewhere; the marks on the floor showed no sign of being swept or smudged. The window latch was still fastened from inside. The lock had not been forced.

I stood in the room and looked at the chair, and then at the marks on the floor, and I could not say how the chair had been moved without opening the door or the window. I closed the door, locked it, and went down the stairs.

The landlord looked at me as I passed.

"Well?" he said.

"The chair has moved," I said. "But I cannot say how."

He nodded slowly, and I saw him take in the weight of it. I went out into the cold, and I carried the question with me.

I went home and wrote in the book:

*The chair was at the window when I returned. My chalk marks remained undisturbed beneath where it had stood that morning. The window was fastened from inside. The lock was untampered. I could not see how the chair had been moved without someone entering the room, and I did not know of anyone entering while I held the key. I have a discrepancy I cannot explain. I will report it to the constable in the morning. For now, I have one fact and no answer.*

I shut the book and put it down. I had not solved the room. But I had learned something useful: a discrepancy could be measured, and measuring it did not make it disappear. I was no longer a witness to a story. I was a man who had been asked to look, and I had looked, and I had found a thing that did not fit.
