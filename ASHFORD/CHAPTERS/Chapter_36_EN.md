# Chapter 36 — The Constable's Note

The frost had held for three days after Goding's word, and the town had settled into the kind of January that does not ask permission. I had been in Ashford sixty-three days, and I had begun to feel the town as a thing that would not let go of me—not with affection, but with the slow weight of a place that had been waiting for someone to ask the right questions.

The lamp burned at nine, and was carried away along the rooms, and no sound came; and I set it down as the thirty-third reading, and let it stand. The alternation had held since the Saturday of the gate, with only the one break kept by itself, and I had come to trust the rule the way one trusts a clock that has not failed in a month—with a faith that was more habit than reason.

But the habit was wearing thin. I had seen the stones, and I had heard Goding's word, and I had carried the weight of the bell's sale in my mind for weeks. The questions were not fading; they were multiplying.

---

I went to Margaret's on the Monday for bread, and the shop was thinner than it had been before Christmas. The room was warm but not full, and the chairs that had once held three or four customers now held one or two, and the talk was of the price of flour and the cold that would not lift. Margaret set the loaf on the counter and did not smile.

"Three customers this morning," she said. "I have seen Januaries before, Mr. Ashcroft, but I have not seen one like this. The rate is in the bread, the flour is up again, and the people have stopped coming. They are not angry. They are just—gone."

I paid the price, which was a halfpenny more than last week, and I did not comment, because there was nothing to comment. The rate was in everything, and the town was in the rate, and I was in the town, and the three of us were in the winter.

Margaret wiped the counter and looked at me. "And what brings you out so early, Mr. Ashcroft? You've been about the town lately." I had expected the question. "The constable has asked me to help with some old records—historical boundaries, property lines, that sort of thing. It keeps me occupied." She nodded, and her eyes said she did not believe all of it, but she did not press.

---

On the Tuesday, a note came to my door.

It was not the kind of note that had been coming—the hand I knew, the fine pen, the upright letters. It was a plain hand, a constable's hand, and it said only:

*Mr. Ashcroft—I would speak with you at your convenience. There is a matter that may benefit from your observation. Calloway.*

I read it twice, and I understood what it meant: the constable had not forgotten the button, or the gate, or the night of the bell. He had been watching me as I had been watching the lane, and he had decided that my watching might be put to use.

I went to his office the same afternoon, because a man who waits on a constable's convenience does not wait long. The room was small, and the fire was low, and Calloway sat behind his desk with the same face he had worn at the roll-reading—plain, careful, and older than his years.

He did not offer tea, because tea was expensive and the constable's office did not run to luxuries. He offered a chair, and I took it.

"You have been on the watch-roll since November," he said. "And you have been watching things that are not in the orders."

"I have been noting what I see," I said.

"Noting," he repeated. "That is a useful word. It is not the same as reporting, and it is not the same as acting. It is the middle ground where a man can stand without committing himself. I have been standing in that middle ground for twenty-three years, Mr. Ashcroft, and I know the shape of it."

He paused, and the fire cracked in the silence.

"I have a role for you," he said. "It is not a constable's role, because you are not a constable. It is not a clerk's role, because you do not keep the town's books. It is a role that has no name in the statutes, and no salary in the budget, and no authority except what I give you. It is a role for a man who notes what others overlook."

"And what would that role be?"

He leaned forward and set his hands flat on the desk, the way a man does when he is about to say something he does not wish to repeat.

"You will be my eyes," he said. "Not in the lane—you are already watching the lane. In the town. I have had reports of things that do not fit. The innkeeper at the Greyhound has a room that will not stay as it is left. A widow on High Street has been receiving letters that speak of tomorrow. An old clock on Mill Lane has been heard to strike thirteen. These are small things—too small for the bench, too small for the magistrates, too small for the London office I spoke of. But they are not nothing. And I would have you look at them."

I waited. He met my gaze.

"I can pay you a small allowance—one pound a week, which is not much but is more than the poor-house will give you. And you may keep your own hours, your own book, and your own counsel. I ask only that you look, and that you tell me what you see."

I thought of the button in my pocket, and the gate at the corner, and the lamp that burned at nine. I thought of the bell that had rung on the Thursday night, and the foot that had gone over my head, and the house at the end of the lane that was not empty. I thought of my purse, which was lighter than it had been in November, and of the winter ahead.

"If the room will not stay as it is left, I would like to see it," I said.

Calloway nodded, and the bargain was made.

---

That night I wrote in my book:

*11 Jan. Calloway has offered me a role: to observe the things that do not fit. One pound per week, no fixed hours, no authority except his word. I have accepted. I did not ask about the London office, and he did not speak of it. There will be a time for that later. For now, I am to look at a room at the Greyhound, a letter on High Street, and a clock on Mill Lane. Three things. Three questions. I do not know yet whether they are the same question, but I have learned that in Ashford, the questions often share a shape.*

I shut the book, and the lamp came at nine, and was carried away along the rooms, and no sound came. I let it stand.

The winter had settled into Ashford, and I had settled into the winter. I had been in the town sixty-four days, and I had begun to see the shape of the questions. I did not know yet whether I would find the answers, but I knew that I would keep looking.
