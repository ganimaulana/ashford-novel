CHAPTER 36 — THE CONSTABLE'S OFFER

The frost had held for several days.

Ashford had become quieter beneath it. Footsteps sounded farther away than they ought to, carts moved more slowly along High Street, and the windows of the houses remained shut long after morning had arrived.

I had been in the town long enough to learn that silence did not always mean peace.

On Tuesday morning, a note arrived at my door.

Mr. Ashcroft,

I would speak with you at your convenience.

Calloway.

I went that afternoon.

Calloway received me in his small office, with a fire that had burned low and a stack of reports beside his elbow.

"You have been noticing things," he said.

"I have been writing them down."

"That is what concerns me."

He pushed three papers across the desk.

"The first is from the Greyhound. A room that does not remain as it is left."

"The second?"

"A woman on High Street receiving letters concerning events before they happen."

"And the third?"

"An old clock on Mill Lane. It has been heard striking thirteen."

I looked at the papers.

"Why me?"

"Because you noticed the button."

I said nothing.

"And the gate," he added.

Still I said nothing.

Calloway leaned back.

"I don't need another constable. I need someone who will look at a thing before deciding what it means."

"And if I find nothing?"

"Then you report nothing."

There was a pause.

"One pound a week."

That settled the practical question.

I accepted.

The Greyhound was the first.

The innkeeper showed me the room at the top of the stairs. A bed, a chair, a basin, a cold hearth.

Nothing remarkable.

Except that every morning something was different.

The bed had been disturbed.

The basin emptied.

The chair moved.

The ashes warm.

I examined the lock, the window, the floorboards and the ceiling.

Nothing.

That night I stayed inside.

Nothing happened.

At dawn, everything remained exactly where I had left it.

The innkeeper stared at me.

"You see?"

"I see that it didn't happen."

He lowered his voice.

"That's not the same as saying it won't."

I wrote that down.

The second case was Mrs. Hart's letters.

They contained ordinary predictions.

Flour would rise.

A neighbour would fall ill.

Rain would come.

All of them had been correct.

I examined the envelopes.

The paper was ordinary.

The ink was ordinary.

The postmark was ordinary.

Nothing about the letters explained anything.

Then I noticed something.

The date.

The letter had arrived before the date stamped upon it.

I read it again.

This time I did not call it a prediction.

I wrote:

There may be a difference between knowing something will happen and causing someone to believe it will.

I did not know which applied.

The third case took me to Mill Lane.

The clock stood inside an abandoned house.

Its mechanism was old.

Its pendulum was still.

And, according to the neighbours, it sometimes struck thirteen.

I examined the works.

One gear was missing.

I expected that to explain everything.

It did not.

A clock with a missing gear should not run properly.

Yet someone had heard it.

I returned that night.

At midnight, it struck twelve.

Nothing more.

I waited.

Nothing.

I returned the following evening.

Again, twelve.

Then, as I was preparing to leave, something touched the floor behind me.

A small metallic sound.

I turned.

There was a gear lying beside the clock.

It had not been there when I entered.

I picked it up.

It was warm.

Not hot.

Just warm enough to make me hesitate.

I turned it between my fingers.

It was not shaped like the missing gear.

I could not identify the metal.

I put it in my pocket.

For the first time since Calloway had offered me the work, I did not know what to write.

So I wrote the simplest thing.

The object appeared.

Nothing more.
