CHAPTER 94 — THE CHOICE

I woke before dawn the next morning. The air was cold and still. I lay in the narrow bed, staring at the ceiling, and thought about the four men who had left the grounds the day before. They had been there in the morning. By noon they were gone. I did not know their names. I did not know whether they had been sent home or elsewhere. I only knew that they were no longer candidates.

The note came after breakfast. It said only: The field. Eight o'clock.

I put on my coat and went.

The field was empty. The posts and ropes were gone. The markings on the ground had been erased. Only the grass and the dirt remained. The other candidates were already gathering at the edge of the field. I counted nineteen of them. Twenty of us remained, including myself.

Calloway stood on the low steps of the main building. He was not holding a paper this time. He simply waited until we were silent.

"There are twenty of you," he said. "The next task will not be performed as a group. It will be performed individually. You will each be given a choice."

He paused.

"At the far end of the field, there are two tables. On each table, there is a sealed envelope. One envelope contains a map of the grounds with a specific location marked. The other contains a key. The next stage will require both knowing where to go and being able to enter. One of these items will give you the location; the other will give you access. You may choose only one now. The other you will have to obtain later, and it will cost you time, effort, or cooperation. You will not know which envelope contains which item until after you have chosen. You may take your chosen item and return to your room. You will not open the envelope until you are alone. You will not show its contents to anyone. You will not discuss your choice with any other candidate. You will not be told whether your choice was correct."

He looked at us.

"You will make your choice alone. When your name is called, you will walk to the center of the field. The clerk will place one envelope on the left table and one on the right. You will choose one. You will not touch the other. You will then return to your room. That is all."

He stepped back.

I stood in silence. I did not know what the choice meant. I did not know whether the map or the key would be more useful. I did not know whether there was a correct answer or whether the office simply wanted to see what I would choose.

I looked at the field. The left table was nearer the main building. The right table was nearer the gate that led to the eastern section of the grounds, an area I had not yet explored.

I considered my situation. I was not the strongest candidate. If I chose the key and it turned out I needed to find the correct door first, I might exhaust myself searching. If I chose the map, I would at least know where to go, even if I had to earn the key later. The map would give me a clear direction, and I could conserve my strength for the task of obtaining the key. The key might give me immediate access, but without knowing the location, I could waste time and energy looking for the right place.

I thought about the previous rounds. In the first round, I had chosen the reliable route over the faster one, and it had worked. In the second round, I had chosen the longer, firmer path over the risky shortcut. In both cases, I had succeeded by avoiding unnecessary risk and conserving my strength. I did not know whether the same reasoning would apply here, but it was the only reasoning I had.

I looked at the other candidates. Weaver was near the edge, his hands in his pockets, his face unreadable. He had always been careful, practical. He would choose what seemed most useful for the next task. Merritt was standing apart, his arms crossed. He would choose based on what he had observed about the grounds. Peale was near the fountain, watching the crowd. He would choose based on what he thought the office wanted.

The clerk began to read names. One by one, the candidates walked to the center of the field, chose an envelope, and walked back. I watched them. Some chose the left table. Some chose the right. None of them looked back. Weaver chose the right table. Merritt chose the left. Peale chose the left. Ellison chose the right.

When my name was called, I walked to the center of the field. The clerk placed one envelope on the left table and one on the right. They were identical in size and weight. I could not see any difference between them.

I had no way to know which was which. But I had to choose.

I chose the left table.

I took the envelope and walked back to my room. I closed the door and sat on the bed. I opened the envelope slowly, carefully. Inside was a map.

It was a map of the grounds, marked with a red circle near the eastern gate. A note was written on the back: "Report to the clerk at dawn."

I folded the map and put it in my pocket. I had the map. I knew where to go. But I did not know what the clerk would ask of me. I did not know whether I would earn the key, or whether I would be given another task, or whether I had chosen correctly.

I sat on the bed and waited.

I waited for the next instruction. I waited to find out what the clerk would require. I waited to see whether my choice would matter.

I did not know what the next day would bring. I only knew that I had chosen, and that the choice could not be undone.
