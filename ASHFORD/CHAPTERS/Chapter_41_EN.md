# Chapter 41 — The Interlude

The days between the letters and the clock were quiet, the way the days between cases often are. I had learned that the room changed when it was listened to, and that the letters arrived when they were expected, and I had begun to see the shape of a pattern that I could not yet name.

I went to Margaret's for bread on the Tuesday, and the shop was as empty as it had been the week before. Margaret stood behind the counter with her cloth in her hand, and she looked at me the way a woman looks at a man who has been carrying something heavy.

"You have been working for the constable," she said. It was not a question.

"I have been helping him with some matters."

She nodded, and she did not ask what matters. She knew the way of such things: a man who helps the constable is a man who has chosen to stand on one side of a line, and she would not ask which side he had chosen. "Four days since your last visit," she said. "That's not like you."

"Daniel came by yesterday," she said. "He asked about you. He said you had been seen at the Greyhound, and at a house on High Street. He said you were becoming a man of the town."

I did not answer. I did not know what Daniel had meant by that, and I did not want to guess.

I took the bread and went out, and I walked through the streets of Ashford with the cold on my face and the weight of the week in my pocket. I had been in the town fifty days, and I had begun to feel the town as a kind of language—a language I was learning to speak, but not yet to understand.

At noon, I went to the Academy to find Daniel. He was in the library, surrounded by books, with the look of a man who had been reading all morning and would read all afternoon. He looked up when I came in, and he smiled the way a man smiles when he has been expecting someone.

"I heard you were working for the constable," he said. "I did not believe it at first. But then I saw you at the Greyhound, and I knew it was true."

"Did you follow me?"

"I did not follow you. I happened to be passing. And I happened to see you go in. And I happened to wonder what you were doing."

I sat down across from him, and I did not explain. Daniel was a good man, and a good friend, but he was not a man who could be told everything. He would ask questions, and he would not stop asking, and he would not understand the answers.

"I am helping the constable with some matters," I said. "Nothing more."

He looked at me the way a man looks at a thing he is trying to understand, and then he nodded. "All right. But if you ever need help—I am good at finding things. That is what I do. I find things in books, and I find things in records, and I find things in people's memories. If you ever need help, you can ask me."

I thanked him, and I went home.

*20 Jan. I have begun to see the shape of it: the room changes when it is listened to, the letters arrive when they are expected, and the clock strikes when someone is present. There is a pattern, and I am beginning to understand it. The pattern is this: the anomaly is not the thing itself—the room, the letters, the clock—but the condition that triggers it. The condition is the key. I am learning to find the condition before I find the answer. That is the difference between a man who observes and a man who understands.*

I shut the book and sat with the thought. I had been given three cases, and I had learned something from each. The room had taught me to look for the trigger. The letters had taught me that the trigger could be a condition, not a cause. The clock had taught me that the trigger could be an object, not an event. I did not yet know how the three were connected, but I was beginning to suspect that they were all pieces of the same question.

The question was not what was happening. The question was what was making it happen.
