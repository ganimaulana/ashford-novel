# Chapter 41 — The Agreement's Two Hands

I had not intended to return to Vale's cottage so soon. I had the copy of the Agreement, and I had the questions it raised, and I had my notebook with the facts and the gaps set down in separate columns. That should have been enough for a week. But the Agreement had a weight that did not leave the room when I closed the book, and I found myself on the path to his door two days later, without having decided to go. The document had stayed with me in ways I could not explain—its words turning in my mind like stones in a slow stream, each question opening into another, until I could no longer tell whether I was investigating the past or being drawn into it.

The lane was quiet, the frost still holding in the shadows, and the cottage at the end stood with its chimney smoking in the pale grey light. The garden was neat, the hedges cut back, and the gate swung open easily under my hand. I had not knocked when I came through the gate; I had simply walked up to the door and found him already in the garden, cutting back a hedge that had grown wild over the winter. He did not look up when I came through the gate. He kept his hands on the shears and his eyes on the branch he was cutting, and he said: "You have read the document again."

"I have."

"And you have more questions."

"I have more questions."

He set down the shears and straightened. "I was the same, when I first saw it. I thought I could find the answer if I read it enough times. I cannot tell you how long it took me to understand that the document was not written to be understood. It was written to be kept." He looked at me for a moment, then turned and walked towards the cottage, motioning for me to follow.

He led me into the cottage and poured two cups of tea, and we sat at the table where he had laid the Agreement before me. The leather folder was not on the table. I did not ask where he kept it. The fire burned low, and the light through the window was thin and grey, and the room had the particular stillness of a place that had been lived in for a long time.

"You said the Agreement was made between several families," I said. "Do you know which families?"

He took a sip of his tea. "Some names are still known. Others are not. The document lists them, but the list is incomplete. Some of the names have been crossed out, and I do not know whether that means they withdrew from the Agreement, or whether they died out, or whether they were removed for another reason. I have tried to trace them, but the records are too fragmentary. The names that remain are the ones that stayed—whether by choice or by necessity, I cannot say."

"Ashcroft is still on the list."

He looked at me. "Ashcroft is still on the list. The name is legible, and it has not been crossed out. That is one of the reasons I showed you the document. I have been keeping it for fifteen years, and I have known for fifteen years that the Ashcroft name was still attached to it. I did not know whether there was anyone left to take up the responsibility."

"Now you do."

"Now I do."

He set down his cup and leaned forward. "The Agreement has been preserved by a series of people. Some belonged to names in the document. Some did not. The responsibility passed from one hand to another, not always in a straight line. There are gaps in the record, and I do not know whether the gaps are losses or omissions."

"Who appointed the keepers?"

He was quiet for a moment. "I do not know. The Agreement itself does not say. It names responsibilities, but it does not name an authority that enforces them. The keeping seems to have been a matter of custom and consent, passed down by men who understood that something had to be preserved, even if they did not fully understand what."

"Did you ever meet the man who gave it to you?"

"Yes. He was an old man, older than I am now. He lived in this house before me, and he gave me the folder and the key to the cabinet and a list of instructions that I have since lost. He told me to keep the Agreement safe, and to pass it on when the time came, and to make sure the boundary was preserved. He did not tell me why."

"Did he know?"

"I do not think so. I think he was like me—a man who had inherited a duty and had not inherited the explanation. He had kept it for thirty years, and he had not opened the door, and he had not asked the question that would have made him break the Agreement. He was careful in his way. He kept what he was given, and he did not look for what was not there."

I thought about that. "And you?"

He looked at me steadily. "I have looked. I have gone through parish records, old maps, and every document I could find. I have spoken to the descendants of the families named in the Agreement. I have found fragments, but I have not found a complete answer. I know that the door beneath the mill has been closed for as long as anyone can remember. I know that the boundary line was drawn before the Agreement was written. I know that the Agreement was meant to preserve a condition that existed before it. But I do not know what that condition was."

He reached beneath the table and brought out a small leather pouch. He opened it and placed a worn key on the table between us. It was old, and the metal was dark with age, and it did not look like it would fit any lock I had seen. The key was small, with a simple head and a shaft that was worn smooth by years of handling. I could see the marks of a hand that had held it many times.

"This is the key to the cabinet where the Agreement is kept," he said. "I am giving it to you so that you can read the document whenever you need to. You may keep it, and you may return it when you are finished. I do not expect you to take up anything. But I expect you to understand what you are looking at before you decide what to do with it." He placed it on the table between us, and I saw the marks of years on its surface—the darkening of the metal, the faint scratches where it had been turned and turned again. It was not a key of ceremony; it was a key of use.

I looked at the key. I did not touch it.

"Why me?" I said.

"Because you are an Ashcroft. Because you have already found the door. Because you have kept a notebook and written down what you saw and did not write down what you did not see. Because you came back." He paused. "And because no one else has."

I took the key. It was cold in my palm. I turned it over, feeling its weight, the smoothness of the metal where it had been held. It was a key to a cabinet, and to the record inside it. Whatever else it represented, I was not prepared to decide yet.

I went home with the key in my pocket and the name Vale in my mind. I had not agreed to anything. I had accepted a key, and I had heard an old man tell me that he had spent fifteen years preserving a record whose full meaning he did not understand.

That night I opened the leather folder again and read the Agreement more carefully. It had been written in formal language, and it used terms that were old even by the standards of the time it was written. I did not understand all of them. But I found a phrase that I had not noticed before: *The line shall be maintained by the keeper of the house and the keeper of the record, in equal measure.*

I copied it into my notebook. I did not know what it meant. I did not know who had written it, or why it was placed in the middle of a document that otherwise concerned boundaries and doors. I knew only that the Agreement was not just about preserving a line. It was about two people working together.

I looked at the key on the desk. It had been given to me by a man who had kept the Agreement for fifteen years, and it had been given to him by a man who had kept it for thirty, and it had been passed down through a chain of hands that I could not trace. I did not know whether the chain had anything to do with me at all.

I wrote in my notebook:

*Vale says the Agreement has been preserved by a succession of people, some connected to the names in it and some not. It describes two responsibilities, one concerning the house and one concerning the record. I do not know whether those responsibilities were always held by separate people. The key gives me access to the document; it does not tell me what I am meant to do with it.*

I shut the book and sat with the fire low. Outside, the lane was quiet, and the house at the end stood dark, and I thought of the door beneath the mill, and the Agreement, and the chain of hands that had passed it down without knowing why. I did not know whether I was the next link in that chain. But I knew that the chain had not been broken yet.

I sat for a long time, watching the fire. The weight of the key was still in my palm, and the weight of the Agreement was still in my mind. I had not asked for a responsibility, and I had not accepted one. I had only taken a key because I wanted to know what was written in the record it opened. For the moment, that was enough.
