# Chapter 74 — Physical Trial

The notice came on a Tuesday morning. It was an envelope without a seal — just the flap folded and tucked, the kind a runner delivers by hand. I opened it at the table and read what was inside: a single sheet, typed, with instructions for the field assessment.

*You will report to the east gate at first light tomorrow. You will carry the kit provided. You will follow the route marked on the map. You will complete the circuit within the window specified. You will not deviate from the route. You will not stop except at the points marked. You will return to the gate before the window closes.*

The map was attached, folded twice. It showed the eastern district: a loop of about twelve miles, through the fields and back along the ridge. The route crossed the old stone bridge, passed the ruined mill, climbed the ridge path, and returned along the lower road. There were three marked points: the bridge, the mill, and the ridge. The window was four hours — first light to mid-morning.

I traced the route with my finger. It was not a difficult walk. It was not even a long one. But the kit included a water flask, a survey compass, and a sealed packet that I was told contained a test object. I was to carry the kit, follow the route, and at each marked point I would find a task waiting.

Orme appeared in the doorway as I finished reading.

"You have the route," he said.

"Is that all?"

"That is the first part." He studied me. "You have been walking since you arrived at the cottage. Your pace is steady. Your stride is even. Your breath is regular."

I had not noticed him watching my walk. "Does that matter?"

"It tells me whether you can carry the kit for twelve miles on varied terrain. It tells me whether you will arrive at the points in time." He stepped closer. "Tell me: what happens when you are tired?"

"I slow down."

"And when you are lost?"

"I stop and find my bearings."

"And when the weather turns?"

"I look again before I choose a direction."

He nodded once.

I closed the map. "And the test object?"

"The packet contains a seal. It is blank. At each point, you will find instructions for what to do with it. Follow the instructions. Report what you find."

"Is that all?"

"That is the first part."

---

At first light I was at the east gate. The air was crisp and still. The path rose gently into the fields, and the grass was heavy with dew. I carried the kit — the flask, the compass, and the sealed packet — in my satchel. The walk began well.

The first point was the bridge, a stone arch over a small stream. At the base of the bridge, tucked against the masonry, was a tin box. Inside it was a note: *The seal has been moved downstream. Follow the water. You have twenty minutes.*

I read the note twice. "Downstream" — but the stream was barely a trickle. It would not carry the seal far. And it was dry in places. I looked at the water level. The bridge had been crossed by many feet before mine. The stream was known to flood after rain, and the last rain had been three days prior.

I had twenty minutes. I could walk downstream for twenty minutes, or I could return to the bridge and wait for someone else to move the seal. The note said "follow the water" but the water was a trickle. The seal had been "moved" — by whom, and how?

I followed the stream for ten minutes. The water narrowed, then widened, then narrowed again. At no point was the seal visible. At fifteen minutes I turned back. At the bridge I found a second tin box, hidden under a loose stone. Inside it was another note: *You chose correctly. The water does not move a seal. It is carried by hand. Return to the gate.*

I returned to the gate at 07:30 — thirty minutes early.

---

The second point was the mill, a skeleton of timber and stone half-swallowed by ivy. The wheel was broken, and the path past it was overgrown. At the base of the mill wall I found another tin box, rusted shut. It took me three minutes to pry the lid open with the compass housing.

Inside was a note: *The seal is here. You may take it, or leave it. If you take it, mark the location on your map. If you leave it, write why.*

I looked at the seal through the box — the packet I had been carrying. I had the seal already. This was a second seal, placed here. Or was it the same seal, moved? The note did not say.

I took out my map and marked the location of the mill. Then I wrote: *I leave the seal in place. I already carry one. Taking a duplicate without purpose risks confusion. The office must know where this one waits.*

I did not sign the note. I did not know I was supposed to.

I returned to the gate at 09:45 — ten minutes late.

---

The third point was the ridge path, steep and stony, leading up to the plateau. The climb was harder than the flat walk. My breath came faster. My hands gripped the stone ledges. At the top, I found the third tin box nailed to a tree.

Inside was a note: *Time remaining: thirty-two minutes. The kit must be returned to the gate. You may not descend the same way. The lower road is open. You must decide.*

I looked at the map. The ridge path was the only route marked for descent. The lower road was an alternative — longer, but gentler. The lower road ran along the base of the ridge, parallel to my route home. It would take me back to the gate, but it would take longer.

Thirty-two minutes. If I ran down the ridge path — dangerous, stony, steep — I could make it in twenty. If I walked the lower road, I could make it in forty-five. If I took the lower road and stopped at the mill to rest, I could make it in fifty.

The note said I "must decide." It did not say I must arrive on time. It said I must decide.

I wrote in my notebook: *Ridge path is faster but dangerous. Lower road is safer but longer. Decision: take the lower road. The kit is not urgent. Arrival time is not the measure of the test.*

I started down.

But halfway down, I heard voices. Two figures, speaking quietly. I stopped and listened.

"It should be at the mill," one said. "The seal was supposed to be there."

"I checked. It is not." The voice was familiar. "I think we should split up. One of us checks the lower road, the other checks the ridge."

I recognised the voices. The first was Constable Owen — the man who had met me at the gate on my first visit, the man who had walked the perimeter with me when I was looking for Vale's papers. The two of them met. Owen was searching for the seal that I had left at the mill.

I waited until they were out of earshot, then continued down the lower road.

At the gate I arrived at 10:17. I was seven minutes over.

---

The gate was empty. The kit was sitting on the table where I had left it. I was alone with the compass, the flask, and the sealed packet. No one appeared. I waited ten minutes, then went to the office.

Orme was in the back room. Calloway was by the fireplace. The clerk was copying into the ledger.

"How do you feel?" Calloway asked.

"Tired," I said. "My legs are tired. My breathing is slow. I am hungry."

"And the decision at the ridge?"

"That was the question. The lower road or the ridge. I chose the lower road because the kit was not urgent, and arrival time was not the measure of the test."

"And what did you hear on the lower road?"

I told him about the voices. About Owen. About the search.

Orme was quiet. "You heard two constables discussing a seal. You chose not to approach. You chose not to follow. You chose to continue your own task."

"Is that right?"

"That is correct." He closed the packet I had carried. "You returned the kit. You did not open the seal. You did not interfere with the constables. You completed the route as instructed, with one deviation that you reported."

"And the score?"

"By what measure?"

"The route. The decisions. The timing."

"The route was followed. The decisions were yours to make. The timing was not the question." He looked at me. "The question was whether you would interfere with the office's other work. You did not. That is a passing score."

I wrote that in my book: *Route complete. Kit returned. Seal unopened. Did not interfere with other constables. Score: pass.*

"Owen was looking for something on my route," I said. "Something the office put there."

"Owen is a good tracker. A good climber. A good runner. He would have found the seal on the ridge faster than I found it on the lower road. But he would not have heard me listening. He would not have known I was there."

"That is the second question."

"I see."

"Owen sees the ground. You see the space between things." Orme stood. "That is why both of you are here."

Calloway set the paper down. "There will be another."

"How many?"

"As many as the office needs."

---

I walked home along the ridge, not the lower road. I had finished the test and I had time. The sun was high and the path was clear. I walked slowly. I thought about what Orme had said — about Owen seeing the ground and me seeing the space between things.

At the cottage I lit the fire and opened my notebook. I wrote: *One route. Three points. One decision. The kit was not urgent. The timing was not the measure. I heard constables searching for the seal. I did not interfere. I did not join their search. I did not report their names.*

I wrote: *The office put something on the ridge. Owen looked for it. I found it by accident. The difference is not skill. It is position. The office tests both.*

I closed the notebook and sat by the fire. The seal was still in the packet. I had not opened it. I had not needed to. The test had not been about finding what was lost. It had been about knowing what to do when you found something that was not yours to take.

Outside, the wind moved through the grass. Somewhere on the ridge, a stone marker waited for the next walker to notice it. I did not need to find it. I had already passed the point where it mattered.
