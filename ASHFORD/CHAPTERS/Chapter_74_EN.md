CHAPTER 74 — THE DISCREPANCY

I went to the office on Saturday morning, because Friday had ended with Calloway's promise and the constable had sent for me.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open. I had been there seven times before. But I had never been there as a man who was expected to make a judgment, and I had never been there as a man who was not answerable to the office at all.

I stepped inside.

The room was not the same. The chairs had been moved back to their usual places. The desk was clear except for two sheets of paper and a pencil. The clerk was there, copying into the ledger. Calloway was there, standing by the fireplace. Orme was there, sitting in the chair opposite the desk.

Calloway looked up. He set the paper down. He took off his hat and set it on the desk.

"You have done one test," he said.

"I have," I said.

"This is not another room," he said. "This is a question. The clerk will give you two statements. They concern the same incident. They disagree. You will read them. You will tell the office which you believe and why. The clerk will write it. You will sign it. The record will be the office's record. The judgment will be yours."

"What is the incident," I said.

"That is in the statements," Calloway said.

He nodded at the clerk. The clerk turned the ledger. He took the two sheets of paper from the desk and placed them in front of me.

The first was dated the tenth of January. It said:

A farmer states that his gate was found open at dawn on the tenth of January. The post was not split. The hinge was not pulled. The gate hung straight. There was a scratch on the lower rail, new and pale, as though something had brushed against it. The farmer says he heard no sound during the night. The farmer says he locked the gate himself at dusk.

The second was dated the eleventh of January. It said:

A carter states that he passed through the lane at half-past nine on the evening of the tenth. He saw the gate standing open. He saw no one nearby. He says the wind was strong from the east. He says he did not stop. He says he did not touch the gate.

I read them twice. The first said the gate was open at dawn. The second said the gate was open at half-past nine. The first said the post was not split and the hinge was not pulled. The second said nothing about the post or the hinge. The first said there was a scratch on the lower rail. The second said nothing about a scratch. The first said the farmer had locked the gate himself at dusk. The second said nothing about the farmer.

I set the papers down. The clerk looked at me. He did not say anything. He waited.

"The first statement says the gate was locked at dusk," I said. "The second statement says the gate was open at half-past nine. The two cannot both be true unless the gate was opened between dusk and half-past nine."

"That is the question," Calloway said. "Which do you believe?"

"I do not believe either of them," I said. "I believe the first man locked the gate at dusk. I believe the second man saw the gate open at half-past nine. What I do not know is whether the first man is mistaken about having locked it, or whether someone opened it afterwards, or whether the second man is mistaken about the time or the gate. The statements do not tell me that."

"Then which do you act on," Calloway said.

"I would not act on either," I said. "I would look at the gate first. The first man mentions a scratch on the lower rail. The second man says nothing about it. If the scratch is there, the gate was touched by something after dusk. If the scratch is not there, the first man imagined it or the second man missed it. The office can determine which by looking at the gate. Until the gate is examined, choosing between the statements is only guessing."

Calloway looked at the clerk. The clerk wrote quickly, the same small precise letters. He wrote: Statement one: gate locked at dusk, open at dawn, scratch on lower rail. Statement two: gate open at half-past nine, no mention of scratch. Subject declines to choose between statements. Subject says office should examine the gate before choosing which statement to credit. Scratch noted as one detail to check.

He set the pencil down. He turned the page. He looked at me.

"Sign," he said.

I signed my name at the bottom of the line.

Calloway took the paper. He read it. He set it on the desk.

"The test is not finished," he said. "You have done what was asked. The record is made. The office will consider it."

He looked at Orme. Orme looked at the floor. He did not say anything.

"There will be another," Calloway said. "The constable will send for you."

He stood up. He put on his hat. He walked to the door. He opened it. He looked out at the lane. He turned back.

He went out.

The clerk looked at me. He did not say anything. He turned back to the ledger. Orme stood up. He put on his hat. He looked at me.

"The office has a duty," he said. "The duty is not finished."

He went out.

I went out.

The lane was quiet. The house was shut. I set the key in the lock and turned it. The door opened. The room was the same.

I did not know whether the gate would be examined. I did not know whether my refusal to choose was what they had wanted or the opposite of it. I did not know whether Orme was satisfied or whether he had not yet decided.

But I knew the examination had continued.
