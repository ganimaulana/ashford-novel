CHAPTER 85 — THE BARGAIN

I did not hear from the office for two days after the book was found. I had expected something—a word, a summons, a sign that the examination was continuing. But nothing came, and I found myself walking the lane each morning, watching the door, waiting for a note that did not arrive.

On Sunday morning I went to the market again. The stalls were open, the lanes were crowded, and the morning was cold enough that the breath of the men and women who passed me rose in white clouds. I bought bread and cheese and a small knife to replace the one I had lost, and I was turning toward the lane when I saw Peale.

He was standing by the chandler’s, the same place he had stood before. He was not writing. He was not looking at anyone. He was looking at the sky, and when he saw me, he nodded once, as though he had been waiting for me.

I nodded back. I did not approach him. I did not know what to say to him, and he did not say anything to me. We stood there for a moment, and then he turned and walked away, and I went home.

On Monday morning a note came. It said only: The office. Nine o’clock. Bring nothing.

I put on my coat and went.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open, and the crowd was already there. There were fewer of them than before—perhaps eighteen, perhaps fewer. Some of the faces I remembered. Some I did not. The man with the notebook was there, standing apart from the others, his notebook closed but in his hand. The older man with the frayed cuffs was there, leaning against the wall by the pump. Peale was there, standing near the door, and he looked at me when I arrived and nodded once.

I nodded back.

I looked for Ellison and Merritt. Ellison was standing near the back, his hands in his pockets, his face unreadable. Merritt was standing alone by the gate, not looking at anyone.

I walked over to Ellison.

“You are here,” I said.

“I am here,” he said. “I did not think we would be called again so soon.”

“Do you know what we are to do?”

“No,” he said. “But I know who does.”

He pointed to Calloway, who was standing on the step, looking at the crowd the way he always looked at it.

Calloway spoke.

“You have completed the first tasks,” he said. “Some of you found what you were looking for. Some of you did not. The office does not care which. The office cares how you chose to look.”

He paused.

“The next task is different,” he said. “You will be given a location and an instruction. The instruction will be incomplete. You will have to decide what to do with the missing information. You have until noon. That is all.”

He turned and went inside. The clerk did not come forward. There were no papers, no instructions, no division into groups.

We stood in silence.

“An incomplete instruction,” Ellison said. “What does that mean?”

“It means they want to see what we do,” I said.

“What do we do?”

“I do not know.”

I looked at the other candidates. Some were already moving. The man with the notebook was walking, his group following him. The older man with the frayed cuffs was walking, his hands in his pockets. Peale was walking, his head down, his steps even.

“We should go,” I said.

We walked. The instruction was simple: Go to the old mill. Find the stone with the mark. Bring what is underneath it.

We reached the old mill. The door was open, the wind blowing through it. We went inside.

“Find the stone with the mark,” Ellison said. “What mark?”

“I do not know.”

We searched. I looked at the floor. I looked at the walls. I looked at the old machinery. I found nothing.

Ellison was examining the floorboards. He ran his hand along them, looking for anything unusual.

Merritt walked to the far end of the room. He stood still, looking at the wall.

“There is a mark here,” he said.

His voice was rough, as though he had not used it in days. I walked over to him. He was pointing to a small mark on the wall, a line and a circle, the same mark we had seen before.

“That is the mark,” I said.

“It is,” he said.

“Where is the stone?”

He did not answer. He looked at the floor beneath the mark. The floorboards were loose, as though someone had lifted them recently.

I knelt and lifted the boards. Beneath them was a small stone, flat and grey, with the same mark carved into its surface. I lifted it. Beneath the stone was a piece of paper, folded and sealed.

I picked it up and opened it. Inside was a single word: *Trust.*

I read it. Ellison read it over my shoulder. Merritt did not look.

“Trust,” Ellison said. “What does that mean?”

“I do not know.”

I folded the paper and put it in my pocket. I did not know whether it was the instruction or part of the task. I did not know whether it was meant for me or for anyone who found it.

We walked back to the office. The clerk was at his desk, his ledger open, his pencil in his hand. He looked up when we entered.

“You have found something,” he said.

“We found a paper,” I said. “It said ‘Trust.’”

He wrote something in the ledger. He did not look surprised.

“You may go,” he said.

We went. I walked home slowly. I did not know what the word meant. I did not know whether it was a test or a message or a trick.

I sat in the chair by the window and thought about it. *Trust.* I thought about Ellison, who had searched the floorboards. I thought about Merritt, who had pointed to the mark. I thought about the man who had found the book before me.

I did not know whom I could trust.

The next morning, I went to the market again. I had not planned to go. I had simply found myself walking, and the stalls were open, and the lanes were full. I bought nothing. I walked the length of the market and was turning back when I saw Peale.

He was standing by the chandler’s, the same place he had stood before. He was not looking at the sky this time. He was looking at me.

I walked over to him.

“You have something to ask,” he said.

I did not deny it. “The word. ‘Trust.’ Do you know what it means?”

“I know what it says,” he said. “That is not the same thing.”

“Do you know why it was there?”

He was silent for a moment. Then he said, “I have seen the mark before. On a door in the old part of town. The house has been empty for years, but the mark is still there, carved into the wood.”

“Do you know who put it there?”

“No,” he said. “But it is not a mark I have seen the office use. It looks older.”

I did not know whether to believe him. He was not smiling. He was not threatening. He was simply offering a fact.

“Why would you tell me that?” I said.

“Because I want to know what you found in the garden,” he said. “The book. I want to know what was written in it.”

“I did not read it,” I said. “I returned it.”

“Then tell me what you remember of it. The binding. The ink. The way it was placed.”

I thought about it. I had held the book for only a few minutes. I had not examined it carefully. But I remembered the dark leather, the brown ink, the way it had been half buried in the soil.

“It was dark leather,” I said. “The ink was brown. It was placed in the garden, half buried in the soil near the vegetable patch.”

Peale listened. He did not write anything down. He simply listened.

“And the box?” he said. “The one you found at the mill?”

“Dark leather, tied with a cord. The ink looked similar to what I saw in the book, but I could not be certain it was the same hand.”

“You are certain?”

“I am certain of what I saw. What it means, I cannot say.”

He nodded slowly, as though weighing what I had given him.

“Then I will tell you this,” he said. “The door I mentioned is on Water Lane, near the old brewery. I do not know what you will find there. I have not opened it. But the mark is the same.”

He turned and walked away, his hands in his pockets, his steps even.

I stood in the market, watching him go. I did not know whether he had told me the truth. I did not know whether the door he mentioned existed. I only knew that he had given me a direction, and that the direction was a thing I could follow.

I walked home slowly. I thought about Peale, about the word *Trust*, about the door on Water Lane. I thought about what I had given him in return.

I did not know whether the bargain had been fair. I did not know whether the door would tell me anything. But I knew that I had learned where to look next, and that the price of learning was giving something away.

I did not know whether I would trust Peale.

But I knew the question would come again.
