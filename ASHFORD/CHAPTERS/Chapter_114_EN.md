The season came again to Ashford.

I had known it would come. I had known it for years, since the first winter, when I had stood at the end of the lane and watched the lamp burn at nine and heard the bell at the corner. The season came every year, from the thirteenth to the twenty-first of November, and it ceased on the twenty-second.

I had kept the account through four seasons now.

I had watched the lamp burn, and the bell come, and the house at the end of the lane keep itself through the season. I had walked the boundary, and the wall, and the passage. I had kept the door closed, and the bell silent, and the houses kept.

The season came again.

And this year, I understood it differently.

---

I stood at the end of the lane on the thirteenth of November.

The light was failing. The town was settling into its evening. The lamps were being lit along the High Street, and the windows were taking light one by one.

The end house stood in its place, dark, with the rosemary in its pot at the sill.

I waited.

At nine, the lamp came.

It burned in the upper window, steady, at its old height. And then it was carried away along the rooms, the way it had been carried every year, without a servant's voice, without footsteps.

I watched it.

I had watched it for years. I had noted it. I had written it in the red book.

I had not understood it.

Now I understood it differently.

The lamp was not a ghost. It was not a haunting. It was a keeping â€” a keeping that had stood for longer than the town had a name.

And the keeping was part of the count.

I could not yet tell what the count was counting. But I knew that the lamp was part of it.

The lamp burned at nine. The bell came when the lamp did not. The house kept itself through the season.

All of it, part of the count.

I stood at the end of the lane, with the object warm against my chest, and I understood that I was watching the count.

---

I went to the common on the night of the fifteenth.

The house was there.

It stood at the edge of the common, where the grass met the lower pasture, its windows dark, its front door open.

I had entered it once, in the first year. I had found the clock stopped at 00:00. I had heard the three knocks from beneath the floor. I had not entered it again.

I stood at the edge of the common now, in the dark, and I looked at the house.

It had appeared, and disappeared, for years. It had left only the flattened grass, and the keeper's sign pressed into the earth.

I did not approach it.

I stood at the edge of the common, with the object warm against my chest, and I looked at the house.

The object was warm.

I had not felt it warm at the common for a long time â€” not since the first year, when I had carried the object to the keeper's sign in the earth, and it had warmed, and I had understood that the first sign responded to the keeper's sign.

It warmed now.

It warmed at the edge of the common, where the house appeared.

I stood in the dark, and I looked at the house, and I understood that the house was part of the count.

The house appeared. The house disappeared. The house kept something â€” the first sign, carved into its wall, hidden where only the object could find it.

The house was part of the count.

I did not enter it.

I stood at the edge of the common, and I watched the house, and I waited.

I went to the tower on the night of the twentieth.

I had the key. I had always had the key â€” the keeper's key, to the tower, to the bell.

I climbed the stairs. I stood in the bell chamber.

The bell hung in the dark, silent, the first sign on its rim.

I had not rung it. I had not needed to ring it. The bell was the keeper's bell, and it was kept, and it was not rung.

I stood in the bell chamber, in the dark, and I looked at the bell.

The first sign was on its rim.

The bell was part of the count.

I did not ring it.

I stood in the bell chamber, and I understood that I was watching the count.

---

The season ended on the twenty-second of November.

The lamp came, and the bell came, and the house kept itself through the season. And on the twenty-second, it ceased.

I stood at the end of the lane on the morning of the twenty-second, and I looked at the end house, and I understood that the season had passed.

The count had turned.

I did not know what the count was counting. I did not know how many seasons it had counted. I could not say how many were left.

But I knew that I was part of it.

I walked home, and I sat at my desk, and I opened the red book, and I wrote:

*The season has passed. The lamp burned, and the bell came, and the house kept itself through the season.*

*I watched it differently this year.*

*The lamp is part of the count. The bell is part of the count. The house is part of the count. The object is part of the count. The stones are part of the count.*

*I do not know what the count is counting.*

*But I know that I am part of it.*

*And I know that the count is older than the keeping.*

*I will learn what it is counting.*

*The season has passed.*

*And the count has turned.*
