# Chapter 3 — The Man Who Talks Too Much

I was beginning to recognise the sounds of the house. Not because I had lived there long—only three days—but an old house has its own habits. The floorboards in the front hallway creaked beneath certain steps. The kitchen pipe gave a faint hiss for a few moments after the tap was closed. The dining-room window trembled slightly when the wind came from the east. Small things. Trivial things. Yet when living alone in a house that had not yet truly begun to feel like home, such small things became hard to ignore.

I was making coffee when a knock came at the door. Three times. I set down the cup. When I opened it, Daniel stood there. He wore the same jacket as the day before, carried a shoulder bag that appeared far too full, and held two books under his arm.

"Good morning."

"You're very early."

"I know."

"You said yesterday you had a class."

"I did."

"So?"

"It was cancelled."

I waited for an explanation. Daniel shrugged. "The professor is ill."

"You look pleased."

"I am pleased."

He then looked past me. "Have you had breakfast?"

"Not yet."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"Because I haven't had breakfast either."

I looked at him. He smiled. "I know a place."

Ten minutes later we were walking towards Margaret's. I could have had breakfast at home, but after inspecting the larder, I decided that going with Daniel was not a bad choice.

"So," said Daniel, "what did you do yesterday?"

"I walked."

"A desk?"

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Haven't found one I like."

"You're too particular."

"I've only been here three days."

"That's enough for a start."

"For you, perhaps."

Daniel laughed. "You're harder to compromise with than you look."

"I simply don't want to buy a desk I'll use for years just because it's cheap."

"Fair enough."

We walked a few steps in silence. Then Daniel said: "I have a desk."

I turned.

"At home?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"It's full of books."

I laughed. "Then it's not a desk."

"Technically it still is."

"Technically."

That morning, Bellweather Lane had changed from the quiet street I had first seen. Some curtains were open. An old man was watering plants in front of his house. Two schoolchildren walked towards the stagecoach stop carrying bags almost as large as themselves. A baker's cart stopped in front of a small shop at the end of the road. I was beginning to recognise some of the houses. Not because I had intentionally memorised them, but because after a few days of walking, shapes that had once been unfamiliar were beginning to find their places in my memory. The house with the white fence. The house with the large tree. The small grocery. The stagecoach stop. The junction towards High Street. Alderwick was beginning to transform from a map into a place.

We continued walking. The closer we came to the town centre, the busier the street became. Shops were opening their doors. Lamps behind the glass were lit even though the sun was already bright enough. A newsboy arranged the morning papers. A stagecoach stopped at the stand. A few Academy students alighted.

I began to notice a pattern I had not fully realised before. Morning in Alderwick had its own sound. The braking of a stagecoach. The opening of shop doors. Crates being unloaded from carts. Music from a newly opened shop. The footsteps of people on the still-damp pavement. There was no heavy traffic. No tall buildings. But the town centre was never truly quiet. Alderwick might be small compared to the city where I had lived before, but small did not mean empty.

Margaret's was already fairly busy when we arrived. Margaret saw us from behind the counter. "He's brought trouble again."

Daniel pointed at me. "He invited me."

Margaret looked at me. "Is that true?"

"I did."

"Good."

She picked up two menus. "Sit down. I know what you'll order."

Daniel sat. I followed. "Do you come here often?" I asked.

"Almost every day."

"Why?"

"Because I can't cook."

Margaret interjected from a distance. "He also can't make tea."

Daniel raised his voice. "That's not true."

"Your tea once made me ill."

"Just once."

"The most memorable one."

I laughed. Daniel shook his head. "She never lets anything pass."

"Because those who forget their mistakes will repeat them."

Margaret brought two cups and placed them on the table.

"Learn from mistakes."

Daniel looked at his cup. "This is coffee?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because yesterday you said my tea was bad."

Daniel immediately took the cup. "I never said that."

Margaret smiled. "True. I said it." She walked away.

I looked at Daniel. "Is she always like that?"

"Every day."

"And you still come."

"As I said, I can't cook."

I laughed.

Margaret's felt different that morning. Perhaps because I had come with Daniel. Or perhaps because now I was beginning to recognise the people inside.

The old man near the window was still reading his newspaper.

The two women I had seen before were talking while comparing shopping lists.

A man in a dark coat sat alone for a few minutes, finishing his coffee, then left with his paper.

At another table, two Academy students opened their notebooks and discussed before class.

This place was more than just an eating-house. People came for breakfast, to read, to meet, to wait—to exchange news. Margaret knew who came and for what purpose. I was beginning to understand why Daniel called it the unofficial information hub. If something happened in Alderwick, someone would talk about it here.

We had breakfast. Daniel opened one of the books he had brought. I recognised the cover.

"Is that not the book from yesterday?"

"No."

"How many books did you bring?"

"Two."

"For what?"

"One for class."

"The other?"

"For myself."

I looked at the book. "History again?"

"A novel."

I was surprised. "You read novels?"

Daniel looked at me as though I had said something deeply offensive.

"Of course."

"I thought you only read history books."

"If I read history all day, I'd become a very dull person."

"You talk quite enough already without the help of history books."

He pointed at me. "Personal attack."

"Observation."

"Worse."

I smiled.

He opened his novel.

"Good?"

"Do you like it?"

"Don't know yet."

"Why read it, then?"

"Because I want to know."

"And if you don't like it?"

"I'll stop."

I nodded. Simple. Daniel had a great deal of curiosity. Sometimes too much.

I looked around again. Near the counter was a small rack of newspapers and magazines. Mostly local papers.

I picked up a local paper. The main headline: roads to be repaired in the north end. The rest was rentals, jobs, the winter market—the ordinary business of a town that had to keep running. I folded it. In a small place, news was not always grand; it was the accumulation of small things. That felt honest.

After breakfast, Daniel said he wanted to walk toward the market square. I followed. I had a question of my own: what did Bell's old maps say about Bellweather Lane before the house lost its number?

We passed an old stone building.

Daniel pointed. "This used to be the post office."

I looked at the old nameplate still attached to the side of the building.

"Since when?"

"Around the beginning of this century."

"Are you sure?"

Daniel stopped. "Yes."

I looked at the board. The paint had faded. There was a small inscription below the old name of the building. I moved closer.

"Then why is the date there different?"

Daniel followed the direction of my finger. He read. Paused. Then took out his notebook from his bag.

"Wait."

I waited. He opened a few pages.

"Ah."

"Ah?"

"Right." He looked at the board again. "The building is older than I thought."

I smiled. "So you don't know everything."

"I never said I knew everything."

"Yesterday you said you knew more than most people."

"And that's still true."

I laughed. Daniel looked at the building again. Then smiled. "I'll find out later."

I liked that answer. He didn't feel defeated when he found something he didn't know. He just became more interested.

We stopped for a moment on the pavement. The former post office stood between two much newer buildings. At its base now sat a small shop selling stationery and paper. Yet the shape of the building was still clearly visible. Its entrance was tall. The upper windows were narrow. The stone around the frames had changed colour with age. A small plaque was fixed to the wall. Not too conspicuous. I read the date. 1798.

"So the post office isn't the original building?"

Daniel opened his notes again. "It seems not."

"What does that mean?"

"According to these notes, the building was originally a small administration office. Then it was expanded."

"Then?"

"It became the post office."

I nodded. "And now a stationer's."

"Yes."

I looked at the building again. Three functions. Perhaps more. Alderwick was beginning to look like a town that kept layers beneath every building.

We continued walking. Daniel talked about the town's history. I listened more than I spoke. Sometimes he was right. Sometimes he was merely guessing. Sometimes he quoted something he had once read. And when he was not sure, he usually said: "I don't know." That made him easier to trust.

We came to the narrow lane where the old town began to climb behind the market square. Daniel stopped.

"Bell's," he said.

I looked up the lane. The building stood at the top, older than the ones around it. A sign hung above the door, half hidden by ivy. The door itself was painted green.

"Arthur's shop," Daniel said. "Some of the older men call him Sir Arthur. I never found out why—probably some old joke that outlived its meaning."

"He doesn't seem like a knight," I said.

Daniel shrugged. "That's what makes it a good joke, I suppose."

I tried the handle.

It did not move.

Daniel waited. I stepped back and looked through the window.

Inside, the shelves were crowded with books, some upright, some stacked, some placed wherever there had been room. I had seen this before—two days ago, from outside. I had stood in the same spot then, looking in at books I could not reach. No one had been inside. I had stood a moment and stepped back, telling myself I would come again.

I had not come again until now.

Daniel looked over my shoulder. "You've seen it before."

"Yesterday."

"And?"

"Nothing."

He looked where I looked. "There are too many of them," he said after a moment.

"Books?"

"Books. And the other things. They don't seem arranged by subject. Or by size. Or by date."

"Or by anything I can see."

He tilted his head. "That is the point, perhaps."

I looked at him. "You think someone arranged them that way on purpose."

"I think someone arranged them for someone who already knew how to find things."

I looked back through the glass. The shelves rose on both sides of the narrow room. Maps of Alderwick hung on the walls in frames of dark wood. There were prints of buildings long since altered. There were boxes on the lower shelves, their contents visible only as edges and corners. And there were books—a great many books—laid horizontally, set upright, stacked in narrow columns, as if the man who placed them had had a system he did not wish to explain to strangers.

"I don't know," Daniel said. "But I have never seen a shop that looked like this."

"You may not be the intended reader."

Daniel laughed. I smiled.

We stood there a moment longer. Then Daniel lowered his voice.

"Some of those books have been there a very long time."

"How can you tell?"

"By the dust. By the way the light falls on the shelves. By the care with which certain things are placed and others are not."

I did not answer. He was guessing. He said so himself, in everything but words. But he was the kind of person who guessed well.

We went on.

The lane was steep. The shops here were smaller. Some still used family name boards. A tailor's shop with fabric displayed in the window. A hardware shop showing tools behind the glass. A flower shop with buckets set out on the pavement. Among them stood old houses whose front sections had been turned into shops. Alderwick had no clear boundary between commercial and residential areas. The two mixed. People could live on the upper floors and open a shop below. A family could run the same business for several generations. And a building that once had a different function could be reused without entirely losing its old shape.

We walked in silence for a while. Then Daniel said: "You know, Margaret will be pleased."

"Why?"

"Because now she has new material to tease me with."

"Why?"

"Because I'm usually the one who buys the most books."

I smiled. "Perhaps I could take your place."

"Don't."

"Why?"

"That's the only reputation I have left."

I laughed.

We walked along the road leading back to Market Square. Lunchtime made the town centre busier. Some workers left their offices. Academy students filled the small eating-houses. A queue formed in front of the bakery. A newsboy shouted offering the afternoon edition. Stagecoaches came and went every few minutes. I was beginning to recognise some routes. The stop. High Street. Market Square. The Academy. North Alderwick. Those routes were slowly becoming part of the mental map in my head. Alderwick was beginning to have a direction.

We stopped at a bench near the river. Daniel opened his novel. I read the newspaper. A few minutes passed.

"Why did you move here?" he asked.

I lowered the paper. "You already asked that yesterday."

"I know."

"So?"

"I'm curious."

I thought for a moment. "Because of work."

"That's a practical reason."

"Yes."

"And the real reason?"

I looked at the river. "I wanted a new place."

Daniel did not answer immediately. Then he nodded. "I understand."

"Do you?"

"A little." He closed his book. "Sometimes old places remind us too much of something."

I looked at him. He looked at the river. For the first time since I had known him, Daniel was quiet. I did not ask further. He did not explain either. A few seconds later he opened his book again.

"By the way, you should try the pub near the stop."

I laughed. "We were just talking about something serious."

"I know."

"And you changed the subject?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I don't like serious conversations to last too long."

I shook my head. "At least you're honest."

The river itself was one part of the town I had not paid much attention to before. Its water was not wide. Yet its course cut through several parts of Alderwick and formed a natural boundary between the town centre and some residential areas. Along its banks ran a footpath. Some wooden benches. Old trees. And a stone bridge connecting the two sides of the town. A few people walked their dogs. An old man sat fishing. Two children ran past us. No one treated it as a landmark. To them, it was just part of the way home. Perhaps that was how a town kept an important place. Not always through monuments. Sometimes through something that had become too ordinary to notice.

We returned to Margaret's that afternoon. Margaret immediately saw the book bag in Daniel's hand.

"Ah." She smiled broadly. "Already influenced by someone."

Daniel pointed at himself. "He chose."

"Is that so?"

"Yes."

Margaret looked at Daniel. "Good. That means you have competition now."

"Don't start."

Margaret laughed.

I filed that information away. Not because it was important. Only because I was beginning to notice that everyone in Alderwick had a different way of judging others. Daniel by knowledge. Margaret by habit and relationships. At least, that was my impression so far.

Margaret's grew busier as the afternoon wore on. A mother came with her child. Two workers from the shop across the street sat at the table near the window. An Academy student came in only to buy tea and took the cup away. Margaret remembered almost everyone's orders. I began to understand that this café was not merely a place to eat. There was a kind of flow of people passing through it all day long. Morning for breakfast and the newspaper. Midday for workers and Academy students. Afternoon for those who wanted to sit a while before going home. If Alderwick had a place to hear the town's conversations without having to stand in the middle of the street, this was probably one of them.

Daniel sat beside me. "I want to ask you something."

"What?"

"Do you believe everything people tell you here?"

I looked at him. "Why?"

"Because Alderwick has a lot of stories."

"Every town does."

"True." He took his cup. "But in a small town, the stories are closer."

I waited.

"People know who owns the old houses. Who married whom. Who moved. Who died. Who used to own a shop."

I nodded.

"And?"

"Sometimes people mix story with fact."

I smiled. "That's not new."

"No." He looked out the window. "That's why I like history."

"Because you can distinguish?"

"Because we can look for evidence."

I recalled the archives at the library.

"What if the evidence is wrong?"

Daniel smiled. "Well." He pointed at me. "That's a good question."

Towards evening I returned home. I opened the door. The house was still quiet. Helen was not there. I put the shopping in the kitchen. Then I walked from one room to another. For the first time I looked at this house more carefully. There were many rooms I had not used. The parlour. The dining room. The reading room. The kitchen. The back room. A closed door beneath the stairs. I tried to open it. Locked. I did not have the key. I put my hand in my pocket. The room key. The house key. Nothing. I remembered Helen's words. Don't ask too many questions about this house. I smiled. "Then don't give me a locked door." I did not force it. Not yet. I returned to the parlour.

Night fell. I cooked a simple supper. Then sat in the parlour reading the newspaper. Nothing unusual happened. No sounds. No footsteps. I began to feel a little foolish. Perhaps I had been too tired yesterday. Perhaps the things that had happened yesterday were mere coincidences. I opened the book from upstairs—the record Helen had left on the table before I came down. I had not finished it. There were pages I had turned without reading. I found one of them.

Nothing.

I closed it.

Then something caught my attention. Between two entries near the middle of the book, someone had drawn a small symbol in the margin. A circle. Three lines like branches. One dot at the bottom.

I stopped.

I knew it.

I had seen this mark before. Two days earlier, in the same book, on its final page. I had stared at it for a long time and written nothing about it in my notebook. I had told myself there was no point pretending that seeing the same mark in several places meant I understood it.

Now it was here again.

I turned the page. Nothing more. Back to the previous page. The symbol was still there, still, as if waiting. I touched it with the tip of my finger. The ink was slightly darker than the surrounding paper—as if deliberately placed. Perhaps an owner's mark. Perhaps a note to a reader who had once been expected to understand it. Perhaps something else.

I closed the book. There was no reason to think about it further.

I stood and carried the book to my room. I placed it on the table. Then opened my notebook. I looked at yesterday's notes.

I almost added the symbol just now.

My hand stopped.

No.

Not yet.

I knew the mark. I had seen it twice in the same book. I did not know what it meant. There was a difference between recognition and understanding, and I had no wish to confuse them.

I closed the notebook. Then I put out the lamp.

The day ended without any incident worth noting.

At least, nothing important enough to record.

Tomorrow I still had not yet started work. I still had a few days to get to know this town. To find a shop selling a writing desk. To find a cheaper place to eat. Perhaps to meet Daniel again. Perhaps to go back to Bell's when the door was open. Ordinary things.

I lay down. Before sleep, my mind returned to the symbol. I knew where I had seen it. The record book upstairs. Twice. I could not say I understood anything more than that. I closed my eyes.

Tomorrow, then.

Not everything needed an answer today.

And without my noticing, the record book I had left on the table was still open to the same page.

Between two entries about old Alderwick's history…

the small symbol remained there.
