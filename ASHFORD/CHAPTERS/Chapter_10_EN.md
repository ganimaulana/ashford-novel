# Chapter 10 — A Name on a Sheet

The letter to Pembroke & Clarke took me the better part of a morning, partly because I wrote it twice and partly because I wanted it to do two things without appearing to ask for either.

The first thing was the common thing: a gentleman acknowledging the receipt of papers touching an inheritance, wishing rather to be satisfied on points than to trouble them, and asking — plainly — whether the house had ever in its knowledge received any letter marked *Bellweather House*, or dated about Monday last, or at any time addressed to a name rather than to a man. If such a thing had gone through their offices it had been done without their knowledge, which I hoped, in the writing of it, that they would believe I believed.

The second thing asked for less and meant more: I requested the full copy of the will, not the extract, and the name of the executor at whose instance the office had written to me, so that as the affairs of my late relative must be settled I might know by what hand they had been opened. A man who pays the charges, I reasoned, is a man who is owed the papers. If they would not send the papers, they would tell me the name; and if they would not tell me the name, then I should know, by the shape of a silence from four hundred miles off, something about what that name was worth.

I sealed it with plain wax, having no coat of arms to suppose useful and none of my own to use.

Down at the post office the man kept one ledger, called his son's boy his cousin — the errand runner of Monday was not named but, when described, could not not be recognised: a tallish lad of perhaps twelve or thirteen, quick of eye, who delivered the letters that wanted no frank for the lane.

"That's my nephew," the postmaster said, with the particular tone of a man owning a fault in the family. "He goes where he's sent and carries what he's given, and I'm the one that says every night where what he's given was took from. But it's the lane you're asking about, sir, and I'll say this, and no more: there's hours on the lane. There's the morning, and the afternoon, and it's after the lamps that the lane belongs to itself, and it's then my cousin runs his errand and takes no more notice of what's given him than of the water in the kennel. Ask him. He's just about at his dinner."

He was fetched accordingly, in the manner of errand boys everywhere, reluctantly, with a mouth already forming the answer that had already been denied — and at sight of me he took off his hat, which was neither courtesy nor impudence being the thing boys do before men they have carried letters to.

"Do you know what you carry into Bellweather Lane in the evenings?"

"Yes, sir."

"Do you know who gives it you?"

"No, sir. There's gentlemen, sir."

He said it in the tone of one who considered the matter closed, and the postmaster said, "Go back to your dinner" — and the boy was gone along the passage with the particular air of a young person who had got the better of the affair. That was all of it; but I did not think it little: *there's gentlemen.* A letter given in the evening by someone to someone who hires a child whose uncle is the postmaster, and the uncle knowing, or not knowing, how children are employed on the lane by after dark.

I walked out with the morning on my hands, and thought the boy was the third watchful witness of the week; and then I considered that *witness* was perhaps too handsome a word; that a boy is a boy, and that I was beginning to assemble the town into parties.

---

The office of the rolls is in a room at the east end of the town hall, kept by an ancient officer who has read so much paper that he regards living men as a lesser argument. When I showed him the copy of the copy, and said how the library's Bellweather papers had been drawn out of their shelf, and asked whether the town held yet other record of the like, he put on a second pair of spectacles to look at the first pair's work, and sent for the original.

It came out of a tin case; the case out of a chest; the chest had been unlocked out of a press where the keys have not turned a dozen times in my time, he said. And when the volume was open at the page I had come for, the officer's face did a thing I thought he was not made for. It put on interest.

"Sit down, sir," he said.

"The page is wrong," he said, before I could put the question. "You see a fair leaf, with the name scraped out of it. That is not the page I knew. The ledger was bound in the year ninety-three; I was young, but I'd see the book again before you, sir, and say the paper. That's not that paper."

He took the leaf up to the window, held it a certain way — and I saw what he saw, later by the light through it, a shade different in grain and date from the leaves beside it, and at the inner foot of the page one stitch-hole where a stitch had once been.

"Some hand," he said, "has taken this book to pieces, and put it together again, and not altogether with a binder's skill. It is the work of one that has done the like, sir, but by candle, and in haste — a year, two years — these twenty, say — I couldn't tell you to half a dozen, sir, but not longer than the town has had the book, nor so old as you'd wish it to be."

I had a name scraped from a page; and the page itself, I now learned, was a new insertion. The man who took the trouble — cutting a leaf from a bound town record and fitting another in its place — had done it recently enough to want, as it were, to be living on the side of it when it was found out; which was, I thought, a strange kind of courage, and stranger still that it had been practised so quiet a long while — for it had been found out now, and the officer seemed more provoked than shocked.

He made no comment of any. He wrote for me a certificate of the fact, with the date and the two hands of him and of the book, and the charge of it I paid; it was not a sum to ruin a man, but it was not a sum a man could afford to part with often, and I noted the weight of it in my purse as I walked away. And it occurred to me, walking away, that it was the first paper written about Bellweather House — and indeed about me — that had passed out of the town in living memory.

---

There was a hand-bill on the church door, and I did not know the thing until I was halfway down the steps and the whole town seemed to know it, but it was a fair thing and it said it plain.

*At the present period, when the parts about London are discomposed by meetings of thousands upon thousands, and atrocious doctrines* — it was the magistrate's pen, I judged, though the paper did not name him, for there was a sound to that word *discomposed* which means the person writing it is not, in the event, composed —

It called upon the respectable householders to enroll themselves in a voluntary association for the preservation of order in Ashford, and to give their names to the constable within the week, and to be steady men, and not to be led by designs to bring the peace, so lately preserved by Providence, into hazard.

I stood in the fog a long time looking at it; the verger's church door and the magistrate's sheet, with a crowd going by it, which is the fate of all notices in the market of a Wednesday.

"They want the names against the price," said the woman at the pump by the steps, without looking up from the water, when no one had spoken to her — or perhaps about the weather, since the people about pumps speak to whatever is there. But it's what we do in a year that sees wheat sold at eight shillings the strike: "the price against them, and them against the price; and the sheet's for the price."

It was not politics. They never said it like politics. In the coffee house at noonday they said it plain: the price was up, the peace was safe, and the sheet of paper would keep it so: and Michael at two tables back held the pen before the book was opened, the way people read a menu — out of the corner of both eyes — and said *who'll have me?* to the room, and the room's laugh was not exactly no.

And I said nothing, and the coffee was not good, and then Daniel was late, and the boy took me no letters, and that morning I stood the only place in the town where I have been since that I meant to be: which is not a thing anyone in Ashford has asked me to be, yet: and I had a paper in my pocket the copy of a scraped name; for I had carried out of the town hall a page's date, and the fact that someone within two years had undone a binding; and the two pieces together meant this: the eraser was here now, and walks the same four streets, and had had at one time the book itself, out of a press that was unlocked.

By the time of the post office my name was on the sheet.

Daniel told me so, in the street, out of breath.

"They've sent your letter round the lane," he said. "It goes to the constable by the afternoon, and the constable goes by the evening. It's the way things are done. He knows to ask the new men."

"I've given him no reason to ask me."

"Your name has that on it already," Daniel said — and then, because Daniel is the sort of man who knows the moment he has said too much: "What I don't mean to say is that I want it asked. What I mean is that if you're on a list with Michael, then you're on Michael's side of whatever comes; and if your name's down there by Michael and the man at the bell and the squire's people, that's a sort of address. And that'll do for the one who gives a nameless note to his cousin to carry, to look up a man's address and see if he's there."

It is a thing I have since learned about Ashford and about the sheet: that lists are a sort of book with a binding, and you would think the paper's not much, and you'd be right, to think so: and you'd be right, too, that when the names are all down on the same sheet the writing of them is the paper's. I put my name; and there it is, in the hand of the constable's clerk, in the town's own list, among the people it would never have entered my head in London to want to be reckoned with: and if it means nothing it means nothing, and if it means a thing then it has meant it this morning, and there's been a week the lane had been quiet of the letters, since the day it asked, by the lamp, what I was.

---

It did not come that night. There was, I began to believe, a reason for the quiet of the town being the quiet of the light, and the light not.

I went home again by the water, and stood on the old stone, and at last gave up walking, about dusk — and on my way along the lane the first lamps shone; and of the two houses on the further side — the one by Helen's, at eleven, the other, with the gate of Marlowe, that's said to come up from the garden of the house of which we speak, I looked straight on, up to the end; and I saw it on; and it lit the window of the end; and a man in his place and his own hour that I have not known; and as I watched, as before, it moved; and behind it, on the sill, I made out with the glass the small common shape — the lamp's own shape, turned the other way, a hand behind it.

And then it carried out of the reach, and the window was dark as usual, and the glass was only a glass that keeps the night's own reflection the way water does; and what I saw there was me; and it did not frighten me that it had frightened me.

It was the verger who met me, late, at the turning, on the road up from the churchyard, with his lantern low; and I had no wish to be seen, as I thought it, by a person of the church; and the verger looked over my shoulder, the verger said:

"Light, sir."

"I see it."

"I'm meaning, sir, a thing the bell can't do is a thing the light do. There's no one to be seen in a light going up that stairs by itself, sir." He looked up into the dark, with the lantern low, as if the glass were doing the listening. "But I'll tell you what: there's some one been, sir; and the tower door last night been locked."

"Is it not locked every night?"

"By my hand," said the verger, with the particular pride of a small man in the particular use of such keys as he has. "And it's the church bell's rung by neither. And that's what the two of us are now standing here to ask ourselves, sir, and you, sir, and no one else in this town will ask, or if they asks it, it's not in the tone, sir, in which God knows they'll get no answer."

He wished me good night, going down the one road, and went up to bed in the vestry, where the sleep does, as he'd sworn; and I went in, and the lamp on the sideboard burned at a height that wanted trimming, and the note I had written to London was somewhere on the road towards a franking, and what I had said was done: and the whole thing that I wrote, with the ink and the light and the hand of the boy who is paid by the unknown, in that house that was not empty —

and I sat down to put it in the book, and could not, for the life of my pen; for the whole thing was not true till I had wrote it:

I do not, I wrote, know what the town will do, in case the truth is found out — but the town is in the act, that same, of not thinking nothing; — and the first thing in Ashford that has stopped thinking nothing, as I understand, is the bell.

I shut the book and sat a moment with my hand still on it. The cold from the bell had not quite left my fingers, though I had not touched it for hours. I did not think much of it — the weather, the late hour, the strain of the week's watching. But I noted it, as I note all things that do not fit, and let it stand.
