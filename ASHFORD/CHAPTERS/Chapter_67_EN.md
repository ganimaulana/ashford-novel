CHAPTER 67 — THE COLD MEDALLION

I carried the second medallion for three days before I took it to the common.

I could not yet tell why I waited. I had taken my own medallion to the common, and it had warmed, and the house had answered it, and the door had knocked. I had expected the second medallion to do the same — or to do something different — but I had not taken it. I had waited.

I had sat at my desk, with both medallions before me, and I had looked at them, and I had not known what to do with the second.

I had carried my own medallion all my life. The second had been kept. I did not know whether it was meant to be carried, or whether it was meant to stay in the house at the end of the lane, or whether I had taken it from its place and broken something I did not understand.

On the third day, I decided. I took the second medallion — the cold one — and I went to the common.

The house was not there. I had not expected it to be. I knelt at the edge of the grass, where the keeper's sign was pressed into the earth, and I held the second medallion over it. It did not warm. I held it there for a long time, waiting. It stayed cold.

I set it down on the grass, at the centre of the keeper's sign. I waited. It did not warm. I picked it up. I put it back in my coat.

I took out my own medallion — the warm one — and I held it over the same spot. It warmed.

I sat at the edge of the common, in the grey light, with one warm medallion in my hand and one cold medallion in my coat, and I understood that the second medallion did not answer the keeper's sign. My medallion answered. The second did not.

I did not know what that meant. I put my own medallion back in my coat. I sat there, with both medallions against my chest, one warm and one cold, and I thought about the difference.

My medallion had been carried. The second had been kept. My medallion had been taken to the common, and to the house, and to the door, and it had answered each time. The second had been kept in a box, in a windowless room, at the end of a forgotten lane, and it had not answered anything.

I did not know whether the second medallion was meant to be carried, or whether it was meant to be kept, or whether I had done the wrong thing by taking it from its box.

I stood. I walked home. I opened the drawer. I took out the red book. I wrote:

The second medallion does not warm at the keeper's sign. My medallion does. They are identical, but they are not the same.

I have carried mine all my life. The second was kept in a house at the end of Bellweather Lane, kept empty, kept closed, kept forgotten.

I do not know whether the second medallion is meant to be carried, or whether it is meant to be kept. I have taken it from its place. I do not know whether that was right.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer. I took out the second medallion and held it in my hand. It was cold. I had carried it for three days, and it had not warmed against my skin. I had carried my own medallion for years, and I had never noticed it warm. I had not known it was warm until I had brought it to the common and felt it change. The second medallion did not change.

I looked at it. The first sign — the circle, the three lines, the dot. The same as mine. The same metal. The same age. But cold.

I did not know what it was. I did not know why it had been kept. I did not know whether I had been meant to find it, or whether I had taken something that should not have been taken.

I sat in the dark, with the cold medallion in my hand, and I thought about the house at the end of the lane, and the box on the table, and the windowless room, and the years of silence. The second medallion had been kept for a reason. I did not know the reason. I had taken it. I could not put it back.

I did not know whether I was meant to carry it, or whether I was meant to keep it, or whether I was meant to do something else with it. I put it in the drawer, beside the red book, and the agreement, and the map, and my notebook. I left my own medallion on the desk.

I did not know what to do with the second. But I knew that I would not leave it in the drawer forever. It had been kept for too long. I would carry it. I would carry both. And I would find out what the difference meant.

---

The next day, I went to the vestry. I took the second medallion with me. I placed it on the table before the clerk.

He looked at it. He did not touch it.

"There is a second," he said.

"There is. I found it at the end of Bellweather Lane, in a house that has been kept empty, in a box, in a windowless room."

The clerk was silent.

"It is cold," I said. "My medallion warms at the keeper's sign. This one does not."

"The first medallion was carried," he said slowly. "The second was kept. That is the difference."

"Then what is the second for?"

He did not answer at once.

"I do not know," he said. "I have no record of two medallions. I have no record of a second. The first sign is the first sign. I have never heard of a second."

"Then why was it kept?"

"Because it was meant to be found," he said. "By you. At the end of Bellweather Lane, in a house that has been kept empty, in a box, in a windowless room — waiting for the keeper who would find it."

He looked at me. "The first medallion was carried. The second was kept. One was carried so that the keeper would know the first sign. The other was kept so that the keeper would know there was more than one."

I stood in the vestry, with the cold medallion in my hand. The clerk had not known. No one had known. The second medallion had been kept, in a house at the end of a forgotten lane, in a box, in a windowless room, waiting for the keeper who would find it.

I had found it. I had carried it. I had not known what it was. But I had found it.

And I understood, standing in the vestry, that the keeping was not over. The second medallion had been kept for a reason. I did not know the reason. But I had found it. And I would carry it. And I would find out what the second medallion was for.

I put it in my coat, beside my own. Two medallions. One warm, one cold. I carried them both. I was the keeper of both.
