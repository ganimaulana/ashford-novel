# Chapter 61 — Eleanor Shaw

The day after my conversation with Crowe, I walked through the streets of Ashford with the name Eleanor Shaw turning over in my mind. I had not met her, but I knew that I would. Calloway had said she was at the mill, and at the house at the end of the lane, and with Crowe. She was one of them. And she had been watching me, as I had been watching the lane.

I found her on the twenty-seventh of February, in the market square, buying apples from a stall. The square was busy that morning, the stalls full of winter vegetables and salted meat, the air thick with the smell of wet wool and woodsmoke. She stood at the stall with her back to me, and I saw her before she saw me: a woman of perhaps thirty-five, with dark hair pinned up beneath a plain bonnet, and a coat that was well-made but not showy. She moved with the careful economy of someone who does not waste a gesture.

I approached her, and she turned as if she had known I would come. She looked at me the way a woman looks at a man she has been expecting, and she did not seem surprised.

"You are the man who has been helping the constable," she said. It was not a question.

"I am," I said.

She nodded, and she did not look away. "I have heard about you. You have been asking questions about the boundary, and about the house at the end of the lane, and about the bell that was sold." She paused, and her eyes moved over my face as if she were reading a document. "You are the keeper now, are you not?"

I did not answer, and she did not wait for an answer. She turned to the stall and paid the woman for the apples, and then she said, "Walk with me. There are too many ears in this square."

I fell into step beside her, and we walked out of the square, past the church, and down a narrow lane that led toward the river. She did not speak until we had reached the water's edge, where the sound of the current covered our words.

"I know who you are," she said. "And I know what you have found. The bell. The gear. The space beneath the mill. You have found the things that were hidden, and you have kept the agreement." She looked at me, and I saw that she was not afraid. She was curious. "You are the keeper of the boundary," she said. "But you do not know what the boundary is for. You do not know what is on the other side."

"Tell me," I said.

She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile of someone who has been carrying a secret for a long time and is tired of carrying it alone. "The boundary is not a wall," she said. "It is a seal. And the seal was made to keep something in, not to keep something out. The space beneath Ashford is not empty. It contains a thing—a thing that was placed there by the one who made the agreement." She paused, and I saw her weighing her next words. "I was not always a member of the Order," she said. "I was a scholar. I studied the old records of this town, the histories that no one reads, the deeds that no one touches. And I found the same thing you have found: the name Aurelius Ashcroft, written in places where it should not be, and absent from places where it should."

I stopped walking. "You know about Aurelius."

"I know about Aurelius," she said. "I know that he was the first keeper. I know that he made the agreement, and that he sealed the space, and that he built the house at the end of the lane. And I know that he did not leave Ashford. He disappeared. And I know that what he sealed was not a thing, but a part of himself." She looked at me, and her face was unreadable. "The Order believes that the seal should be opened. Not because we want chaos, but because we believe that the seal was a mistake. Aurelius Ashcroft was not a keeper. He was a prisoner, and he made the agreement to keep himself prisoner."

I stood by the river with the cold wind on my face, and I felt the weight of her words settle over me. "The one who made the agreement," I said. "Aurelius Ashcroft."

She did not answer, but I saw in her face that I was right.

We stood in silence for a long moment, and then she said, "I have told you more than I have told most. But I have told you because you are the keeper, and because the keeper must decide. The Order will ask you to open the seal. The agreement will ask you to keep it. And you will have to choose." She turned to me, and her eyes were steady. "When you decide, I will be there. Not to persuade you. To witness."

She walked away then, back toward the square, and I stood by the river with the weight of her words in my chest.

I went home and wrote in my book: *27 Feb. Eleanor Shaw has confirmed that the space beneath the town contains a thing—a thing that was placed there by Aurelius Ashcroft. The Order believes that the seal should be opened. I do not know what is inside, but I know that I must find out. She is not an enemy. She is a witness. And she is waiting.*

That night, I did not sleep well. I lay in bed and thought about Aurelius Ashcroft, about the house at the end of the lane, about the bell that was a lock and the gear that was a key. I thought about Eleanor Shaw, and her steady eyes, and her careful words. She had not asked me to join the Order. She had only asked me to decide.

And I knew that I would have to decide, whether I was ready or not.
