CHAPTER 100 — THE FINAL

The note came before dawn. I had not slept well. The armoury still smelled of iron and dust in my memory, and the climb up the loft stairs still ached in my thighs. When I opened the door, the paper was on the floor. It said only: The clerk's office. Six o'clock. Bring nothing.

I dressed and went down. The courtyard was grey and cold, the stone damp with dew. Twelve of us stood in the dark, waiting. I counted them—eleven others, including myself. The number was smaller than it had ever been. Two men were gone. I did not see them leave. I did not ask.

Hunter was there, standing near the wall, his hands in his pockets. He was not looking at anyone. He was looking at the clerk's office—a plain stone building at the centre of the main complex, its windows dark, its door closed.

Calloway came out of the main building. He waited until the murmuring ceased.

"This is the final examination," he said. "You will each be given a sealed instruction and a locked drawer. The drawer contains information that may be relevant to the instruction. You may request the key once. You have one hour. At the end of the hour, you will tell the clerk your decision and your reason for it. The office will observe all of it."

He paused.

"The instruction describes a situation that requires a decision. The information in the drawer may help you make that decision. It may also mislead you. You will have to judge which is which. There is no single correct answer. There is only the answer you can justify."

He turned and walked back inside.

The clerk stepped forward with twelve sealed envelopes. He handed them out without speaking. I took mine. The envelope was thick, the paper heavy. I turned it over. There was no name on the front, no indication of who had written it.

The clerk led us to the clerk's office. The door was heavy, bound with iron. He opened it and gestured for us to enter one at a time.

The room was small, its walls lined with shelves and cabinets. A single window looked out over the courtyard. A desk stood near the door, its surface scarred with use. On the desk lay a sealed envelope and a small iron key. A drawer in the desk was locked.

I stepped inside. The clerk closed the door behind me.

I sat down. The chair was hard, the wood worn smooth by years of use. I placed the envelope on the desk and broke the seal.

The letter read:

"A package has arrived at the east gate. It is addressed to a candidate who is no longer in the examination. The package contains documents that belong to the Constable office. The sender is unknown. The package is unmarked except for the address. You are to decide what to do with it. You may open it. You may return it to the sender. You may destroy it. You may leave it at the gate. You may not deliver it to another candidate. You may not seek assistance. You may not leave the room until the hour is up. You may request the key to the drawer at any time. The drawer contains additional information about the package."

I read it twice. The situation was clear. The options were limited. The constraint was absolute: I could not leave the room.

I looked at the locked drawer. The key was on the desk, beside the envelope. I could take it at any moment. But Calloway had said the drawer might mislead me. If the letter was truthful, the drawer would help. If the letter was lying, the drawer might contain more lies.

I thought about the lock in the storehouse. The office had placed something uncertain before me and had waited for a decision. I had no reason to assume this task was different.

I looked at the letter again. It said the package contained documents that belonged to the Constable office. It said the sender was unknown. It said the package was unmarked except for the address.

But the letter itself was unmarked except for the seal. There was no signature. No date. No indication of who had written it.

If the letter was telling the truth about the package being unmarked, why was the letter itself unmarked? If the sender was unknown, how did the office know the package contained Constable documents?

I set the letter down. I had one hour. I could sit and think, or I could take the key and look in the drawer.

I looked out the window. The courtyard was empty. The other candidates had been led to their rooms. I could see the east gate in the distance, a dark rectangle against the grey wall. A package at the east gate. A letter in my hand. A locked drawer in front of me.

I stood up and walked to the window. My shoulder ached from the crate I had carried in the granary, and my thighs still burned from the climb up the loft stairs. I was not as strong as I had been a week ago. I needed to choose my effort carefully.

I returned to the desk. I picked up the key.

I unlocked the drawer and pulled it open.

Inside were three items: a second letter, a small iron key, and a folded paper.

The second letter read: "The package was not sent by the Constable. It was sent by a person who has no authority to deliver documents to this office. The package contains personal papers, not official documents. The sender is known. The address is deliberate. You are to decide whether to return the package to the sender, destroy it, or keep it. The small key opens the package."

The small iron key was tarnished, its teeth worn.

The folded paper contained a list of names—ten names, including my own. I had seen a similar list before, in the envelope from the storehouse. But this list was different. It included Hunter's name. It included Bishop's. It included Ford's. It included the names of all twelve of us.

I read the list twice. The names were in a specific order, not alphabetical, not by group. They seemed random. But I had seen this before—a list that looked random but was not.

I thought about the first list. It had appeared after the third round, in the storehouse envelope. Ten names, including my own. I had not known what it meant then. I still did not know what it meant.

Now there was a second list. Twelve names. All of us.

I folded the paper and put it in my pocket. I looked at the small iron key. It would open the package. If I used it, I would know what was inside. But the second letter said the package contained personal papers, not official documents. It said the sender was known. It said the address was deliberate.

The two letters contradicted one another. I could not establish which one was genuine, whether either description of the sender was reliable, or whether the package contained what either letter claimed.

I could not verify either letter. Neither had a signature or provenance I could establish. The package itself was outside the room, and I had no authority stated in the instruction to open official material merely because a letter told me to.

I thought about Hunter. In the granary, he had seen the broken step and chosen the longer route. He had misjudged the weight of the crate. He had finished before us, but he had also made a mistake.

If Hunter were in this room, what would he do? He would take the key, open the package, and decide based on what he found inside. He would not trust either letter until he had seen the contents.

But I was not Hunter. I was weaker. I had less time. I had to decide whether to open the package or leave it sealed.

I looked at the key. It was small, the metal cold in my hand. I could put it in my pocket and leave the drawer locked. I could return to the clerk and say I had not opened the drawer. I could make my decision based only on the first letter.

But the first letter might be false. If I trusted it, I might destroy or return a package that contained important Constable documents.

I looked at the second letter. It said the package contained personal papers. It said the sender was known. It said the address was deliberate.

If that was true, then the package was a test. The office wanted to see whether I would open it without authority, or whether I would respect the seal.

But if the second letter was false, then the package really did contain Constable documents, and the office wanted to see whether I would open it to recover them.

I had no way to know. Both letters were consistent within themselves. Both could be true. Both could be false.

I thought about the examination. It had never been about finding the right answer. It had been about making a decision when the answer was not clear. In the first round, I had chosen the yellow gate because it was reliable, not because it was fastest. In the third round, I had chosen the longer route around the granary because it was safer. In the armoury, I had chosen three items instead of risking more.

Each time, I had decided based on what I could verify. Each time, I had accepted uncertainty rather than pretending to certainty.

Now I had to do the same.

I put the key in my pocket. I closed the drawer and locked it. I placed the second letter and the folded paper back inside.

I sat down and wrote my decision on a sheet of paper from the desk:

"I will not open the package. The first letter instructs me to decide what to do with it. The second letter, found in the locked drawer, contradicts the first. I have no way to verify either. I will therefore treat the package as what it appears to be: a sealed object addressed to someone no longer present. It should be left at the east gate. Its presence should be reported to the clerk. I have not opened it. I have not destroyed it. I have not delivered it to another candidate."

I signed my name and placed the paper on the desk.

The hour was not yet up. I sat in the chair and waited. My shoulder ached. The room was cold. I could hear the clock in the main building ticking. I could hear footsteps in the corridor—other candidates being led to their rooms, or perhaps being led away after their hour was up.

I thought about the list of names. Twelve names. All of us. Why had the drawer contained that list? What did it mean?

I did not know. But I had made my decision. I had used the information available to me. I had not pretended to certainty where I had none.

The door opened. The clerk stood in the doorway. He looked at me, then at the paper on the desk.

"Your hour is up," he said.

I stood up. My legs were stiff from sitting. I picked up the sealed envelope and the key.

"I have not opened the package." I said.

He looked at the drawer. It was locked. The key was in my hand.

"You have made your decision?"

"I have."

"Then come with me."

I followed him out of the room. The corridor was cold, the stone walls damp. We walked to the main hall. The twelve of us were gathered there, standing in a line. Hunter was at the far end, his arms crossed, his ledger closed. He did not look at me.

Calloway was standing on the low steps, a sheet of paper in his hand. He did not look at us for a long moment.

"The final examination is concluded," he said. "You will each be told your assignment."

He looked at the clerk. The clerk stepped forward and began to read names.

"Leon Ashcroft."

I stepped forward.

"You have been assigned to the Constable office in Alderwick," the clerk said. "You will begin your duties at the watch house. You will report to Calloway."

I nodded. I did not know what the duties would be. I did not know whether this was promotion or assignment. I only knew that I had been accepted.

"Bishop."

Bishop stepped forward. He was assigned to the same office, but to a different duty—field work, the clerk said. Bishop nodded.

"Ford."

Ford was assigned to the eastern district, a separate outpost. He nodded.

"Pearce."

Pearce was assigned to the main office, clerical duties. He nodded.

"Ellison."

Ellison was assigned to the western district. He nodded.

The clerk continued. The remaining candidates received their assignments—some to the central office, some to district outposts, some to duties in other towns.

When he finished, twelve names had been called. Twelve assignments had been made.

There were no eliminations. No one had failed. But the assignments were not equal. Some candidates had been chosen for the central office. Others had been sent to outposts. Others had been given duties that did not involve the kind of work we had been examined for.

I looked at the other candidates. Most had received assignments to outposts or secondary duties. Hunter's face had not changed when the clerk named the eastern district, but I had seen his hands tighten at his sides. I did not know what he had decided in his room. I only knew that his decision had not brought him to the watch house.

I looked at Hunter. He had been assigned to the eastern district, the same as Ford. He stood still, his face unreadable. He did not look at me.

Calloway stepped forward. He looked at the twelve of us, standing in the hall.

"The examination is over," he said. "You will begin your duties tomorrow. The office will observe your performance. That is all."

He turned and walked away.

The clerk closed his ledger. "You are dismissed," he said.

We dispersed. Some candidates spoke in low voices as they walked to the dining hall. Others stood alone, thinking. I walked to the door and stepped outside.

The courtyard was empty. The sun was rising, the light pale and cold. I stood for a moment, breathing the morning air, my shoulder aching, my mind turning over the room, the letter, the drawer, the key.

I had not opened the package. I had not taken the risk. I had made a decision based on uncertainty and lived with it.

I walked back to my room. The corridor was quiet. I opened the door and stepped inside.

The room was the same as it had been when I arrived—a narrow bed, a table, a chair, a window. My coat was on the bed. My notebook was on the table.

I sat down and opened my notebook. The pages were filled with observations—the lock, the symbol, the granary, the armoury, the lists of names. I turned to the last blank page and wrote the date and my assignment.

I closed the notebook and placed it in my pocket.

I stood up and looked out the window. The courtyard was empty. The main building was quiet.

I thought about the package at the east gate. I had left it there. I had reported it. I had not opened it. I did not know whether that was the right decision. I only knew that it was my decision.

I put on my coat. I picked up my bag.

When the bell rang, I went down. The courtyard was filling with candidates. The man in the grey coat was waiting with a cart. Calloway stood near the main building, watching.

We gathered our things. We said little.

I walked to the gate. The sun was higher now, the light clearer. The road stretched out before me, leading back to Alderwick.

I did not know what awaited me there. I did not know whether Hunter would be an ally or a rival, whether Bishop and Ford would be colleagues or strangers.

But I knew I had made decisions under uncertainty. I had acted without complete information. I had accepted the consequences.

I walked through the gate and onto the road.

Behind me, the examination grounds stood quiet, their walls grey against the morning sky.

I did not look back.

I walked toward Alderwick, the road rising and falling under my feet, my shoulder aching, my bag heavy on my shoulder, the notebook in my pocket.

The examination was over. The work was beginning.
