# Chapter 4 — The Shop at the Top of the Lane

I did not go back to Bell's because I had meant to. I went because the morning had been long enough already, and I had run out of reasons to stay indoors.

The lane was the same as before. Narrow, climbing away from the market square into the older part of town. The houses here had the look of having been built for a different century and then simply kept. A tailor's shop with fabric in the window. A hardware shop showing tools behind glass. A flower shop with buckets on the pavement. Among them stood houses whose front sections had become shops, as if commerce had found the easiest door and used it.

I stopped at the corner and looked up.

The green door was open.

A small bell hung above it. I could not yet hear it from where I stood, but I could see the narrow shape of the interior beyond the threshold, and the dark blur of shelves rising on both sides of the room.

I stepped up.

The bell rang once. A thin, dry sound. Not a shop bell as I had known them. More like an announcement than an invitation.

The scent of old paper and wood reached me at once. Dust, too, but not the stale kind. The kind that settles slowly in a room where few windows stay open for long.

I stepped inside.

The shelves were crowded in the way I had already seen, but now I could see that the crowding had a shape I had not been able to make out from the street. Books were laid horizontally in some places, upright in others, stacked in narrow columns in a few corners as if someone had placed them there with a system he did not wish to explain to a glance. Maps hung in frames of dark wood along the walls. There were boxes on the lower shelves, their contents visible only as edges and corners. Prints of buildings I did not always recognise lay flat in a few places, face up as though waiting to be looked at by someone who had the time.

No one spoke.

Then a man emerged from behind a shelf carrying a stack of books.

He was older than I had expected. Not old, but older in the way a man becomes older after a life spent bending over tables and shelves. His coat was worn, with dust on the sleeves. He wore wire spectacles that sat on a narrow nose. His hair was grey and thin and untidy, as if he had been handling it with his hands while reading. When he set the stack down, I saw that his fingertips were marked with ink.

He looked at me. Then at the package wrapped in paper that I held in one hand.

"Margaret?"

"She asked me to bring this."

He took it. "Good."

He carried it to a table at the back and set it down. Then he looked at me again, more carefully.

"I was beginning to worry she'd send someone younger."

"Why?"

"Because if Daniel delivered it, half the food would likely be gone."

I laughed, though I had not meant to. "Is he really like that?"

"He once ate my birthday cake before I even saw it."

"Why do you still let him into the shop?"

He did not answer at once. He glanced at the question, then back at me, and gave a small shrug that was not quite a smile. "Because he's a good boy."

I followed him to the table. He set the package down and looked at the book I had brought with me. I had not planned to bring it. I only knew that I had it with me when I left the house, folded into the inside pocket of my coat as if it had attached itself to me.

"You brought that book."

"I'm still reading it."

"Good."

He pulled out a chair. "Sit down."

I sat. He put on his spectacles and the room changed around him, or perhaps only around the space between us. He became, in that moment, the sort of man who belonged to the shelves.

"What's caught your curiosity?"

I turned a few pages to the paragraph I had marked. "In this section. There's a term I've never heard before."

He read. Then he leaned back.

"Ah."

"You know it?"

"I've heard it."

"What does it mean?"

He smiled, but not in the way a man smiles when he knows the answer and is pleased to give it. It was a quieter smile, more like a man smiling at the shape of a question he had met before.

"A harder question."

"Why?"

"Because terms like this don't always have a single meaning."

I waited.

He opened a drawer in the table and took out an old map. "Sometimes a name changes." He unfolded it and spread it between us. "Sometimes streets change." His finger moved along the street lines. "Sometimes people keep using the old name even when the official one has changed."

I looked at the map. It showed Alderwick as it had been at the time it was drawn. Some names had changed. Some streets had been extended. Some parts that were now full of houses had once been open land.

"If you want to understand Alderwick's history, don't only read the names written in books."

"Why?"

"Because the people who live here don't always call places by the same name."

I nodded slowly.

He looked at me. "You're beginning to notice the small things."

"Is that bad?"

"No." He smiled. "Quite the opposite."

He looked at the map again. "People often think history is about great events." He folded it slowly. "Though the smallest things are usually what change a place first."

I did not answer. I only kept that sentence in my head. Not because it sounded mysterious. But because it made sense.

I closed the book. "I'll read it again."

"Good."

He saw me to the door. The bell rang once more as I left.

Outside, the afternoon air had grown cooler. I put my hands in my coat pockets and stood for a moment listening to the lane. No one else was there. No cart. No voices. Just the faint sound of the town moving somewhere beyond the roofs.

I walked home by the same route, though I had begun to know it well enough that it no longer felt like a route. It felt like a road I might keep using.

And when I reached the corner by the river, I turned and looked back at the lane.

The green door was still open.
