# Chapter 4 — The People of Ashford

**Ashford, 16 November 1816**

That morning I woke later than usual. There was no particular reason. I simply did not have work for a few more days, and for the first time since arriving in Ashford, I did not feel the need to go anywhere at once.

I made a simple breakfast. Then I read the newspaper in the dining room.

National news.
Wheat prices.
Weather.
Some political news.
Nothing very interesting.

I folded the paper and looked at the clock. About ten o'clock. I still had little to occupy myself.

Eventually I took my coat. I decided to go to Margaret's.

Not because I had anything to ask. I was simply growing accustomed to the place.

Three days before, I had not even known the café existed.
Now I knew the way there without needing the map.
I knew where to turn towards High Street.
I knew the bakery that was usually full by morning.
I knew the stagecoach stop near Market Square.
I was even beginning to recognise certain buildings by the shape of their windows.

Ashford was slowly changing from a foreign town into a collection of places I could recognise.
Not yet a home.
But no longer entirely strange.

---

When I opened the café door, the sound of conversation reached me at once. Not busy, but lively. A few tables were occupied.
A man read a newspaper.
Two women were speaking in low voices.
A young man sat alone near the window, writing something.

Margaret stood behind the counter. She was speaking with a man perhaps in his thirties.

"I told you, you didn't need to bring it back."
The man set a small box on the counter.
"If I didn't, you'd think I'd stolen it."
Margaret looked at the box.
"I never thought you'd stolen it."
"You said I took it."
"I said you might have taken it."
"It's the same thing."
"No."
Margaret opened the box, looked inside, then closed it again.
"If I said you might have taken it, then I hadn't yet decided."
The man sighed.
"You always have an answer."
"Because you always have a question."
Margaret smiled.
Then her eyes caught sight of me.
"Leon."
I raised a hand.
"Morning."
"Come for breakfast, or come to hide?"
"I haven't decided."
"Then sit down. I'll help you decide."

I sat at a table near the window. Margaret went back to serving customers. I watched her for a moment.
She never looked hurried.
Even when several people needed something at nearly the same time, she managed everything without appearing to lose control.
Not because she moved quickly.
She simply knew what to do first.

I began to notice that Margaret's had its own rhythm. Mornings were dominated by people who came before work, who hadn't had breakfast, and residents who stopped by to read the newspaper.
Towards midday, the tables grew busier. Some people came only to eat. Others used the café as a place to meet before returning to work.
There was no written rule about it.
No need.
Everyone seemed to know how the place worked.
Margaret never announced that her café was a gathering place. The people had made it that way.

---

I ordered coffee and breakfast. Not long after, Margaret brought them.
"You've begun to find your way around town?"
"A little."
"Good."
She was about to leave.
I said:
"Do you always know everyone here?"
Margaret stopped.
"Everyone?"
"Almost."
She looked around.
"Not all of them yet."
"How long until you know all of them?"
"I don't know."
She thought a moment.
"Perhaps ten years."
"That sounds exhausting."
"Quite the opposite."
She smiled.
"People are interesting."
Then she left again.

I began to eat. I thought about that number.
Ten years.
For someone living in a large city, knowing most of a café's customers might sound absurd. But Ashford was different. The town was small enough that one could meet the same people many times.
The same shop.
The same stop.
The same pub.
The same café.
The same school.
The same church.
Even the same street.
Repetition turned strange faces into familiar ones. And familiar faces eventually became names.

---

About ten minutes later, the man who had been speaking with Margaret sat at the next table. He opened a newspaper. A few seconds later Margaret passed by.
"Don't pretend to read."
The man lowered the paper.
"I am reading."
"You've been on the same page for five minutes."
"I was thinking."
"About?"
"Bills."
Margaret set a cup of coffee in front of him.
"How much?"
The man named the sum.
Margaret shook her head.
"Then don't buy a new carriage."
The man stared at her.
"How did you know?"
"You're the third person this week who complains about money but comes in carrying a new carriage key."
I turned.
The man laughed.
"All right. I understand."
Margaret walked away.
I looked at the carriage key on the table.
Then at the man.
His jacket was new.
His shoes too.
But the bottom of his trousers was muddy.
Perhaps he had come from a newly repaired carriage park. I did not think much of it.
The man looked at me.
"Are you new here?"
"Yes."
"Leon?"
I was a little surprised.
"How do you know?"
He pointed at Margaret.
"She said your name."
I looked at Margaret.
She had her back to us.
I smiled.
"Who are you?"
"Michael."
We shook hands.
"Welcome to Ashford."
"Thank you."
Michael went back to reading his newspaper. I continued my breakfast.
Nothing special.
But I began to notice something. In this place, your name could reach you even before you truly knew the person.
Michael left a few minutes later, leaving his newspaper on the table. Margaret picked it up and placed it on the newspaper rack.
No one took it. Local newspapers, it seemed, had a life of their own in a place like this.
Someone read the front page.
Someone else read the sports.
The next person might pick it up just to look at job advertisements or houses for rent.
Information moved from hand to hand. Not only through conversation.

---

Around eleven o'clock, two people came in. One of them went straight to the counter.
"I've needed a plumber for three days. Do you still have the name of one I can trust?"
"I do."
"Which one?"
"The one who won't come three days later."
The man laughed.
"So not the one from yesterday."
Margaret took a piece of paper.
"Here."
She handed it over.
"Thank you."
The man left.
I looked at Margaret.
"Do you keep everyone's names?"
"The useful ones."
"How many?"
"Don't start."
I smiled.
Margaret walked over to my table.
"Why?"
"I'm beginning to understand why people come here."
"Why?"
"Because you know who they should contact."
Margaret shrugged.
"I only know people."
"It seems like more than that."
"Leon."
"Yes?"
"You're too serious for someone who's just drinking coffee."
I laughed. I looked at the piece of paper Margaret had given.
Only a first name and an address.
No other information.
No title.
Just a first name and an address.
In 1816, connecting one person to another still relied on names and addresses.
There was no bureau that would send someone without a clear name.
No announcement that could show who was working.
If someone knew a good plumber, they kept their name.
If someone else needed them, they asked.
And in cases like this, Margaret was a living address book.

---

Not long after, a woman entered.
She was perhaps in her thirties.
Her hair was tied simply.
She carried a large bag and looked slightly annoyed.
Margaret greeted her at once.
"You're late."
"I know."
"He's already gone."
The woman sighed.
"Good."
Margaret pointed to a chair.
"Sit down first."
The woman sat.
I did not mean to listen.
But their voices were clear.
"He left another message."
"The same one?"
"More or less."
"And you still haven't answered?"
"I don't know what to say."
Margaret poured tea.
"If you don't know what to say, perhaps don't say anything yet."
The woman was silent.
Then she smiled faintly.
"Sometimes I wonder why people come to you."
Margaret shrugged.
"Because I provide the chairs."
I looked at them. A simple sentence. But I was beginning to understand something.
Margaret did not always offer solutions. Sometimes she only provided a place. People came to her not because she always knew the answers. They came because they knew she would listen.
I looked around the café.
There was no private room for confidences.
No sign saying that Margaret accepted confessions.
Nothing that made her look like a counsellor.
Only tables.
Chairs.
Tea.
Coffee.
And someone willing to listen.
Perhaps that was enough. In a small town, a place to talk might be as important as a place to eat.
People who lived close together for years naturally had their own troubles. And sometimes they only needed someone who would not immediately carry the story elsewhere.
At least, that was the impression I had of Margaret.

---

After the woman left, I said:
"You never give advice?"
Margaret was clearing a table.
"Of course I do."
"Just now you didn't."
"She didn't need advice."
"How do you know?"
Margaret looked at me.
"Because if she needed advice, she would have asked."
I nodded.
That made sense.
Margaret continued her work.
"Most people don't need someone to solve their problems."
"So?"
"They only want someone who won't make their problems worse."
I smiled.
"You're rather clever."
"Don't be too quick to praise me."
"I mean it."
"I know. That's what makes it dangerous."
I laughed.

A few minutes later, Margaret returned to the counter. A man came in and handed her an envelope. Margaret looked at it.
"Have you got an answer for it yet?"
"Not yet."
"Why not?"
"Because I don't know where to send it."
Margaret read the address on the front of the envelope. Then she thought for a moment.
"I know someone."
She turned to me.
"Leon."
I pointed at myself.
"Yes?"
"Have you still got time?"
"I have."
Margaret took the envelope.
"This man is trying to find someone's address."
I looked at the envelope.
"And you know the address?"
"I think so."
The man cut in.
"If you know it, why not tell me?"
"Because I'm not certain."
Margaret handed the envelope to me.
"Could you look at it?"
I took the envelope. I studied the name and address on the front.
The handwriting.
The house number.
The street name.
I did not open it. A few seconds later I said:
"This address has been changed."
The man frowned.
"Why?"
I pointed at the lower part of the envelope.
"The ink here is different."
Margaret moved closer. I went on.
"This part was written later."
I pointed at the house number.
"And this number was added after the rest of the address."
The man took the envelope. I turned it over. There were faint traces of writing, almost invisible.
The man stared at it.
"Good heavens."
Margaret took the envelope.
She looked at the back.
"Right."
The man sighed.
"So he's still at the old address."
"Perhaps."
I pointed at the faint writing.
"Or the person who sent this knew the old address but didn't know he'd moved."
The man was silent.
Then he smiled.
"At least I know where to start."
Margaret handed the envelope back.
"Good luck."
The man thanked her before leaving.
I sat back down. Margaret stood in front of me. She looked at me for a few seconds.
"You know?"
"Know what?"
"That the writing was different."
"It was visible."
"Not to me."
"You didn't see it because you knew the person."
Margaret smiled.
"Interesting."
"What?"
"Usually I know the people. You see the things I don't."
I shrugged.
"Everyone has their own field."
"True."
She thought for a moment.
"If this continues, I really will have to pay you."
"I didn't do anything."
"You just saved that man time."
"That wasn't work."
Margaret smiled.
"Not yet."

After the man had gone, I still saw the envelope in my mind. Not because it felt mysterious. Quite the opposite. There was something very ordinary about the problem.
An old address.
A new address.
A house number written over.
Someone who had moved.
Someone who didn't know.
In a larger town, such a letter might simply be lost. In Ashford, someone might know who to ask.
Margaret knew the person.
I could read the handwriting.
And the man could continue his search.
One small problem passed from one person to another until it found its way.

---

Daniel arrived around midday.
He carried two books and looked slightly hurried.
Margaret said at once:
"You're late."
Daniel set down the books.
"I had a lecture."
"You always have an excuse."
"It's not an excuse. It's a schedule."
"Your schedule is bad."
Daniel looked at me.
"Leon."
"Daniel."
"How long have you been here?"
"A few hours."
Daniel looked at Margaret.
"Has she put you to work yet?"
Margaret answered:
"He works for me now."
I raised an eyebrow.
"I don't remember taking a job."
Daniel laughed.
"Don't worry. She doesn't pay."
Margaret pointed at Daniel.
"And you still owe money."
Daniel sighed.
"We're not discussing that."
"We always discuss that."

I watched them. Their relationship was indeed different. Margaret spoke to Daniel like someone who had known him for a very long time.
No formality.
No caution.
Daniel also did not seem shy.
They could argue about small things without truly being angry.

Daniel sat across from me.
"What happened?"
"Leon found someone's address."
Daniel looked at me.
"Seriously?"
"He only read the writing."
Margaret interjected:
"He also made our customer realise he was too trusting of an altered envelope."
Daniel smiled.
"Good."
Then he looked at me.
"Don't help Margaret too often."
"Why?"
"She'll find work for you."
Margaret smiled.
"It's already too late."

Daniel did not stay long.
He had to return to the Academy.
Before leaving, he said:
"I might come again tomorrow."
Margaret:
"Don't."
Daniel:
"I'll still come."
"Why?"
"Because your coffee is cheaper than at the Academy."
Margaret pointed at the door.
"Go."
Daniel laughed and left.

I watched him go. Then Margaret said:
"He's a good boy."
"Have you known him long?"
"A few years."
"Does he come often?"
"Since he started at the Academy."
I nodded. "He's quite different from you."
Margaret smiled.
"Thank goodness."
"Why?"
"One Margaret in this town is enough."

After Daniel left, I watched a few people come and go. Some only bought coffee. Some sat for a few minutes reading their notes. One carried a large notebook and looked for a spot near the window with good light.
I smiled at the sight. Although the world kept changing, there were still people who preferred books, paper, and pens.
The Academy was not far from here.
And the flow made this part of town feel slightly younger than the area around Bellweather Lane.

---

After Daniel left, the café grew quieter. I read the newspaper. Margaret arranged some cups on the shelf. Occasionally we spoke about small things.
The weather.
Food.
My work, which would begin in a few days.
Then Margaret suddenly said:
"Leon."
"Hm?"
"Do you still have the book Arthur gave you?"
I looked up.
"The Ashford history book?"
"Yes."
"Still."
"Finished?"
"Not yet."
I closed the newspaper.
"There are a few parts I wanted to ask him about."
Margaret smiled.
"Then that's fortunate."
She took a piece of paper from near the counter.
"Arthur called earlier."
"Why?"
"He asked for food to be sent."
I looked at the paper.
"What did he order?"
"Sandwiches."
I laughed quietly.
"Arthur eats sandwiches?"
Margaret looked at me.
"Why? Did you think he only eats soup and old books?"
"I didn't know."
"He ordered two."
"Two?"
"He said one for afternoon, one for evening."
I shook my head.
"That makes sense."
Margaret took a wrapped package of food that had already been prepared.
"You are going to his shop, aren't you?"
"I am."
"Good."
She handed the package to me.
"Then take this along."
I looked at the food in my hand.
"Free?"
Margaret smiled.
"Not yet."
"What's the payment?"
"Free coffee."
"I already get free coffee."
"Then you have no reason to refuse."
I laughed.
"Very well."
Before I left, Margaret said:
"Leon."
I turned.
"Don't eat it on the way."
"I'm not hungry."
"I don't believe you."
"I'm going."
"Give Arthur my regards."

I walked towards Arthur's shop. It was not far from the café. The route was also in the same direction as my house.
I only realised this after a few days in Ashford. If I kept walking from the town centre, past High Street, I could reach Arthur's shop before eventually turning towards Bellweather Lane. So I did not need to go out of my way.
The food package was still warm in my hand.
I did actually want to see Arthur.
Not because of anything strange.
I was still thinking about a passage from the book he had given me.
There was an old term used in one of the notes about the Ashford area.
I had looked for it in the dictionary.
Found nothing.
Perhaps the term was only used in local records.
And if there was someone who might know the answer...
Arthur.
I walked a little faster.

The road from Margaret's to Arthur's shop showed a different part of Ashford from Market Square. The shops here were smaller. Some still used family name boards.
A tailor's shop with fabric displayed in the window.
A hardware shop showing tools behind the glass.
A flower shop with buckets set out on the pavement.
Among them stood old houses whose front sections had been turned into shops. Ashford had no clear boundary between commercial and residential areas.
The two mixed. People could live on the upper floors and open a shop below. A family could run the same business for several generations. And a building that once had a different function could be reused without entirely losing its old shape.

---

A small bell above the door rang when I entered. *Ting.* The scent of old paper and wood met me at once.
Bookshelves filled most of the room. In a few corners, old objects were arranged neatly.
A pocket watch.
Several postcards.
A small wooden box.
An old framed map.

Arthur emerged from behind a shelf carrying a stack of books. He saw me. Then he saw the food package in my hand.
"Margaret?"
"She asked me to bring this."
Arthur smiled.
"Good."
He took the package.
"I was beginning to worry she'd send someone younger."
I looked at him.
"Why?"
"Because if Daniel delivered it, half the food would likely be gone."
I laughed.
"Is he really like that?"
"He once ate my birthday cake before I even saw it."
"Why do you still let him into the shop?"
Arthur carried the food to the back table. "Because he's a good boy."
I followed. Arthur set down the package. Then he looked at the book I was carrying.
"You brought that book."
"I'm still reading it."
"Good."
"There's something I wanted to ask."
Arthur smiled faintly.
"Well."
He pulled out a chair. "Sit down." I sat across from him and opened the book. Arthur put on his spectacles.
"What's caught your curiosity?"
I turned a few pages. "In this section." I pointed at a paragraph. "There's a term I've never heard before."
Arthur read. Then he leaned back.
"Ah."
"You know it?"
"I've heard it."
"What does it mean?"
Arthur smiled.
"A harder question."
"Why?"
"Because terms like this don't always have a single meaning."
I waited.
Arthur opened a desk drawer. He took out an old map. "Sometimes a name changes." He unfolded the map. "Sometimes streets change."
His finger moved along the street lines. "Sometimes people keep using the old name even when the official one has changed." I looked at the map. Arthur pointed at an area.
"If you want to understand Ashford's history, don't only read the names written in books."
"Why?"
"Because the people who live here don't always call places by the same name."
I nodded slowly.
Arthur looked at me.
"You're beginning to notice the small things."
"Is that bad?"
"No."
He smiled.
"Quite the opposite."
Arthur looked at the map again.
"People often think history is about great events."
He folded the map slowly.
"When in fact, the biggest changes sometimes start with something very small."
I did not answer.
I only kept that sentence in my head.
Not because it sounded mysterious.
But because it made sense.
I closed the book.
"I'll read it again."
Arthur nodded.
"Good."

I looked at the map once more before Arthur put it away.
The lines of its streets were not entirely the same as the Ashford I knew now.
Some names had changed.
Some streets had been extended.
Some parts of the town that were now full of houses had once been open land.
There was a small mark near the river.
There was a path to the stagecoach stop.
There was an old commercial area that seemed much smaller than now.
I began to understand something that had previously only felt vague. When people in Ashford referred to a place, they might not always be talking about the official name written on the map.
They might use an old name.
A family name.
The name of a building that no longer existed.
Or a name only known to residents who had lived there long enough.
The town had its own language. And I was only beginning to learn it.
I stood. "Thank you."
"Leon."
I turned.
Arthur pointed at the food package on the table.
"If you go back to Margaret, tell her the food is safe."
I smiled.
"I'll tell her."
I walked to the door. The bell rang once more as I left. Outside, the afternoon air had grown colder. I put my hands in my coat pockets. Then I continued on my way home.
Now I had routes I was beginning to know.
Margaret's café.
Arthur's shop.
The way to Bellweather Lane.
Some faces I was beginning to recognise.
Some names I was beginning to remember.
Not many.
But enough to make Ashford feel slightly different from the day I first arrived.
I was no longer merely passing through this town.
I was beginning to have places I knew.
And people who knew me.
For now, that was enough.
