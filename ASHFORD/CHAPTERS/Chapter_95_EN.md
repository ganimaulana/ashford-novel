CHAPTER 95 — THE THIRD ROUND

I rose before the bell. The air was cold and still. I had not slept well. The map lay folded in my pocket, its edges already worn from being turned over in my hands during the night. I had studied it until the lamp burned low—the eastern gate, the storehouse, the red circle that marked something I did not yet understand.

I went down to the courtyard. The clerk was already standing near the main building, his ledger open, a pencil in his hand. He did not look at me when I approached.

“You are early,” he said.

“The note said dawn.”

He made a small mark in the ledger. “The map you chose. You have it?”

“Yes.”

“Then you know where to go. The storehouse at the eastern gate. There is a document there, sealed in an iron chest. The chest is locked. The key is not with you. It is with one of the candidates who chose the key. You will need to find that candidate and obtain the key. You may trade, cooperate, or convince. You may not take it by force. You have until the bell rings at noon. Those who return with the document will continue. Those who do not will be dismissed.”

He turned the page. “You may begin.”

I stood still for a moment. I had expected instructions, but I had not expected to be told that I would need to find someone else. I did not know who had chosen the key. I had seen some candidates choose left, some right, but I had not noted which was which. I could only guess.

I walked toward the eastern gate, keeping my pace steady. I saw Weaver near the well, his hand brushing his coat pocket. He had chosen the right table. I remembered that. I did not speak to him. I simply walked, and after a moment, I heard his footsteps behind me.

We walked together in silence. The grounds were quiet. The gate was open. Beyond it, I could see the storehouse—a low building of stone and timber, its roof sagging, its windows boarded. The red circle on the map had marked it exactly.

We reached the door. It was locked. Weaver took a small iron key from his pocket and tried it on the door. It did not fit. He put the key away without a word.

I looked at the door. It was old, the wood warped, the iron hinges rusted. I pushed against it, but it did not move.

“There must be another way,” I said.

We walked around the building. At the back, I found a window, its boards loose. I pulled one free, and the window opened inward. It was narrow, but I could fit through.

“I will go in,” I said.

Weaver nodded. I climbed through the window and dropped into the dark interior. The air was thick with dust. I could see several iron chests lined against the far wall, each with a different lock. I studied them, matching the marks on my map. One chest had a symbol that matched the red circle. I walked toward it and tried to lift it. It was heavy—too heavy for me to move.

I heard a sound behind me. I turned. Hunter was standing at the window, his arms crossed, his head tilted. He had not spoken.

“The chest is heavy,” he said.

“I know.”

“I can move it. But I want something in return.”

“What?”

“When you read the document, you will tell me what it says.”

I looked at him. I did not know what the document contained. I did not know whether I was allowed to share it. But I knew I could not open the chest without the key, and I could not move it without help.

“I will tell you what I find,” I said.

He stepped through the window and walked to the chest. He lifted it easily, one hand on each side, and set it down on a low table. Weaver came in through the door, which he had managed to force open. He saw the chest on the table, the key still in his hand.

He unlocked the chest without a word. Inside was a sealed envelope, thick and heavy. He picked it up and handed it to me.

“You found the map,” he said. “You should carry it.”

I took the envelope. I looked at Hunter. He did not ask for it. He simply turned and walked to the window, climbed out, and was gone.

I walked back to the courtyard. The clerk was still standing near the main building. He looked at the envelope in my hand and wrote something in his ledger.

“You are among the first,” he said.

I did not know whether that was good or bad. I only knew that I had succeeded, but that I now owed a debt to Hunter.

I walked back to my room. The sun was rising. The field was empty.

I sat on the bed and opened the envelope. Inside was a single sheet of paper with a list of names—ten names, including my own. I did not know what it meant. I folded it and put it in my pocket.

I waited for the bell to ring. I thought about Hunter, and about the debt I had promised. I did not know whether I would keep it. But I knew that the examination was no longer just about completing tasks. It was about the people I chose to rely on.
