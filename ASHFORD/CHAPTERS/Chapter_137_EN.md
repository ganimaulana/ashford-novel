CHAPTER 137 — THE CROSSING

I waited at the river for three days.

I did not cross. I did not turn back. I stayed at the bridge, and I watched the river, and I waited.

The medallions were warm. They had not changed.

I could not yet tell what the count was telling me.

On the third day, a man came to the bridge.

He was old. He walked with a stick, and he carried a bag over his shoulder. He looked at me, and he looked at the river, and he looked at me again.

"You have been here a while," he said.

"I have."

"You are waiting to cross."

"I am waiting to know whether I should cross."

He looked at the river.

"The river does not care," he said. "It has been here for a thousand years. It will be here for a thousand more. It does not care whether you cross."

I looked at the river.

"I am not waiting for the river," I said. "I am waiting for the count."

He looked at me.

"The count," he said.

"The count of the keepers," I said. "It has been calling me. It led me to the marsh, and to the house on the hill, and to the crossroads. And now I am at the river, and I do not know whether it wants me to cross."

He was silent a moment.

"And if you cross?"

"Then I will be on the other side of the river."

"And if you do not cross?"

"Then I will be on this side."

He looked at me, and he nodded.

"Then the river has already decided," he said. "The river does not care. The count does not care. The count called you to the crossroads, and you chose, and you walked to the river. And now you are at the river, and the river does not care whether you cross."

He looked at me.

"You are the one who decides."

He walked past me, across the bridge, to the other side of the river.

I watched him go.

He walked down the road, on the other side, and he did not look back.

I stood on the bridge, and I understood.

The count had called me to the crossroads. I had chosen. I had walked to the river.

And now I was at the river, and the river did not care whether I crossed.

I was the one who decided.

I looked at the river.

I looked at the road on the other side.

I looked at the medallions against my chest.

I crossed the bridge.

I walked to the other side of the river.

I did not know what the south held. I did not know whether the count would call me again.

But I had crossed.

And I was on the other side.

---

I walked south for a month.

I walked through towns and villages, through fields and woods, through country I had never seen.

The medallions were warm. They had not changed.

I did not know what I was looking for. I did not know whether the count would call me again.

But I walked.

I walked until I came to the sea.

I stood on the shore, in the grey light, and I looked at the water.

The sea was wide. The waves moved, slow and steady, the way they had moved for a thousand years.

I stood on the shore, and I looked at the sea.

I had come to the end of the land.

There was nowhere further south.

I stood on the shore, and I waited.

The medallions were warm.

I did not know what the count was telling me.

I stood on the shore, and I waited.

And in the waiting, I understood.

The count had called me to the sea.

Not to cross it. Not to find anything.

To stand at the edge of it, and to understand that the calling was not about the destination.

The calling was about the journey, but I had begun to wonder whether the distinction between journey and destination was only a story I told myself to make the waiting bearable. The sea had its own rhythm, and the count had its own purpose, and I had not yet learned to hear either clearly.

I had walked from Ashford to the marsh. From the marsh to the house on the hill. From the house on the hill to the crossroads. From the crossroads to the river. From the river to the sea.

I had followed the count.

And I had come to the end of the land.

I stood on the shore, at the edge of the sea, and I understood that the count had called me to the sea.

I had come.

And the keeping was enough.

I stood on the shore, with the medallions warm against my chest, and I looked at the sea.

The count had called me.

I had come.

And I was the keeper.

I did not know what I would do next.

But I knew that I would keep—though I was no longer certain whether I was keeping the count, or the count was keeping me. I only knew that the sea had its own silence, and that silence was the oldest instruction I had ever received.

The keeping was the thing, or so I had been told. I had said it myself, many times, in many rooms. Standing at the edge of the sea, with the medallions warm against my chest and the waves steady and indifferent, I could not tell whether the words were truth or habit.

I was the keeper.

That much I knew. What I did not know was whether the title had outlived the thing it named.

The count of thirteen signs moved forward, and the hour of the bell drew nearer.
