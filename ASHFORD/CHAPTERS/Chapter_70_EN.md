The door of the tower was locked for three days.

I went to the church each morning. I stood at the door of the tower, and I tried the latch, and it was locked, and I did not force it. I could not yet tell who had the key. The verger, perhaps. Or the sexton. Or the warden. The bell was the church's bell, and the tower was the church's tower, and the key would be with the men who kept the church.

I did not ask. I had not asked anyone about the bell. I had asked about the wall, and the boundary, and the signs. I had not asked about the bell. I had not wanted to. The bell had been part of the winter â€” the bell that did not ring, the Thursday of the bell, the thread that Goding had known about and the town had not wanted to speak of. I had put it aside when I had found the gear and the door. I had thought it was a different mystery. I had been wrong. The bell was the keeper's bell. The bell was the third sign. And the door of the tower was locked.

On the fourth day, I went to the vestry. The clerk was there.

"The door of the tower is locked," I said. "I need to see the bell."

The clerk looked at me. "The bell is not for the town," he said.

"I know. It is the keeper's bell. I am the keeper."

He was silent a long moment. "The key is not mine," he said. "It is kept in the vestry, in the chest, but it is not mine to give."

"Then whose is it?"

"It is the keeper's," he said. "The key to the tower is the keeper's key. It has always been kept in the vestry, in the chest, for the keeper. Vale never took it. He never asked. But it is the keeper's key, and you are the keeper."

He stood. He went to the chest at the back of the vestry. He unlocked it. Inside, at the bottom of the chest, beneath the old papers and the broken bindings, there was a key.

A large key, of dark iron, its shaft long and its teeth worn. The clerk took it out. He held it out to me.

"The keeper's key," he said. "To the tower. To the bell."

I took it. It was heavy. I had not known there was a key. I had not known the key to the tower was the keeper's key. I had not known that Vale had never taken it.

I held the key in my hand, and I understood that I was holding something that had been kept for the keeper â€” kept in the vestry, in the chest, at the bottom, waiting for the keeper who would take it. Vale had never taken it. I was taking it. I did not know whether that was right. But I was the keeper. And the bell was the keeper's bell.

I went to the church. I stood at the door of the tower. I put the key in the lock. It turned. The door opened.

The tower was dark. The stairs were narrow, worn stone, winding upward into the dark of the tower. I climbed. The stairs were steep, and the air grew cold, and the light from the door below faded, and I climbed in the dark, with my hand on the wall.

The stairs ended. I stood in the bell chamber.

It was a small room at the top of the tower, with narrow windows on four sides, letting in the grey light. And in the middle of the room, hanging from the beams, was the bell. It was larger than I had expected. Old iron, dark with age, hanging from a great beam, its clapper still, its rim dusted with the years.

I stood in the bell chamber and looked at it. The keeper's bell. The bell that does not ring. It had not rung in living memory. It had hung in the tower, silent, for longer than anyone could remember.

I walked around it. The bell was plain. No engraving, no inscription, no mark â€” or none that I could see in the grey light. I stopped. There was a mark. On the rim of the bell, where the light from the window fell, there was a mark. A small mark, cut into the iron, worn smooth by years.

A circle. Three lines through it. A single dot. The first sign. Cut into the rim of the keeper's bell.

I stood in the bell chamber, in the grey light, and I looked at the first sign on the bell. The first sign was on the medallions. The first sign was on the wall of the common house. The first sign was on the bell.

I took the object from my coat. I held it up to the mark on the bell. The object was warm. I felt the warmth through the metal, the same warmth I had felt at the common, at the keeper's sign, at the door.

The object and the bell. The first sign and the first sign.

I did not ring it. I stood in the bell chamber, and I looked at the bell, and I did not ring it. I did not know what would happen if it rang. I did not know whether it would wake the town, or wake something beneath it, or whether it would simply ring, and be heard, and mean nothing.

I stood there for a long time. Then I turned, and I went down the stairs, and I closed the door of the tower, and I locked it with the keeper's key.

I walked home. I sat at my desk. I took out the red book. I wrote:

The bell is the keeper's bell. It hangs in the tower, silent. It has not rung in living memory. On its rim is the first sign. The object warms at it.

The bell is the third sign. The gear is the Order's sign. The bell is the keeper's bell. The first sign is on both.

I have the keeper's key. Vale never took it. I did not ring the bell. I do not know what would happen if it rang.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer, with the agreement, and the map, and my notebook. I did not put the medallions away. I left them on the desk. I left the keeper's key on the desk, beside them.

And I sat in the dark, and I thought about the bell, and about the first sign on its rim, and about the medallions that warmed at it, and about the house at the end of the lane, and the house that appears, and the door, and the signs.

The bell was the third sign. The keeper's bell. The bell that does not ring. I did not know what it was for. But I knew that it had been waiting. And I knew that I would find out.

I sat in the dark, with the key on the desk, and I thought about the bell. It had not rung in living memory. It had hung in the tower, silent, waiting. And I had stood in the bell chamber, and I had looked at the first sign on its rim, and I had not rung it.

I did not know whether I had chosen rightly. I only knew that the bell had been waiting, and that I had been brought to it. What that meant, I could not yet say. I was the keeper of the first sign. I was the keeper of the keeper's sign. I was the keeper of the bell. And I was the keeper of the keeping.

I did not know what the bell was for. But I would find out. And when I did, I would know whether to ring it. The bell was the call signal, and the call would come from the place beneath—and the call would come at 00:00.
