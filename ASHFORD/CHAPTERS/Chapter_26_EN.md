# Chapter 26 — The Lamp in the Window

The morning after the rate, the frost came back with a clear sky, and the lane was hard underfoot, and the smoke of the town rose straight in the cold. I went about my day with the knowledge of what had been said, and the knowledge of what had not been said, and the two knowledges carried in different pockets.

I had not seen Helen since the Sunday of the snow. She had been at the window of her house, and I had seen her lamp, but I had not seen her face, and the not-seeing of her was the thing that had begun to stand between us, though we had not spoken of it. I went to her door in the afternoon, because the afternoon was the hour when she was most often at home, and because I had a thing to ask, and the asking of it wanted a face.

She opened the door before I knocked. She had been at the window, and she had seen me coming, and she stood in the doorway with the light of the fire behind her, her shawl over her shoulders, and she looked at me the way she looks at a man that has come to her door with a thing he has not yet said.

"Mr. Ashcroft," she said. "You have been busy this week."

"I have been busy," I said. "I have been to the Academy, and I have been to Margaret's, and I have been at the round, and I have been at the desk."

She stepped back, and I came in. The room was warm, the fire low, and the lamp on the sideboard was trimmed and waiting for the evening. She had a book in her hand, and she set it down on the table. I saw the title, and it was a book about the plants of the county, and I did not think she was reading it for the plants.

"You have been asking about the gate," she said. "The yard man came to the shop this morning, and he spoke of it, and the constable has spoken of it, and the town has spoken of it, and I have heard it three ways before the noon. I will tell you what I have heard, if you will tell me what you have found."

I sat, and she sat, and the fire was between us, and I told her what I had found: the Academy copy, the name E. Hodge, the letter from the clerk's father, the London copy with the blank, the note that the occupant was not to be named at law. I told her of Foster, and of the subscription list, and of the man at the table in Margaret's room, and of the year-eight subscription, and of the word returned. I did not tell her of the letter on my table, or the shift of it, because I did not know what to make of it, and I did not want to make a thing of it that I could not unmake.

She heard me without interruption. When I had finished, she said, “You have found a name, and a man who may be looking for it. You have also found a gate, a rate, and a bell. Your book has put them close together. That does not make them one thing.” I have a lamp in my window, Mr. Ashcroft, and I have had it there since I came to this house, and I have kept it there because the keeping of it is the thing I was asked to do. I do not know why it is there, and I do not know what it does, and I do not know whether it does anything at all. I know only that I was asked to keep it, and I have kept it, and the keeping of it is the thing I have done."

She did not say who had asked her. I did not ask. She did not say how long she had kept it, or where the lamp had come from, or what it was for. She let the words stand, and the words stood, and I saw the fire on her face, and I saw that she was telling me the truth, and that the truth was not the whole of it.

"The gate," I said. "The gate opened at eleven, and the padlock was off the hasp, and I shut it, and the bell came at one, and the lamp did not come at nine, and the town has four tellings of it, and I have one telling, and I do not know which of the five is the true one."

"“The true account is the one you can support,” she said. “The town keeps its own accounts. You keep yours. That does not mean either is complete.”

I sat with that, and the fire was low, and the room was quiet, and she looked at me the way she looks at a man that has come to her door with a thing he has not yet said, and I had not said the thing I had come to say, because the thing I had come to say was the thing I could not say, which was that I had found a name, and the name was the name of a man that had kept the keys, and the man was dead, and the name was in the copy, and the copy was in my drawer, and the drawer was locked, and the key to the drawer was in my pocket, and I did not know whether the name in the copy was the name of the dead man, or the name of a living man, or the name of a man that had been made to look like another. I did not say it, and she did not ask, and we sat in the quiet of the room, and the fire settled, and the lamp on the sideboard stood waiting for the evening.

At the door she paused. “The lamp in my window is not the lamp at the end house. I keep mine because I was asked to. I cannot tell you who keeps the other one, or why.” I know only that I keep mine, and the keeping of mine is the keeping of the thing I was asked to do. You have found a name, Mr. Ashcroft. I have found a lamp. The two of us have found two things, and we do not know what either of them means. That is the way of this place. Good evening."

She shut the door, and I stood in the lane with the cold coming down, and I walked home the long way, past the church and past the gate, and the gate was shut, and the padlock was on it, and I did not stop to look at it.

I wrote in my book that evening, and I wrote what she had said, and I set it down as it stood. I left the page open for a long time, thinking about the lamp in her window and the lamp at the end of the lane. They were not the same, and yet they were both kept. I did not know whether the keeping was the same kind of work, or whether one of them was a copy of the other.

I wrote:

*Helen knows of the lamp, but she does not know what it is. She keeps a lamp of her own, and she was asked to keep it, and she does not know who asked. I have a name, and she has a lamp, and the two of us have found two things, and we do not know what either of them means. The gate opened, and the bell came, and the rate was asked, and Hobbs was named, and I have kept the book, and she has kept the lamp, and the keeping of the two things is the thing we do. I do not know whether the keeping is the answer, or whether the keeping is the question.*

I shut the book, and the lamp came up at the end of the lane at nine, steady at its old height, and I watched it from my window, and I thought of what she had said: *I do not know whether it does anything at all.*
Before I left Helen's house, I asked one question.

"When you were asked to keep your lamp, were you told what to do if someone asked about it?"

She looked toward the lamp on the sideboard.

"No."

"Nothing?"

"Only to keep it."

"Who gave you the instruction?"

She was silent long enough that I thought she would refuse.

"I have never found a useful answer to that question," she said. "You may decide what that means."

It was not an answer, but it was more honest than an invented one.

Outside, the afternoon had begun to darken. I stood in the lane and looked once toward the end house. Its window was empty in the daylight. There was no lamp to explain, no movement to follow, nothing I could put into my book without turning absence into evidence.

I went home instead.

The conversation had left me with one useful distinction: Helen was keeping something because she had been instructed to keep it, but she did not claim to understand the instruction. That was different from knowing the purpose. I wrote the distinction down and left the rest alone.
