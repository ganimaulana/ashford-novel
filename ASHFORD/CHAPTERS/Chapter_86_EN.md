CHAPTER 86 — THE HUNTER

I carried the Water Lane door with me through the next two days like a stone I had not yet decided to turn over. I did not go there. I did not tell anyone I had been given the address. I only let it sit in my mind, turning it over when I was alone, testing whether it felt heavier in the morning or in the evening. It felt the same. I did not know whether that meant it was true or only that I had not yet learned to read it.

On Thursday morning a note came. It said only: The office. Nine o’clock. Bring nothing.

I put on my coat and went.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open, and the crowd was already there. There were fewer of them than before—perhaps fifteen, perhaps sixteen. Some of the faces I remembered. Some I did not. The man with the notebook was there, standing apart from the others, his notebook closed but in his hand. The older man with the frayed cuffs was there, leaning against the wall by the pump. Ellison was there, standing near the back, his hands in his pockets. Merritt was standing alone by the gate, not looking at anyone. Peale was there, standing near the door, and he looked at me when I arrived and nodded once.

I nodded back.

I noticed one man I did not recognise. He was standing near the wall, his arms crossed, his head tilted slightly. He was not looking at anyone in particular. He was looking at the room as a whole, his gaze moving slowly from one face to another. I had not seen him before.

Calloway came out. He did not speak at first. He looked at the crowd the way he always looked at it, as though he had seen it before and knew he would see it again.

“You have completed the tasks,” he said. “Some of you found what you were looking for. Some of you did not. The office does not care which. The office cares how you chose to look.”

He paused.

“The next task is different,” he said. “You will go to the market square. You will watch the people there. There is a man in the square who does not belong. He will be there at ten o’clock. He will remain for half an hour. He will not know you are watching him. You will identify him and report to me what you saw.”

He looked at us.

“You may work alone or in groups. You have until noon. That is all.”

He turned and went inside.

We stood in silence.

“A man who does not belong,” Ellison said. “What does that mean?”

“It means we have to decide what ‘does not belong’ means,” I said.

“How do we decide that?”

“I do not know.”

I looked at the other candidates. Some were already moving. The man with the notebook was walking, his head down, his notebook open. The older man with the frayed cuffs was walking, his hands in his pockets. Peale was walking, his head down, his steps even.

The man I had not recognised was still standing near the wall. He was not moving. He was watching the others leave, his head still tilted.

I walked over to Ellison.

“We should go,” I said.

“Go where?”

“To the square. We need to be there before ten.”

We walked. The market square was full of people. Stalls lined the edges, selling bread and cloth and iron and paper. Women carried baskets. Children ran between the stalls. Men stood in groups, talking, their voices low and ordinary. The morning was cold, and the breath of the people who passed us rose in white clouds.

I stopped near the fountain. Ellison stood beside me. Merritt had followed us, but he was standing apart, his arms crossed, his eyes fixed on the crowd.

“We are looking for a man who does not belong,” Ellison said. “What does that mean?”

“I do not know,” I said.

I watched the crowd. I looked for someone who was not buying anything, not selling anything, not talking to anyone. I looked for someone who was waiting, or watching, or standing too still.

I saw a man near the baker’s stall. He was not buying bread. He was standing with his hands in his pockets, looking at the people who passed. I watched him for a minute. He did not move.

“There,” I said. “The man near the baker’s stall.”

Ellison looked. “He is just standing there.”

“He is not doing anything.”

“That is not a crime.”

“No,” I said. “But it is what we were told to look for.”

I looked away from the baker’s stall and scanned the square. My eyes passed over the chandler’s shop, the fountain, the gate at the far end. Then I saw the man I had not recognised. He was standing near the chandler’s shop, his arms crossed, his head tilted. He was not looking at the crowd. He was looking at me.

I looked away quickly.

I watched the crowd for another ten minutes. I saw several men who might have been the one we were looking for. I saw a man who was standing too still. I saw a man who was looking at the clock too often. I saw a man who was wearing a coat that was too clean for the square.

But I could not be sure.

I looked back at the chandler’s shop. The man was still there. He had not moved. He was still looking at me.

I walked toward him. He did not move as I approached.

“You are watching me,” I said.

He said nothing.

“Why?”

He looked at me for a moment. Then he said, “You are watching the wrong part of the square.”

He turned and walked away.

I stood still. He had not told me what to look for. He had not pointed at anyone. He had only said that I was watching the wrong part of the square.

I turned back to the square. I looked at the fountain. I looked at the stalls. I looked at the gate at the far end. I thought about what he had said.

I had been watching the people near the fountain, near the stalls, near the baker’s. I had been watching the people who were stationary, who were waiting, who were not doing what everyone else was doing.

But he had said I was watching the wrong part.

I looked at the gate at the far end. There was a man there, standing near the gate, his back to the square. He was not buying anything. He was not selling anything. He was not talking to anyone. He was simply standing, watching the street beyond the gate. I watched him for a minute. He did not move. Then he turned, walked a few steps into the square, and stopped again, his eyes scanning the stalls.

I had been looking for someone stationary, someone waiting, someone who did not belong. But this man was not stationary. He was moving, but not quickly. He was drifting, as though he had nowhere to be and nowhere to go. He crossed the square slowly, stopping at the fountain, then at the cloth stall, then at the chandler's shop. He was not buying anything. He was not selling anything. He was simply present, moving from one place to another, always staying within the square. He did not leave. He circled the square, drifting from stall to stall, never settling, never leaving.

I watched him for the rest of the half hour. He never left the square. He drifted from the fountain to the cloth stall, from the cloth stall to the chandler's, always moving, never settling. When the clock struck ten-thirty, he stopped near the gate. I did not follow him. I had seen enough.

I did not know whether he was the man we were meant to find. He could have been a man with nowhere to go. He could have been a man who was waiting for someone, or for something. But he had been in the square since before ten, and he was still there when the half hour ended. He had not left. He had only moved.

He was the only person I had seen who never seemed to settle. Everyone else appeared to have somewhere to be. He had remained in the square without making his purpose clear.

I walked back to the office. Ellison followed. Merritt followed behind us. The man with the notebook was already there, standing near the door. The man who had spoken to me was standing near the wall, his arms crossed, his head tilted.

I walked to Calloway.

“I found him,” I said. “He was in the square the whole time. He moved from place to place. He was not buying anything. He was not selling anything. He was not talking to anyone. He was just there, drifting.”

Calloway wrote something in his ledger. He did not look surprised.

“You may go,” he said.

I did not move.

“Who was he?” I said.

“That is not the question,” Calloway said. “The question is whether you noticed him.”

I did not know what to say. I had noticed the man at the gate, but I had not noticed him until the other man told me I was watching the wrong part. I had been watching the stationary people, the waiting people, the people who looked out of place.

But the man who did not belong had been moving. He had never seemed to be going anywhere.

I walked home slowly. I thought about the man who had spoken to me. He had not told me who to look for. He had only told me where I was not looking. He had seen the pattern before I did.

I did not know his name. I did not know why he had helped me. I did not know whether he had helped me or whether he had simply wanted to see what I would do with the information.

I sat in the chair by the window and thought about the square. I thought about the man at the gate. I thought about the man who had watched me watching the wrong part of the square.

I did not know whether I had passed or failed. I did not know whether the man at the gate had been the one we were meant to find or whether he had simply been a man with no particular purpose.

But I knew that someone else had seen what I had not seen. He had not told me the answer. He had only told me I was looking in the wrong direction.

I did not know whether that was a kindness or a test.

But I knew I would not forget his face.

Leon watched the square again after the report was accepted. The baker remained where he had been. The drifting man had vanished among carts and pedestrians. The stranger who had spoken to him was nowhere obvious. Three observations, three uncertainties. He wrote none of them down.
