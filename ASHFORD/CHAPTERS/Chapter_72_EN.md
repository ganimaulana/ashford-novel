# Chapter 72 — The Lie

Orme was in the room when I arrived, not Calloway. He sat at the far table, boots propped on a chair, a pipe in his mouth that he had not lit. He looked up when I came in and nodded once, the way a man acknowledges a door that needs opening. The room was different from Calloway's. There were no ledgers on the shelves, no ink-stained blotter on the desk. Only the table, two chairs, and the file. He had cleared the space for this purpose.

"You have the standing," he said. "That does not mean you are ready."

I set my notebook on the table. "Ready for what?"

"The first real test." He tapped the file on the table. "A constable in the west district reports a missing seal. He says it was on his desk at 1600, and gone at 1730. He says the door was locked both times."

I opened the file. The report was brief, typed on standard paper, the constable's signature at the bottom. Beneath it were three witness statements, each in a different hand. The paper was cheap, the kind used for internal circulation, and the typing was uneven — letter by letter, someone's careful handiwork. I noted that before I read further: a document worth the trouble to produce properly.

"Read them all," Orme said. "Then tell me what is established."

I read the report first. Constable Hargreaves, west district. The seal was a wax impression, red, bearing the constable's mark. He had placed it on the desk in the records room at 1600, stepped out for ten minutes to check a call, and found it missing at 1730.

Then the witnesses:

**Witness 1 — Sarah Kemble, desk clerk:** "At 1600 I was at my desk, facing the ledger shelves. I looked up at 1645 and saw Constable Hargreaves returning from his call. He did not go near the records room. I remained at my desk until 1700 and then went for my tea break."

**Witness 2 — Constable Poole, patrol:** "At 1645 I passed the records room on my way to the west gate. The door was open. I did not look inside. I have not seen the seal."

**Witness 3 — Martha Flynn, cleaner:** "At 1700 I cleaned the records room. The seal was on the desk where Hargreaves said he left it. I did not touch it. I locked the door when I left, which I always do."

I closed the file. "Hargreaves said the door was locked at 1600. Poole says it was open at 1645. Flynn says she locked it at 1700."

"And?"

"And Hargreaves said it was locked at 1730 too."

Orme waited.

"So someone was in the records room between 1600 and 1645." I wrote it in my book. *Entry: locked at 1600, open at 1645, locked at 1700, locked at 1730. Seal: on desk at 1700, gone at 1730.*

"Go on."

"Hargreaves left the room at 1610. He was at his call for ten minutes. He returned at 1620. The records room is next to the call box."

Orme was quiet. I had caught that from the map taped inside the cover of the file — a sketch of the office layout, the positions of the desks and rooms marked in pencil. "You memorised the layout."

"I read it twice."

"Good. What else do you see?"

I looked back at the statements. "Kemble says she was facing the ledger shelves at 1600. The ledger shelves are behind the constable's desk. She would have seen anyone going into the records room."

"And?"

"She says she looked up at 1645 and saw Hargreaves returning. But Hargreaves left his call at 1620. That is twenty-five minutes unaccounted for."

I wrote that down too. *Hargreaves: at call 1610–1620. Returned 1620. Kemble saw him return at 1645. Gap: 1620–1645.*

Orme leaned forward. "What do you conclude?"

"That Hargreaves was not where he said he was between 1620 and 1645."

"And the seal?"

"I do not know where it is. Kemble could not have taken it. She was at her desk. Flynn could have taken it after cleaning. Hargreaves could have taken it himself, and his report is the cover for why it was gone."

"Or?"

"Or none of them are lying. One of them is mistaken about timing. One of them is mistaken about location. One of them is mistaken about what they saw."

"That is the better answer." He set the file down. "You see what is established, and you see what is not. That is the skill the office needs."

I closed my book. "Hargreaves is not the liar, is he?"

"Hargreaves is not the only possibility." Orme stood. "He reported what he chose to see. That is enough to make the report useful and dangerous at the same time."

I wrote: *Report: what Hargreaves chose to see. Truth: not established.*

Orme tapped the file.

"You missed one thing."

I looked down at the statements.

"Who opened the door after Flynn locked it?"

I had no answer.

"And why is Kemble looking at the shelves instead of the doorway?"

I turned back to the first statement. I had noticed the position. I had not asked what it permitted her to see.

"Those are questions," Orme said. "Do not turn them into conclusions."

I nodded.

I wrote: *Three statements. One report. A fourth presence is possible, but not established.*

He took the file from me.

"That is enough."

I looked at the empty place where it had been.

"The seal is still missing," I said.

"Yes."

"Then the test is not about finding it."

Orme paused at the door.

"No. It is about what you are willing to say when you cannot."

He left.

I sat alone for another minute. The file had not become clearer. It had become smaller in one useful way: there were fewer things I could honestly claim.

That was not the same as solving it.

I wrote one final line before I closed the book:

*Ask who is absent. Do not manufacture the missing man.*

The phrase stayed with me on the walk home.

Before I reached my door, I stopped beneath the awning of the closed cooper's shop and took out my notebook.

I had written *Ask who is absent.*

I added another line beneath it.

*Absent from the room is not the same as guilty of the act.*

The distinction looked obvious on the page. It had not been obvious in the room.

I turned back toward the office.

I did not go inside. Instead I stood across the lane and looked at the windows. The west district records room was nowhere near this building, but I found myself reconstructing the map Orme had given me: desk, call box, records room, corridor, gate.

The missing twenty-five minutes bothered me more than the missing seal.

A missing object could be misplaced. A missing interval belonged to a person only if the record made it belong to one.

I opened the notebook again.

*Hargreaves: says one thing.*

*Kemble: sees something different.*

*Poole: gives an opening.*

*Flynn: confirms the seal remained.*

*Fourth person: possible, not established.*

I closed the book.

That was the first time I understood why Orme had refused to tell me whether there was a liar.

If he had named one, I would have spent the rest of the exercise trying to prove him wrong or right.

Instead he had left me with a record that resisted a clean answer.

When I reached home, I found a blank sheet on my desk. I used it to write the question I would send to Kemble:

*Who else had reason to enter the records room between four and half-past five?*

I did not write *Who entered?*

The difference mattered.

One question asked for a fact I did not possess.

The other asked for a route by which the fact might be found.

I folded the sheet and set it beside the lamp.

Tomorrow, if the office allowed it, I would ask.

---

The walk home was longer than it should have been. The town was quiet, and the lane was empty, and the cottage at the end of it was dark. I passed the baker's shop, and the smell of bread came out into the cold air, and I did not stop. I went to my own door and let myself in, and the room was quiet, and the notebook was on the desk. I sat for a while before I opened it. The room still held the shape of the conversation, and I wanted to remember it.

I opened the notebook and wrote the date, and I wrote: *One examination. Three witnesses. A missing seal. One person unaccounted for. I did not name the liar. I did not claim to know what happened. I asked who was missing. The question is not new. Only the language is.*

I closed the notebook and sat in the chair by the window. The fire had gone out, and I did not light it. I sat until the light failed, and I wrote nothing else. Some questions needed time to grow roots before they could be pulled.

The clock on the mantelpiece ticked through the evening, and I listened to it. In the morning I would write to Kemble and ask who else had been in the records room that afternoon. That was a question that could be answered. The others would come in time.
