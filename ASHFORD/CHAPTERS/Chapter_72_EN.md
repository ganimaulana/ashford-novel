CHAPTER 72 — THE STATEMENT

I went to the office on Wednesday morning, because Tuesday had ended with the clerk's pencil on the paper and the constable had said we would continue.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open. I had been there five times before. But I had never been there as a man who was expected to give a statement, and I had never been there as a man who was not answerable to the office at all.

I stepped inside.

The room was not the same. The chairs were still arranged on the other side of the desk. The clerk was there, sitting in one of them, a fresh sheet of paper on his knee, his pencil ready. Calloway was standing by the fireplace. Orme was sitting in the chair opposite the desk, his hat on his knee.

"The constable sent for you," Calloway said.

"I came," I said.

"You will sit," he said. He nodded at the chair next to Orme's. "We will continue the statement."

I sat. The clerk looked at me. He did not say anything. He looked at the paper on his knee.

"The statement is not finished," Calloway said. "Yesterday you told us what you saw," Calloway said. "Today you will tell us what you were told and what you concluded. The distinction is the work."

"I know," I said.

"The clerk will write what you say," Calloway said. "You will sign it. The record will be the office's record. The knowing will be yours."

He looked at the clerk. The clerk looked up.

"The journal," he said. "Vale gave it to you."

"He gave it to me to keep," I said. "Not to share."

"Have you read it?"

"I have."

"Did he ask you to keep it private?"

"He did."

"Did he say why?"

"He said some of it was nothing. Some he had never understood in fifty years. He could not say which was which."

"Did he say who might want it?"

"No."

"Did he say who might want to know what was in it?"

"No."

Calloway paused. He turned the paper in his hands.

"The map," he said. "You have seen it."

"I have."

"Could it be a cache map?"

"I do not know what it is. I can tell you what I saw. I cannot tell you what it means."

"Could it concern the parish?"

"I do not know."

"Could it concern the office?"

"I do not know."

"The letters," Calloway said. "Sealed."

"Sealed."

"Have you opened them?"

"No."

"Did Vale ask you not to open them?"

"He did not ask. He said some were nothing. Some he had never understood. He could not say which was which."

"Could any of them concern men who are no longer alive to ask?"

"I do not know."

"Could any of them concern the office?"

"I do not know."

Orme leaned forward. His hands were on his knees.

"The office has a duty," he said. "The things Vale kept may belong to men who are dead. The letters may be the only record those men have. The office cannot leave them sealed."

"The office may request," Calloway said. "The office may record. The office may advise. The box is in his keeping. He has said he will not open it. The office cannot compel him."

"The office can ask what he knows," Orme said. "And he will tell us."

"He has told us what he saw," Calloway said. "What he was told. What he concluded. He has kept them apart. That is the work."

"The work is not the law," Orme said.

"The law does not settle this for us," Calloway said. "We have a room, a man, and a record. That is all."

The clerk wrote on his paper. The pencil made a sound on the paper.

"There is the matter of the box itself," Calloway said. "The lock. The mechanism. The cloth."

"The lock is simple," I said. "Vale opened it with a strip of paper. He showed me once."

"Did he tell you why he used a strip of paper instead of a key?"

"He said the mechanism was not meant to be locked from the outside. He said the box was meant to be kept, not secured."

"Did he say who made it?"

"No."

"Did he say how long he had had it?"

"No."

"The cloth," Calloway said. "You have seen it."

"I have."

"What is its purpose?"

"I do not know. It is folded. The shape of the Gear is pressed into the weave."

"The Gear," Orme said. "You found it at the mill."

"I did."

"Did Vale put it there?"

"I do not know."

"Did he tell you anything about it?"

"He told me he had read the inscription and could not say what it was for. He told me the response was in the stone, not in the object."

Orme looked at Calloway. Calloway looked at the clerk. The clerk wrote on his paper.

"The office has questions," Calloway said. "The office has records. The office has a duty. You have the box. You have the things Vale left with you. You have the Gear you found. You have what you saw and what you were told. The office will continue to ask. The office will ask about the journal. The office will ask about the letters. The office will ask about the bottles. The office will ask about the things he left with other people. The office will ask about the February paper."

He paused. He looked at the paper in his hands.

"The office has considered what you have said," he said. "The office has considered what the clerk has recorded. The office has considered what Vale understood. The office has considered what the parish allows. The box remains in your keeping. The office does not take it. The office does not open it. The office does not compel you to open it. The record is made. The statement is signed."

He looked at Orme. Orme looked at the floor. He did not say anything.

Orme sat for a moment longer. Then he stood up. He put on his hat. He looked at me.

"The office has a duty," he said. "The duty is not finished."

He went out.

The clerk looked at me. He did not say anything. He turned back to his paper. He wrote the final line. He set the pencil down. He turned the page. He looked at me.

"Sign," he said.

I signed my name at the bottom of the line.

The clerk closed the ledger. He set it on the shelf. He took off his hat. He set it on the desk.

"The constable will tell you what to do next," he said.

"I know," I said.

He nodded. He remained at the desk.

Calloway stood by the fireplace. He looked at me.

He paused. He turned his hat in his hands.

"The office has another matter," he said. "It is not the box. It is not the journal. It is not the letters. It is an examination. The office examines men who are not of the office. The examination is not a position. It is not a rank. It is not a badge. It is a test. The office wants to know what you notice when you look. The office wants to know whether you can say what you saw without adding what you think. The office wants to know whether there is more in you than the seeing."

He looked at me. He did not smile. He did not frown. He was simply stating what was.

"Will you take it?" he said.

"I will," I said.

"The constable will send for you," he said. "Not today. Not tomorrow. When the time is set."

He went out.

The clerk wrote one more line in the ledger. He set the pencil down. He closed the ledger. He set it on the shelf. He stood up. He took off his hat. He set it on the desk.

"You may go," he said.

He went out.

I went out.

The lane was quiet. The house was shut. I set the key in the lock and turned it. The door opened. The room was the same.

The box was in the press. The papers were on the table. The bundle was tied. The ruled sheet was folded. The service file was on the left. The notebook was closed.

I did not know what the examination would be. I did not know what the office would see. I did not know what the motives had been. I did not know whether Orme was right or Calloway was right or whether both were right and the rightness did not help.

But I knew the box was mine, and I knew the examination was coming.
