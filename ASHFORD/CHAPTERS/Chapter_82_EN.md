CHAPTER 82 — THE NEXT TEST

I did not wait long. On Wednesday morning, a note came under the door before I had finished breakfast. It said only: The office. Nine o’clock. Bring nothing.

I had thought the examination might pause. I had thought there would be days of waiting, of watching, of wondering what the office intended. But the note was brief and ordinary, and I folded it and put it in my pocket and went out.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open, and the crowd was already there.

There were more of them than before. I counted thirty, then thirty-two, then lost count as they shifted and moved. Some stood in groups, some stood alone. The man with the notebook was there, standing apart from the others, his notebook closed but in his hand. The older man with the frayed cuffs was there, leaning against the wall by the pump. Peale was there, the man with the clean coat, and he looked at me when I arrived and nodded once, as though he had been expecting me.

I nodded back.

The door opened. Calloway came out, followed by the man in the grey coat. Calloway did not speak at first. He looked at the crowd the way a man looks at a thing he has seen before and knows he will see again.

“There is a house at the end of Tanner’s Lane,” he said. “A family named Harkness lives there. One of their children has lost a book. The book is small, bound in dark leather, and it has writing in it. The child says she last saw it in the garden, but the garden has been searched and the book is not there.”

He paused. No one spoke.

“You will find it,” he said. “You will bring it to this office by noon. If you bring the book, you will be recorded. If you do not bring the book, you will be recorded as well. The book belongs to the child. It is not the book that matters. It is that you find it.”

He turned and went back inside. The door closed.

The crowd stood for a moment. Then it moved.

I had thought there would be instructions. I had thought there would be a map, or a description, or some sign of what the house looked like. But there was nothing except a name and a place and a child’s lost book.

The man with the notebook was already walking. I saw him move through the crowd, his head down, his notebook open, writing something as he went. The older man followed him, and I saw them turn down the lane toward the river.

Peale was not moving. He was standing where he had stood, watching the crowd. He looked at me, and I looked at him, and he said, “Tanner’s Lane is on the other side of the church.”

“I know,” I said.

“Do you know where the Harkness house is?”

“No.”

He nodded, as though that was the answer he had expected. “Neither do I. But I know who does.”

He walked away, and I did not follow him.

I went the other way. I walked down the lane past the office, past the chandler’s, past the pump where the older man had shaken his head on Tuesday. I had not been to Tanner’s Lane before, but I had seen it on the map in the office, the map that Calloway had left on the desk one afternoon when I was waiting to be called. I had not looked at it carefully. I had only glanced at it, the way a man glances at a thing he does not think he will need.

I remembered the direction. That was all.

The lane opened onto a narrow street with houses on both sides, the doors low, the windows small. A woman was sweeping the step of the second house. I stopped and asked her where the Harkness house was.

She pointed to the end of the lane, where a hedge grew high and a gate stood open.

“The garden is through there,” she said. “But the child has already looked and found nothing.”

I thanked her and walked to the gate.

The garden was larger than I had expected, a long strip of grass with trees at the far end and a vegetable patch on one side. The hedge was thick and green, and the gate stood open the way the woman had said.

I walked the length of the garden, slowly. I looked at the grass. I looked at the vegetable patch. I looked at the trees. I did not know what I was looking for, only that the book was not here, or not where the child had last seen it.

I stopped by the vegetable patch. The soil was soft and dark, and there were footprints in it, small footprints, the kind a child would leave. I followed them to the far end of the patch, where the beans grew tall and the earth was disturbed.

The book was there, half buried in the soil, the dark leather stained with wet earth. I picked it up. It was small, as Calloway had said, and it had writing in it, faded brown ink that I could not read without better light.

I stood up and turned to go back to the gate.

Peale was there. He was standing by the gate, not in the garden, not on the lane, but exactly at the edge of the gate, as though he had been waiting for me.

“You found it,” he said.

“I found it,” I said.

“How did you know where to look?”

“I did not know,” I said. “I looked.”

He nodded, as though that was the answer he had expected. “The others went to the house first. They are still there, waiting for the child’s mother to let them in.”

“And you?”

“I came here. I thought the garden was more likely.”

“Why?”

He did not answer. He looked at the book in my hand, and then he looked at me, and then he turned and walked back down the lane.

I did not follow him. I went to the office.

The office was quieter than it had been in the morning. The crowd had gone. The man in the grey coat was standing on the step, and he looked at the book in my hand and did not seem surprised by anything he saw.

“You are the first,” he said.

I did not know whether that was good or bad. I only handed him the book, and he looked at it, and then he wrote something in a small book of his own.

“What is your name?” he said.

I told him.

He wrote it down.

“You may go,” he said.

I went.

I walked home slowly. I did not know whether I had passed anything, or failed anything, or whether there was anything to pass or fail. I only knew that I had found the book, and that Peale had been standing at the gate, watching me, and that he had said he thought the garden was more likely.

I did not know whether he had let me find it, or whether he had simply been slower, or whether he had stood at the gate because he wanted to see what I would do.

I set the key in the lock and turned it. The door opened. The room was the same.

I sat down and looked at the window, and I thought about Peale’s question.

_How did you know where to look?_

I had not known. I had only remembered the direction, and followed the footprints, and looked where the child had left them. That was not knowing. That was only being willing to look.

I did not know whether that was enough.

But I knew the examination had changed. It was no longer rooms and objects and statements. It was the town, and the people, and a book buried in the soil of a garden, and a man standing at a gate, watching.

The next test would be different again. I did not know what it would be. But I knew I would be ready to look.
