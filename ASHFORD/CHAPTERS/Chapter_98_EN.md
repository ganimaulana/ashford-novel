CHAPTER 98 — THE HUNTER

The note came before dawn. I had not slept well. The lock, the symbol, and the clerk's unchanged expression had turned over in my mind like a stone that would not settle. When I opened my door, the paper was on the floor. It said only: The granary. Six o'clock. Bring nothing.

I dressed and went down. The courtyard was grey and cold, the stone still damp with night dew. Sixteen of us stood in the dark, waiting. I counted them as they arrived—fifteen others, including myself. The number was smaller than it had been the day before. Weaver's group was gone. I did not see them leave. I did not ask where they had been sent.

Hunter was there, standing near the wall, his hands in his pockets. He was not looking at anyone. He was looking at the granary, which stood at the far end of the eastern grounds—a long, low building of stone and timber with a sagging roof and heavy oak doors bound with iron.

Calloway came out of the main building. He waited until the murmuring ceased.

"You have been divided into four groups," he said. "The groups remain as they were. You will be taken to the granary. Each of you will receive a sealed partial manifest. The manifest will list deliveries to be made from the granary to locations around the grounds. There are six deliveries in total for each group. Each candidate holds two of the six entries. You must share information with your group to learn the complete set. You may not share information with other groups. You may not assist another group. You may not enter a room that is not on your manifest. You will complete the deliveries before the bell rings at noon. The three groups with the highest combined completion will be recorded as passing. The last group will be recorded as not passing. Within each group, the two candidates with the lowest individual contribution will be reviewed."

He paused.

"The granary has two floors and a service passage. The ground floor has four rooms. The upper floor has two. The service passage connects three ground-floor rooms but is narrow and has a broken step. You will choose your routes. You will manage your loads. You will decide when to cooperate and when to move alone. The office will observe all of it."

He turned and walked back inside.

The clerk stepped forward with sixteen slips of paper. Each was sealed with blue wax. I took mine. I did not break it.

The man in the grey coat was waiting with a cart at the gate. We followed him across the grounds. The granary stood at the far end of the eastern enclosure, beyond the storehouse where we had found the lock. Its doors were heavy, the iron bands rusted. The man in the grey coat unlocked them and pushed them open with a sound that echoed inside the building.

The interior was dim, the air thick with the smell of grain and dust and old timber. Light came through high windows, throwing long shadows across the floor. There were four rooms on the ground floor, each with a number painted in black on the door. A narrow staircase led to the upper floor, its steps worn in the centre. At the back of the building, a passage ran between three of the ground-floor rooms—the service passage, I assumed. It was narrow, the walls close on either side, the floor uneven.

The clerk followed us in. He pointed to a table near the door.

"Open your manifests here," he said. "You may begin when you are ready."

I broke the wax seal. The manifest was a single sheet, folded in three. I unfolded it. There were two entries, each with a room number, an item description, a weight, and a delivery location. The first entry was room one, ground floor: a crate of tools, heavy, to be delivered to the storehouse. The second entry was room five, upper floor: a bundle of papers, light, to be delivered to the clerk's office in the main building.

I looked up. Bishop was reading his manifest, his face unchanged. Ford was reading his, his eyes moving quickly across the page. Pearce was reading his, his hands at his sides.

"Room one," Bishop said. "Tools."

"Room five," Ford said. "Papers."

"Room two," Pearce said. "Grain sacks."

I looked at the other groups. Hunter was already moving toward the stairs, his manifest in his hand. He did not look at anyone. He simply climbed, his steps even, his head low, his body moving with the same economy I had noticed from the first day.

I looked at the service passage at the back of the granary. I could see the broken step near the entrance—the wood splintered and dark, the tread missing a fragment where it had rotted through. It would be a hazard for anyone carrying a load. Any man who stepped on it with weight on his back would risk twisting an ankle or dropping his cargo.

"We need to share," I said. "We each have two entries. We need all six to plan the route."

Bishop nodded. Ford nodded. Pearce nodded.

We pooled our manifests on the table. The six deliveries were:
1. Room one, ground floor: crate of tools, heavy, to storehouse.
2. Room two, ground floor: grain sacks, medium, to eastern gate.
3. Room three, ground floor: iron fittings, heavy, to main building.
4. Room four, ground floor: canvas bundles, light, to dining hall.
5. Room five, upper floor: papers, light, to clerk's office.
6. Room six, upper floor: lanterns, medium, to field.

"Two on the upper floor," Ford said. "Four on the ground."

"The upper floor is faster between rooms," Bishop said. "But the stairs are narrow. We can only carry light loads up. And there are three other groups. The stairs will be congested."

"The ground floor is longer," I said. "But we can carry heavier loads. And we can make fewer trips. The service passage cuts the distance between rooms one, two, and three."

I looked at the passage. The broken step was clearly visible from where we stood. A man carrying a heavy load would have to step over it carefully or risk falling.

"If we use the passage," I said, "we avoid the long walk around the outside of the building. But that step is broken. We would have to step over it with a load. It is not worth the risk."

Bishop looked at the passage. "It is too narrow for two of us side by side. And the step is at the entrance. Any load that shifts will throw us off balance."

"Then we avoid it," I said. "We take the longer route around the outside. It costs time, but it is safer."

Ford nodded. "Safer. But slower."

I looked at the stairs. Hunter's group was already on the upper floor, moving between rooms. I could see them through the gap in the staircase railing—Hunter carrying a light load, his steps precise, his head low. He was not rushing. He was moving at a steady, economical pace that used no more energy than necessary.

"Hunter is taking the upper floor," I said.

Bishop looked up. "He will be bottlenecked at the stairs. Other groups will be using them too."

"Unless he planned around it," I said.

But I did not believe he had. The upper floor had only two rooms. Once he completed those, he would have to come back down and move the ground-floor items. The stairs would be congested with other groups coming up and going down. It would slow him. It seemed like a mistake.

"We take the ground floor," I said. "We carry the heavy items first. We make fewer trips. We avoid the stairs and the broken step."

Ford and Bishop agreed. Pearce did not speak, but he picked up the crate of tools without being asked, settling it onto his shoulder as though it weighed nothing.

We moved out. The ground floor was clear. We carried the crate of tools to the storehouse first, a distance of perhaps two hundred yards across uneven ground. I felt the strain in my shoulders immediately, but I kept my pace steady. Bishop and Ford moved together, the heaviest loads balanced between them. Pearce moved ahead, his light load allowing him to set the delivery point and return for the next.

At the third delivery, I looked back at the granary. Two other groups had chosen to use the service passage. The first group had just entered. I saw the broken step. One of the men stepped on it, and the wood gave. He staggered, his load tilting, and had to catch himself against the wall. His companion stopped, waiting. The delay was brief—no more than a few seconds—but it was plain to see. The man recovered, adjusted his grip, and continued. But the moment of instability was there, visible to anyone who was watching.

I thought of Hunter. He had chosen the stairs. He had not chosen the service passage. I had assumed it was because he preferred the shorter route on the upper floor. Now I wondered whether he had seen the broken step and decided it was not worth the risk.

I looked up at the upper floor. Hunter's group was moving between the two rooms, their loads gone. They had finished the upper floor. Now they would have to descend and move the ground-floor items. The stairs would be congested with other groups coming up and going down. It would slow them.

I felt a moment of satisfaction. We had chosen the better route.

Then I saw Hunter at the bottom of the stairs. He was not waiting. He was moving toward the service passage.

I watched. He stepped to the entrance, looked down at the broken step, and shook his head almost imperceptibly. He did not enter. He turned and walked around the outside of the building, following the longer path.

He had seen the step. He had chosen the longer route.

But why? The service passage would have saved him time. Unless he had calculated that the delay from the broken step—the recovery, the adjustment, the cautious stepping—would cost more than the longer walk. Unless he had seen the other group enter and knew they would block the passage.

I did not have time to think about it. We had our own deliveries to complete. We moved on, the canvas bundles to the dining hall, then back to the granary for the final delivery.

When we returned to the granary with the last load, Hunter's group was just emerging from the far side of the building. They had completed all six deliveries. So had the group from the upper floor that had used the stairs without incident. We were third. The fourth group was still inside, struggling with a load at the broken step—one of their men had twisted his ankle on the splintered wood and was leaning against the wall, his face tight with pain, his load set down beside him.

The clerk stood at the door, his ledger open. He wrote as each group returned.

When the bell began to ring, the fourth group had not completed all six deliveries. The clerk marked them as not passing.

"The group has not passed," the clerk said. "Two of you will leave the examination. The other two will continue under review."

Two men from that group stepped forward. They did not speak. They did not look at the rest of us. They gathered their coats and followed the man in the grey coat out of the granary.

Fourteen of us remained.

The clerk closed his ledger. He looked at us, standing in the yard, the morning light behind him.

"The next stage will be individual," he said. "You will receive no further group assignments. You will be told when to assemble."

He turned and walked away.

I stood in the yard, my shoulders aching, my hands trembling slightly from the weight I had carried. I looked at the granary, at the broken step, at the stairs where Hunter's group had climbed. I thought about Hunter's choice—the stairs, the ground floor, the longer walk around the outside. He had seen the hazard I had seen only after someone else had been hurt by it. He had also chosen the slower route, and he had finished before us.

Bishop came up beside me. "We passed," he said.

"We passed," I agreed.

Ford nodded toward the granary door. "Hunter's group passed too."

"I know."

"Did you see the broken step?"

"Yes."

"And the weight?" Bishop said.

I looked at him. "What weight?"

"The crate in room three," Bishop said. "The iron fittings. Hunter's group carried it. One of them almost dropped it. Hunter misjudged the weight. The manifest said medium. It was heavier."

I had not seen that. I had been watching the broken step, the other groups, the clock in my head. I had not noticed Hunter's group handling the iron fittings.

"Even careful men misjudge," Ford said.

I looked at Hunter. He was standing near the granary door, his arms crossed, his head tilted. He was looking at the ground, not at anyone. But I had the feeling he was aware of me watching him.

I did not wave. I did not approach. I simply stood where I was, watching.

Then he lifted his head.

Our eyes met across the yard.

He did not smile. He did not nod. He simply held my gaze for a second—no more than that—before turning away.

But that second was enough. He knew I had been watching. He knew I had seen his choice, his avoidance of the broken step, his recovery from the misjudged weight. And he had let me see it.

I turned and walked back toward the main building. The sun was higher now, the light clearer. Fourteen of us remained. The next stage would be individual. No group to cover my weakness. No Bishop to carry the heaviest load. No Ford to pull me up when I stumbled. No Pearce to take the lightest delivery and move ahead.

I thought about the manifest. I had decoded the smudged room number correctly—room three, not eight. That had been observation, not luck. But observation alone was not enough. Hunter had observed the broken step and the weight and the congestion. He had interpreted all of it and made a choice that looked slower but was safer.

I had observed the smudge and the step and the other groups. But I had interpreted them separately. I had not connected them into a single plan.

The examination was not about seeing the most. It was about understanding what you saw.

I climbed the stairs to my room. The courtyard was empty. The granary stood at the far end of the field, dark and silent.

I sat on the bed and thought about the smudge on the manifest and the broken step in the granary and the weight Hunter had misjudged. Observation was not understanding. But it was the beginning of it. And for now, it was all I had.

Fourteen of us remained. The next stage would be individual. The examination had changed again, and I had changed with it.
