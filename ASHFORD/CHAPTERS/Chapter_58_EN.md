CHAPTER 58 — THE STRANGER

I did not go back to the common for three days.

I told myself it was because the house would not appear, that it came on its own schedule, that there was no use standing at the edge of the grass waiting for a door that opened when it chose.

The truth was that I was afraid.

Not of the house. Not of the passage. Not of the door that opened from both sides.

I was afraid of what I had almost done — of my hand reaching for the gear, of the weight of it in my coat, of how close I had come to putting it in the depression and turning it, without knowing what I was opening, without knowing who had carved the fresh signs that marked the way.

I had told myself I was keeping the door closed. I had told myself I was the keeper.

But I had stood at that door, on the inside, in the candlelight, and I had almost opened it. And the only reason I had not was that I had been afraid.

That was not the same thing as choosing.

I sat at my desk and looked at the gear, and I thought about the difference, and I did not like what I saw.

---

On the third day, Margaret found me at the bakery.

She had seen me, I think, in the street, walking with my head down, not meeting anyone's eyes. She came out from behind the counter, wiping her hands on her apron, and she did not ask me how I was, because that was not the way she asked.

"There is a man," she said, "who has been asking about you."

I stopped. I had not meant to stop. I had meant to pass by the bakery, to nod, to continue. But the words stopped me as surely as a hand on my arm.

"What man?"

"I do not know his name. He is not from Ashford. He came in two days ago, asked for bread, asked where a man named Ashcroft might be found." She looked at me. "I told him there were Ashcrofts, and that the one people asked about lived on the High Street, and he thanked me and went out, and I have not seen him since."

"Did he say what he wanted?"

"He did not say." She studied me a moment. "He had the look of a man who has walked a long way. His boots were worn at the heel, and he carried a bag that had seen weather. He asked about you the way a man asks about something he has been sent to find."

I stood in the doorway of the bakery. I thought of Crowe's words: a man who is not from the town, who has the look of a man who has come a long way, who asks questions as if he already knows the answers.

"What did he look like?" I said.

"Middle height. Dark hair, grey at the temples. A scar on the back of his right hand." She frowned. "He had a mark on his coat, I think. A sign, sewn or pinned — I did not see it clearly. He turned it so I would not."

"A mark."

"A sign." She shook her head. "I did not see it clearly, Leon. I am sorry. But I did not like him, and I did not like that he was asking about you, and I thought you should know."

I thanked her. I left the bakery. I walked down the High Street, and I thought about the man who had come to Ashford, who had asked for bread and asked where a man named Ashcroft might be found, who wore a mark on his coat that he turned away from the eyes of the town.

A sign. A mark.

I could not yet tell what sign he wore. I could not say what mark he carried.

But I knew that there were two marks in Ashford — the circle, and the keeper's sign — and I knew that one of them was older than the Order, and I knew that a man had come a long way asking about the gear, and I knew that I had to find him before he found me.

---

I did not find him that day.

I walked the streets of Ashford from the High Street to the river, from the mill to the common, from the brewery to the vestry, and I did not see him. I asked, where I could ask without drawing attention, and no one had seen the man Margaret had described. It was as if he had come, and asked, and gone — or as if he had come, and asked, and was waiting, somewhere, for me to find him on his own terms.

I went home as the light failed. I sat at my desk. I did not write in my notebook. I sat in the dark and thought about the man, and about the mark on his coat, and about the gear in my drawer, and about the passage beneath the common, and about the fresh-carved signs that marked the way.

And I decided that I would not wait for him to find me. I would go back to the common. I would go down into the passage. And I would wait for him there, where the keeper's signs marked the way, where the door opened from both sides, where whatever he had come to find would have to be found.

---

I went to the common that night.

The house was not there. I had not expected it to be. I had come to the edge of the grass, and I had looked at the pressed shape of the floor, and I had known, without having to test it, that the house was not there, that the trapdoor was not there, that the passage was not there — because the passage was only there when the house was there, and the house was only there when it chose to be.

I stood at the edge of the common in the dark.

I was not alone.

I did not see him at first. He was standing at the far edge of the common, where the grass met the lower pasture, in the shadow of the trees. I did not see him until he moved, and then I saw him clearly — middle height, dark hair grey at the temples, a bag at his feet, and on his coat, where Margaret had said it would be, a mark.

I could not see the mark clearly in the dark. But I could see that he had turned it toward me.

"I have been waiting for you," he said.

His voice was quiet. It carried across the common as if the grass were water and the night were still.

"You have been asking about me," I said.

"I have been asking about the gear."

We stood at opposite edges of the common, in the dark, with the pressed shape of the house between us.

"You are the keeper," he said. It was not a question.

"I am."

"Then you know what the gear is for."

"I know what the door is for," I said. "I do not know what the gear is for. I do not know what the keeper's sign is for. I do not know who you are, or what you want, or why you have come to Ashford asking about a gear that has been carried in this town for fifty years."

The man was silent a moment.

"I am not from Ashford," he said. "I am from the place the keeper's sign comes from. I have walked a long way to find the gear, because the gear was made to be used, and it has not been used in fifty years, and the door has not been opened in fifty years, and the time for closing it has passed."

"Passed?"

"The door was never meant to stay closed," he said. "It was meant to stay closed until the keeper understood what it was for. Vale did not understand. He kept it closed because he was afraid, and he taught you to be afraid, and now the door has been closed so long that the signs are wearing away."

"The signs."

"The keeper's signs." He took a step toward me. "They are not a message for you. They are not a warning. They are a count — a count of the years the door has been closed, cut into the stone by each keeper who did not understand what he was keeping."

I stood very still.

The fresh-carved signs. The passage marked at intervals. I had thought someone had been carving them recently. I had thought someone had prepared the way.

"Vale carved them," the man said. "All of them. One for each year. Fifty years. Fifty signs, marking the way to a door he did not understand and was afraid to open. He did not tell you, because he did not know what they meant. He only knew that they had to be kept, and he kept them."

I looked at the grass. At the pressed shape of the house. At the dark where the trapdoor would be, if the house were there.

"The house," I said. "Who built the house?"

"I do not know who built it," he said. "I know only that it was built to hold the passage, and that it appears when the door needs to be opened, and that it disappears when the door is closed and the time has not yet come."

"And the time has come?"

He did not answer at once.

"I have walked a long way," he said. "I have followed the signs from the place the keeper's sign comes from, through towns and fields and years. I have come to find the gear, because the gear was made to open the door, and the door has been closed too long, and whatever is behind it has been waiting for the keeper to decide."

"Decide what?"

"Whether he will open the door," the man said, "or let it be opened by someone else."

He turned. He walked away across the common, into the dark of the trees, and his voice came back to me across the grass, quiet and level:

"The signs are a count, keeper. A count of years. And the count is running out."

I stood at the edge of the common in the dark, alone.

The man was gone. The house was not there.

The signs — the fifty signs — were beneath my feet, beneath the pressed grass, cut into the stone of a passage I had walked with a candle, counting them, losing count, thinking they had been freshly made.

They had been made by Vale. One for each year. Fifty years. Fifty signs, marking the way to a door he had been afraid to open, a door he had kept closed because he had not understood what he was keeping.

And the man who had come to find the gear had told me that the count was running out.

I did not know who he was. I did not know where the keeper's sign came from. I could not say whether he was the third hand, or the first hand, or something older than all of them.

But I knew that the signs were not fresh. I knew that they were a count. I knew that Vale had carved them, one for each year, not understanding what they meant.

And I knew that the man had come to Ashford to open the door — and that he had told me the truth, or a truth, or a lie that wore the shape of the truth.

I walked home through the dark streets. I sat at my desk. I did not write in my notebook.

I took the gear from the drawer and held it in my hand, and I looked at the circle and the short line, and I thought about the fifty signs, and the fifty years, and the man who had come a long way to tell me the count was running out.

I could not tell for certain what was behind the door. I had no way of knowing who had built the house. I could not say who the man was, or who had sent him, or what he truly wanted.

But I knew that the signs were not fresh. I knew that they were a count. And I knew that Vale had not been afraid because he understood the door. He had been afraid because he did not.

And the man who had come to find the gear — the man who wore a mark on his coat that he turned toward me in the dark — had come to find a keeper who would not be afraid.

I could not tell for certain whether I was that keeper. But I knew that the count was running out.

And I knew that I had to decide, before it did, whether I would open the door — or let it be opened for me.
