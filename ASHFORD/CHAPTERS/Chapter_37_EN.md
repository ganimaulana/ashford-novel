# Chapter 37 — The Greyhound and the Letters

I went to the Greyhound the morning after Calloway's offer, because a man who has taken a coin does not wait for the work to find him. The inn stood at the corner of High Street and the lane that led to the old mill, its sign faded to the colour of smoke, its door worn smooth by a hundred years. The innkeeper, a man named Finch—not the archivist, but a cousin—had the tired patience of one who has been asked the same question a hundred times.

"You're the constable's man," he said, before I had spoken. It was not a question.

"I am the constable's man," I said.

He looked at me with the wariness of a man who has been expecting trouble and does not welcome it. "Then you want the room. The one at the top of the stairs. The one that does not stay as it is left."

He led me up a narrow staircase that creaked under every step, past doors that were shut and quiet, to the top of the house, where a single door stood apart from the rest. He unlocked it and stood aside to let me enter. The room was small and plain: a bed, a table, a chair, a basin on a stand, and a cold hearth. The window looked out over the yard and the roofs of the town. The cold in the room was the kind that seeped through the walls and made even the fire feel distant.

"This is the one," said Finch. "I lock it every night, and every morning something has changed. The bed is rumpled, though I did not sleep in it. The basin is less full, though I did not use it. The chair has moved, though I did not sit in it. And the ashes are warm, though I did not light a fire."

I looked at the grate. The ashes were cold now, but I could see the grey of them, and the shape of a fire that had been laid and lit and burned down.

"Has anyone been in this room?" I asked.

"No one. The key never leaves my hand. The window is bolted from the inside. There is no other door. I have checked the floorboards, the ceiling, the walls—there is no way in that I have found. And yet something comes."

"Does it happen every night?"

"Every night since the autumn. I could not tell you the day it started. One morning I opened the door and the basin was empty, and I thought the maid had been careless. The next morning the chair was moved. The next, the bed. I have tried everything I know, and I have found nothing."

I walked to the window and checked the bolt. It was secure. I looked at the floor, the ceiling, the walls—nothing. I asked if he had ever stayed in the room himself, and he said no, he had not, because he was not a man who would sleep in a room that was not his.

"Leave the key with me," I said. "I will stay here tonight, and I will see what I see."

Finch looked at me as though I had offered to do something foolish, but he handed me the key and went down the stairs.

---

I spent the rest of the day in the room, noting its shape and size, the position of every object, the thickness of the dust on the sill, the angle of the chair, the depth of the ashes. I took out my book and drew a plan, marking each thing with its exact place. I wanted to be able to say, come morning, what had changed, and what had not.

The light faded, and I lit a candle and waited. At the hour of nine, the lamp did not burn at the end of the lane—I could not see it from this window, but I felt its absence the way a man feels the absence of a clock that has stopped. I set it down in my mind, and I waited.

Nothing came. The room remained as I had left it. I heard the sounds of the inn below—the talk of men, the clink of glasses, the creak of the floor—and I heard them fade as the night deepened. At midnight I was still awake, and still nothing had moved. I slept in the chair, because I did not want to disturb the bed, and I woke at dawn with the cold on my face and the first grey light in the window. I looked at the room. The chair was where I had left it. The bed was as it had been. The basin was full. The ashes were cold.

I unlocked the door and went down to Finch.

"Nothing," I said. "The room did not change."

Finch looked at me, and then he looked past me at the room, and I saw something in his face that had not been there before. "I have heard," he said slowly, "that the room only changes when someone has said his name."

"Whose name?"

"The name of the man who died in that room. Fifteen years ago. He was a traveller, a stranger, and he died in the night. No one knew who he was, and no one came to claim his things. The room was locked, and the key was lost, and for a long time no one went into it. Then the room was opened again, and the changes began."

I stood at the top of the stairs, and I thought of what I had heard: that the room changed when someone had said the name of the man who had died there. I had not said his name, because I did not know it. And the room had not changed.

"Did you say his name last night?" I asked.

"I did not. Because I do not know it. No one knows it. He was a stranger, and he died with no name, and he left no trace."

I looked at the room, and I understood that I had been asking the wrong question. I had been asking, "What moves?" when I should have been asking, "What triggers the movement?" I thanked Finch and went home to write it down. The room had taught me that the trigger is not the question—the source is. I did not know the source, but I had learned something: the room did not move on its own. It moved because something was listening.

---

The days between the Greyhound and the next case were quiet. I went to Margaret's for bread, and the shop was emptier than it had been the week before. Two customers sat at separate tables, and neither spoke. Margaret wiped the counter and did not smile.

"You look like a man who has found a door and cannot open it," she said.

"I have found a door," I said. "And I have found that the door is locked, and the key is lost, and no one remembers what the door was for."

She set the loaf on the counter and did not let it go. "That is the way of this town. There are doors everywhere, and most of them are locked, and the ones that are open lead to rooms that do not stay as they are left." She paused, and looked at me. "You haven't been in for three days. I was beginning to think you'd found another door."

I took the bread and went home, and I spent the afternoon reading the old notebooks I had found in the drawer. They spoke of rooms that changed, of letters that came from nowhere, of clocks that struck hours they had not been wound for. They were the notes of someone who had been asking the same questions I was asking, and they had not found the answers either.

On the Saturday, I went to Calloway's office to report what I had seen. He listened without interrupting, and when I had finished, he did not speak for a long time.

"A room that listens," he said at last. "And a name that is lost."

"Yes."

He looked at me the way a man looks at a thing he has been trying to remember. "There is a woman on High Street," he said, "who has been receiving letters. Letters that speak of tomorrow. I think you should see her."

I nodded, and he gave me the address, and I went out into the cold.

The woman's name was Mrs. Hart, and she lived in a house that had seen better days—the paint was peeling, the gate was loose, and the windows were dark even in the daylight. She opened the door when I knocked, and she looked at me the way a woman looks at a man who has been sent by the constable: with caution, and with hope, and with the suspicion that he will not be able to help.

"I have been receiving letters," she said. "They come in the post, with the same stamp, the same hand, the same paper. They speak of things that have not yet happened. And they have been right, every time."

"May I see one?"

She went to a drawer and took out a thin envelope, yellowed at the edges, and handed it to me. I opened it and read:

*The flour will be up a penny before the week is out. The man who lives across the lane will take ill on Tuesday. The rain will come on Wednesday, and it will not stop until Friday.*

The date on the envelope was a week old. I looked at her.

"All of those things came true," she said. "The flour went up. The man fell ill. The rain came and did not stop."

"Who sent these?"

"I do not know. They are addressed to me, in my name, but they have no sender. I have asked the postmaster, and he says they come through the regular post, but he cannot tell me who sent them."

I looked at the letter again, and I saw something I had not seen before: the date on the envelope was tomorrow's date. The letter had been postmarked for the day after it was written, but it had arrived the day before.

"These are not predictions," I said. "They are instructions."

I left Mrs. Hart with the letter and went home to write it down. The letters were not prophecies; they were part of a dialogue. And dialogue, unlike prophecy, could be answered.

I shut the book and sat with the question. The room at the Greyhound had taught me that a trigger could be impossible to pull. The letters on High Street were teaching me that a prediction could be a condition. I did not yet know what the clock on Mill Lane would teach me, but I was beginning to suspect that all three cases were parts of the same pattern—a pattern of triggers and conditions, of watching and being watched, of the world responding to the act of attention.

I was learning, slowly, that the anomalies were not the things themselves. They were the responses. And the responses followed rules.

I did not know the rules yet. But I was beginning to see their shape.
