# Chapter 37 — The Greyhound

I went to the Greyhound on the morning after the constable's offer, because a man who has taken a coin does not wait for the work to find him. The inn stood at the corner of High Street and the lane that led to the old mill, and it had the look of a place that had been standing long enough to know the secrets of every room. The sign above the door was painted with a dog that had faded to the colour of smoke, and the door itself was worn smooth by the hands of a hundred years.

The innkeeper was a man named Finch—not the Finch Calloway had mentioned, but another of the name, a cousin perhaps, who had the tired patience of a man who has been asked the same question a hundred times. He was perhaps fifty, with a cloth over his shoulder and the smell of ale and woodsmoke about him.

"You're the constable's man," he said, before I had spoken. It was not a question.

"I am the constable's man," I said.

He looked at me the way a man looks at a thing he has been expecting and does not welcome. "Then you want the room. The one at the top of the stairs. The one that does not stay as it is left."

He led me up a narrow staircase that creaked under every step, past doors that were shut and quiet, to the top of the house, where a single door stood apart from the rest. He took a key from his pocket and unlocked it, and stood aside to let me enter.

The room was small and plain: a bed, a table, a chair, a basin on a stand, and a cold hearth. The window looked out over the yard and the roofs of the town, and the light that came through it was thin and grey. The cold in the room was the kind that seeped through the walls and made even the fire feel distant.

"This is the one," said Finch. "I lock it every night, and every morning I unlock it, and every morning something has changed. The bed is rumpled, though I did not sleep in it. The basin is less full, though I did not use it. The chair has moved, though I did not sit in it. And the ashes are warm, though I did not light a fire."

I looked at the grate. The ashes were cold now, but I could see the grey of them, and the shape of a fire that had been laid and lit and burned down.

"Has anyone been in this room," I said.

"No one," said Finch. "The key never leaves my hand. The window is bolted from the inside. There is no other door. I have checked the floorboards, the ceiling, the walls—there is no way in that I have found. And yet something comes."

"Does it happen every night?"

"Every night since the autumn," he said. "I could not tell you the day it started. I only know that one morning I opened the door and the basin was empty, and I thought the maid had been careless. The next morning the chair was moved. The next, the bed. I have tried leaving the room exactly as I found it. I have tried marking the positions of the objects. I have tried everything I know, and I have found nothing."

I walked to the window and checked the bolt. It was secure. I looked at the floor, the ceiling, the walls—nothing. I asked if he had ever stayed in the room himself, and he said no, he had not, because he was not a man who would sleep in a room that was not his.

"Leave the key with me," I said. "I will stay here tonight, and I will see what I see."

Finch looked at me the way a man looks at a man who has offered to do something foolish, and then he handed me the key and went down the stairs.

---

I spent the rest of the day in the room, noting its shape and size, the position of every object, the thickness of the dust on the sill, the angle of the chair, the depth of the ashes. I took out my book and drew a plan, marking each thing with its exact place. I wanted to be able to say, come morning, what had changed, and what had not.

The light faded, and I lit a candle and waited.

At the hour of nine, the lamp did not burn at the end of the lane—I could not see it from this window, but I felt its absence the way a man feels the absence of a clock that has stopped. I set it down in my mind, and I waited.

Nothing came. The room remained as I had left it. I heard the sounds of the inn below—the talk of men, the clink of glasses, the creak of the floor—and I heard them fade as the night deepened. At midnight I was still awake, and still nothing had moved.

I slept in the chair, because I did not want to disturb the bed, and I woke at dawn with the cold on my face and the first grey light in the window. I looked at the room.

The chair was where I had left it. The bed was as it had been. The basin was full. The ashes were cold.

I unlocked the door and went down to Finch.

"Nothing," I said. "The room did not change."

Finch looked at me, and then he looked past me at the room, and I saw something in his face that had not been there before.

"I have heard," he said slowly, "that the room only changes when someone has said his name."

"Whose name?"

"The name of the man who died in that room," said Finch. "Fifteen years ago. He was a traveller, a stranger, and he died in the night. No one knew who he was, and no one came to claim his things. The room was locked, and the key was lost, and for a long time no one went into it. Then the room was opened again, and the changes began."

I stood at the top of the stairs, and I thought of what I had heard: that the room changed when someone had said the name of the man who had died there. I had not said his name, because I did not know it. And the room had not changed.

"Did you say his name last night?" I asked.

"I did not," said Finch. "Because I do not know it. No one knows it. He was a stranger, and he died with no name, and he left no trace."

I looked at the room, and I understood that I had been asking the wrong question. I had been asking, "What moves?" when I should have been asking, "What triggers the movement?"

I thanked Finch and went home to write it down.

---

*14 Jan. The room at the Greyhound: objects move only when the name of the man who died there is spoken. No one knows the name. The trigger is speech, but the speech is not possible, because the name is not known. The room has taught me that the trigger is not the question—the source is. I do not know the source, and I do not know how to find it. But I have learned something: the room does not move on its own. It moves because something is listening.*

I shut the book and sat with the cold of the morning still on me. The room had given me more than a mystery—it had given me a method. I had watched. I had noted. I had asked the question that mattered. And I had come away with the shape of the answer, even if the answer itself was still hidden.

That was enough for one day.
