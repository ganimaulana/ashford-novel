# Chapter 48 — The Pattern on the Map

I had been in Ashford sixty-seven days, and I had filled my notebook with the shapes of three cases: the room at the Greyhound, the letters on High Street, the clock on Mill Lane. I had recorded what I had seen, and I had recorded what I had not seen, and I had begun to see that the questions were not separate—they were part of a single question, written in a language I was only beginning to learn.

I spread the map of Ashford across my desk on the morning of the thirty-first of January, and I marked the three locations with ink: the inn, the house, the empty house. I drew lines between them, and I saw that the lines formed a shape—not a triangle, but something closer to a curve, a line that bent and straightened and bent again.

I did not know what the shape meant, but I knew that it meant something.

---

I went to Calloway's office that afternoon, because a man who has seen a shape on a map wants to know if the shape has been seen before.

He was at his desk, as always, and he looked at the map when I laid it before him. He did not speak for a long time.

"This is the pattern," I said. "The room at the Greyhound. The letters on High Street. The clock on Mill Lane. They are not random. They lie along a line."

Calloway looked at the line, and then he looked at me. "That is not a line on the map," he said. "That is a boundary. The old parish boundary, from before the turn of the century. It was marked by stones, and the stones were moved, and the boundary was lost."

"Lost?"

"Not lost," he said. "Forgotten. There is a difference. A thing that is lost can be found. A thing that is forgotten must be remembered."

He took a key from his desk and unlocked a drawer, and he drew out a folder of papers that were yellowed and brittle. He opened it and spread the papers before me.

"This is the record of the old boundary," he said. "It was drawn in the year 1690, and it was confirmed in 1720, and it was changed in 1785. The stones were moved, and the line was redrawn, and the new line was the one on the map you have. But the old line—the one you have drawn—is still there. It is not on the map, but it is in the ground."

He looked at me, and I saw that he was not afraid. He was waiting.

"Three cases," I said. "Three anomalies. And all three lie along a line that is not on the map."

Calloway nodded, and he said, "I have known about the line for many years, Mr. Ashcroft. I have known that the town is built on something older than the town. I have known that the stones are not just stones. But I have not known how to read them. And now you have shown me that someone else has been reading them—or has been using them."

I took the map and the papers and went home.

---

That night I wrote in my book:

*31 Jan. The three cases lie along a line that was once a parish boundary. The boundary was changed in 1785, but the old line remains in the ground. The anomalies occur along the old line, not the new one. I do not know why, but I know that the line is the key. I will find the stones, and I will find the line, and I will find what the line is keeping.*

I shut the book, and the lamp came at nine, and was carried away along the rooms, and no sound came. I let it stand.
