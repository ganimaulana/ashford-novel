CHAPTER 48 — THE FIRST DUTY

We buried him on the third day.

Not because the town demanded it.

The town had already moved past his death by the second morning.

A constable is remembered in proportion to how recently he stopped someone's cart.

Vale had stopped no carts in years.

Still, I gave him what the town would give any man.

The burying ground behind St. Bartholomew's.

A grave beside the hedge.

Rain the night before, and the ground soft underfoot.

Calloway came.

He stood at the back, hat in hand, and said nothing through the whole service.

I had never seen him uncertain before.

Perhaps he was not uncertain.

Perhaps he was the only man in Ashford who remembered what Vale had been.

Afterward, the small crowd dispersed.

They had bread to buy.

Work.

Gossip.

The ordinary machinery of a town that had buried one of its own without noticing.

I remained.

I looked at the fresh earth.

I thought of the fifty years.

No stone was raised.

No one had ordered one.

I would see to it later.

Or perhaps I would not.

I did not know then that keeping a man's memory was part of what I had inherited.

---

The agreement was in my room.

I had kept it in the drawer of my desk, beneath the papers of the parish account, where anyone opening the drawer would see the account first and the agreement not at all.

I read it again that evening.

The words had not changed.

They were the same words Vale had shown me, in the same careful hand.

Somewhere between the first line and the last, I hoped for a sentence I had missed.

There was none.

The document told me what the door was for.

It did not tell me what lay behind it.

It told me who had signed it.

It did not tell me why.

I folded it and put it back.

The gear was in my coat.

It had not warmed in the hours since I had taken it from the stone.

It would not warm.

It had not warmed for Vale in fifty years, and it would not warm for me.

I took it out.

The mark was still there.

A circle.

A short line through it.

The same mark as the stones.

The same mark as the door.

I had stopped being surprised by the sameness of things.

I had begun instead to wonder whether the mark meant the same thing on all of them.

That was the question I could not answer.

And I had begun to suspect that the answer was the difference between a Watchman and a Keeper.

---

On the fifth day I walked to the end of Bellweather Lane.

The house was quiet.

It had been quiet since the morning Vale died, but this quiet was different.

This was the quiet of a place no longer being lived in.

I let myself in.

The door was unlocked.

Vale had never locked it.

Perhaps he had never seen a reason.

The chair was where it had been.

The cold fireplace.

The blanket, folded now on the seat, as I had left it when I had pulled it from his knees.

I had closed the window that morning.

I had done it myself, standing at the sill, watching the leaves of the rosemary move in the air.

The window was open now.

I stood in the middle of the room and looked at it.

The sash raised two inches.

Exactly two inches.

I had closed it.

I knew I had closed it.

I had stood there and pulled it down and watched the latch seat.

No one had reason to open it.

Vale had died in the chair.

No one had entered since.

Except me.

And Crowe.

Crowe had come that evening.

He had walked past the chair.

He had stood at the cold fireplace.

He had looked at the empty room and asked whether I understood.

He had not touched the window.

I did not think he had touched the window.

But I had not watched him every moment.

I had been watching the empty chair.

I closed the window.

I latched it.

Then I stood for a long time with my hand on the sill, trying to decide whether I was being careful or foolish.

I could not decide.

I went through the house slowly.

I told myself I was looking for Vale's papers.

I did not know what I was looking for.

The rooms were spare.

A bed.

A press.

A few coats that smelled of rain and old tobacco.

In the kitchen, the bread he had not finished, hard now.

I did not take anything.

I did not know what belonged to the keeper and what belonged to the man.

Perhaps there was no difference.

Perhaps that was the trouble.

---

I returned to the mill before dark.

The stones were where they had been.

I checked the first.

Then the second.

Then the third.

The cracks were the cracks.

No wider.

No narrower.

Then I checked the fourth.

The fourth had been sound.

I had knelt beside it on the night of the vigil and seen nothing.

I knelt again.

There was a line in the stone.

Thin.

Almost invisible in the failing light.

I put my finger to it.

It ran the width of the stone, faint as a hair.

I did not know whether it had been there before.

I had looked.

I had been careful.

But the stone was old, and the light had been poor, and a man can look at a thing and not see it, and call himself careful.

I took out my notebook.

I wrote:

Fourth stone. Hairline mark.

I looked at the sentence.

Then I added:

Origin unknown. Time of appearance unknown.

I closed the book.

I walked on to the mill.

The door was closed.

I had left it closed.

I knelt beside the mechanism.

The depression was empty.

I looked at the ground around it.

The ground was soft there.

Rain had fallen.

The soft ground held marks.

There were my own marks from the night of the vigil.

And there was another.

A heel.

Not mine.

My boots leave a wider print.

This was narrower.

Newer.

I crouched and looked at it.

It faced away from the door.

Whoever had stood here had stood looking at the door, and then turned, and walked back toward the town.

I did not know when.

I did not know who.

I knew only that while I had been standing at a grave, or folding a blanket, or closing a window, someone else had stood where a keeper stands.

And they had not been a keeper.

They had not carried the gear.

The door had not moved.

But they had come to look at it.

That was something.

That was not nothing.

I wrote it down.

I did not write it in the town's book.

I wrote it in my own.

And then I stood there in the fading light and tried to understand the difference between the two.

A Watchman writes in the town's book.

His account is owed to the town.

He sees a thing, and he notes it, and the noting is the end of his part.

The town sleeps easier because someone has written that the door is closed.

But a Keeper—

I stopped.

I did not know what a Keeper owed.

No book had been given to me.

No one had said, write it here.

Vale had written nothing.

In fifty years he had left no account.

He had left only the document, and the gear, and the silence.

Perhaps the account was not meant to be written.

Perhaps it was meant only to be kept.

I turned away from the mill.

I walked back toward town as the light failed.

The lamps were being lit.

The lamplighter moved along the road with his long pole, and the windows took light one by one.

I passed the end of the common.

I stopped.

There, at the edge of the common, where the grass met the lower pasture—

the house.

It was there.

I had seen it twice before.

Once from a distance, when it had faded before I could reach it.

Once in the spring, when I had gone inside and found the clock stopped at 11:17 and heard the knocking from beneath the floor.

I stopped now.

It was not fading.

It stood in the twilight, dark against the grey.

The windows were dark.

The front door was open.

A Watchman would have noted it.

He would have written the time, the weather, the position.

He would have walked to the edge of the common to confirm what he saw.

And then he would have reported it, and the report would have gone into the town's book, and the town would have decided what it meant—which is to say, the town would have decided that it meant nothing.

I did not walk to the edge of the common.

I did not approach.

I stood at the road and looked at the house for a long time.

The door was open.

I had gone in, the spring before, and the house had been empty except for the clock.

The clock had stopped at 11:17.

I had heard three knocks from beneath the floor.

I had not learned what they were.

I did not know whether I would learn by going in again.

I could have gone to the constable.

I could have gone to Crowe.

I could have written the house's appearance into the town's book, and let the town decide it meant nothing.

I did none of those things.

I went home.

I sat at my desk.

I opened my notebook.

I wrote:

The house appeared at the edge of the common. Door open. I did not enter.

Then I wrote:

I have decided not to tell anyone about the stones. Or the door. Or the house.

I looked at the sentence.

It was a small sentence.

It did not look like a decision.

But it was the first decision I had made as keeper.

A Watchman reports.

A Keeper—

I do not yet know what a Keeper does.

But I know what a Keeper does not do.

A Keeper does not let the town decide that a door means nothing.

A Keeper does not let the Order decide when it opens.

I closed the book.

I put it with the agreement, in the drawer, beneath the parish account.

Then I took the gear from my coat and placed it on the desk where I could see it.

I did not know what the mark meant.

I knew only that it would not be put into that door by accident.

Whatever was behind the door would wait.

And whoever had stood at the mechanism in the soft ground would come again.

When he did, he would find the door closed.

He would find the keeper.

He would find me.
