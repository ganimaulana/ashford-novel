CHAPTER 48 — THE FIRST DUTY

We buried him on the third day.

Not because the town demanded it. The town had already moved past his death by the second morning. A constable is remembered in proportion to how recently he stopped someone's cart, and Vale had stopped no carts in years. He had become, in the town's memory, an old man who kept to himself at the end of a quiet lane, and the town had found other things to occupy it.

Still, I gave him what the town would give any man. The burying ground behind St. Bartholomew's. A grave beside the hedge, where the yew trees kept their dark shade and the grass grew long in the summer. Rain the night before, and the ground soft underfoot, so that the sexton's boots left deep prints beside the open grave.

Calloway came.

He stood at the back, hat in hand, and said nothing through the whole service. I had never seen him uncertain before. He was a man who moved through Ashford with the settled confidence of one who had read every book in the vestry and found most of them wanting. He stood now at the edge of the burying ground, in his dark coat, and he did not approach the grave, and he did not leave.

Perhaps he was not uncertain. Perhaps he was the only man in Ashford who remembered what Vale had been — a constable, once, before Calloway himself. Perhaps he was remembering the same thing I was remembering: that Vale had spent fifty years guarding something he could not fully explain, and that whatever he had carried with him could no longer be asked.

Afterward, the small crowd dispersed. They had bread to buy. Work. Gossip. The ordinary machinery of a town that had buried one of its own without noticing. They filed out through the lych-gate, past the sexton, past the wet stones of the path, and the burying ground emptied, and I remained.

I looked at the fresh earth. The sexton had not yet finished levelling it. The mound was raw, darker than the grass around it, and the rain had begun again — a thin, steady rain that darkened the earth and flattened the few flowers someone had laid.

I thought of the fifty years.

No stone was raised. No one had ordered one. The town did not raise stones for men who had stopped no carts in years.

I would see to it later. Or perhaps I would not. I could not yet tell then that keeping a man's memory was part of what I had inherited.

---

The agreement was in my room.

I had kept it in the drawer of my desk, beneath the papers of the parish account, where anyone opening the drawer would see the account first and the agreement not at all. It was not a large drawer. It was the drawer where I kept the things I did not wish to look at often — the things I kept because they were mine and I had not decided what else to do with them.

I read it again that evening.

I lit the lamp and drew the curtain and sat at the desk, and I took out the agreement, and I unfolded it, and I read it.

The words had not changed. They were the same words Vale had shown me, in the same careful hand, on the same yellowed paper. I had read them in the spring, when Vale had first shown me the document. I had read them again after his death. I read them now.

Somewhere between the first line and the last, I hoped for a sentence I had missed — a clause, a note, a marginal mark that would explain what I was supposed to do. There was none.

The document told me what the door was for. It told me that the door beneath the mill was to be kept closed, and that the keeping was the keeper's duty, and that the agreement bound the keeper to that keeping for as long as the boundary stood.

It did not tell me what lay behind it.

It told me who had signed it. Aurelius Ashcroft. The name at the foot of the document, in ink that had faded to brown. The name I had seen in the parish records, in the margins of books that no one else opened.

It did not tell me why.

I folded it and put it back. I slid the drawer closed, and I sat in the lamplight, and I thought about the gap between what the document said and what it did not say.

The gear was in my coat.

I had carried it since the morning of Vale's death. I had taken it from the stone beside the door beneath the mill, where he had left it — or where it had lain — and I had put it in my coat, and I had not taken it out except to look at it.

It had not warmed in the hours since I had taken it from the stone. It had not warmed in the days since. It would not warm. It had not warmed for Vale in fifty years, and it would not warm for me.

I took it out.

It was a thing of iron, dark and heavy, its shape worn by use I had never seen. A circle, and a short line through it — the mark that was on the stones, and on the door, and on the gear.

The same mark.

I had stopped being surprised by the sameness of things. I had begun instead to wonder whether the mark meant the same thing on all of them — whether the circle and the line on the gear was the same circle and line as on the door, or whether the sameness was a trick of my own seeing.

That was the question I could not answer.

And I had begun to suspect that the answer was the difference between a Watchman and a Keeper.

A Watchman notices things. A Watchman records what he sees. A Watchman reports, and the reporting is the end of his part.

A Keeper — I did not yet know what a Keeper did. I had been keeper for three days. I had buried my predecessor. I had read the agreement. I had carried the gear. I had not yet kept anything.

---

On the fifth day I walked to the end of Bellweather Lane.

The house was quiet. It had been quiet since the morning Vale died, but this quiet was different. This was the quiet of a place no longer being lived in — the quiet that follows the removal of a person from a room, when the room does not know yet whether to resume its ordinary life or to stand still.

I let myself in.

The door was unlocked. Vale had never locked it. Perhaps he had never seen a reason. The lane was quiet, and the house held nothing worth taking, and the lock, like so much else, had seemed unnecessary.

The chair was where it had been. The cold fireplace. The blanket, folded now on the seat, as I had left it when I had pulled it from his knees. The room was as I had left it, and I had not been back since the morning of his death.

I had closed the window that morning. I had done it myself, standing at the sill, watching the leaves of the rosemary move in the air, and I had closed it, and I had latched it, and I had left.

The window was open now.

I stood in the middle of the room and looked at it.

The sash raised two inches. Exactly two inches — not the few inches of a hand pushed carelessly, but a precise distance, as if someone had raised it deliberately and left it so.

I had closed it. I knew I had closed it. I had stood there and pulled it down and watched the latch seat. I had not imagined it.

No one had reason to open it. Vale had died in the chair. No one had entered since. Except me. And Crowe.

Crowe had come that evening. He had walked past the chair. He had stood at the cold fireplace. He had looked at the empty room and asked whether I understood what I had taken on. He had not touched the window.

I did not think he had touched the window. But I had not watched him every moment. I had been watching the empty chair, and the folded blanket, and the place where Vale had sat, and I had not watched Crowe's hands.

I closed the window. I latched it. Then I stood for a long time with my hand on the sill, trying to decide whether I was being careful or foolish. I could not decide.

I went through the house slowly. I told myself I was looking for Vale's papers — for anything he had left that might explain what I was supposed to keep. I did not know what I was looking for.

The rooms were spare. A bed. A press. A few coats that smelled of rain and old tobacco. In the kitchen, the bread he had not finished, hard now, and a cup with tea dried at the bottom.

I did not take anything. I did not know what belonged to the keeper and what belonged to the man. Perhaps there was no difference. Perhaps that was the trouble.

---

I returned to the mill before dark.

The stones were where they had been. I checked the first. Then the second. Then the third. The cracks were the cracks — no wider, no narrower than they had been on the night of the vigil. The boundary had not failed. It was still there, holding.

Then I checked the fourth.

The fourth had been sound. I had knelt beside it on the night of the vigil, and I had seen nothing — no mark, no crack, no disturbance. I had written "fourth stone, sound" in my notebook, and I had moved on.

I knelt again.

There was a line in the stone.

Thin. Almost invisible in the failing light. It ran the width of the stone, faint as a hair, so faint that I had to put my face close to the surface to be certain it was there.

I put my finger to it. It was smooth — not a crack, but a mark, cut into the surface, worn by years of weather until it was nearly invisible.

I did not know whether it had been there before. I had looked. I had been careful. But the stone was old, and the light had been poor, and a man can look at a thing and not see it, and call himself careful.

I took out my notebook. I wrote: Fourth stone. Hairline mark.

I looked at the sentence. Then I added: Origin unknown. Time of appearance unknown.

I closed the book. I walked on to the mill.

The door was closed. I had left it closed.

I knelt beside the mechanism. The depression was empty — the shape where the gear would fit, if the gear were meant to fit. I had never put the gear in it. I had held the gear over it, once, and I had felt the shape of the thing, and I had not put it in.

I looked at the ground around it. The ground was soft there. Rain had fallen, and the soft ground held marks. There were my own marks from the night of the vigil — the deep prints where I had knelt, the drag of my heel where I had turned.

And there was another.

A heel. Not mine. My boots leave a wider print. This was narrower, newer, pressed into the soft earth as if the one who made it had stood for a time, looking at the door, before turning away.

I crouched and looked at it. It faced away from the door. Whoever had stood here had stood looking at the door, and then turned, and walked back toward the town.

I did not know when. I did not know who.

I knew only that while I had been standing at a grave, or folding a blanket, or closing a window, someone else had stood where a keeper stands. And they had not been a keeper. They had not carried the gear. The door had not moved.

But they had come to look at it. That was something. That was not nothing.

I wrote it down. I did not write it in the town's book. I wrote it in my own.

And then I stood there in the fading light and tried to understand the difference between the two.

A Watchman writes in the town's book. His account is owed to the town. He sees a thing, and he notes it, and the noting is the end of his part. The town sleeps easier because someone has written that the door is closed.

But a Keeper —

I stopped. I did not know what a Keeper owed. No book had been given to me. No one had said, write it here. Vale had written nothing. In fifty years he had left no account. He had left only the document, and the gear, and the silence.

Perhaps the account was not meant to be written. Perhaps it was meant only to be kept.

I turned away from the mill. I walked back toward town as the light failed. The lamps were being lit. The lamplighter moved along the road with his long pole, and the windows took light one by one, and the town settled into its evening.

I passed the end of the common. I stopped.

There, at the edge of the common, where the grass met the lower pasture —

the house.

It was there.

I had seen it twice before. Once from a distance, when it had faded before I could reach it. Once in the spring, when I had gone inside and found the clock stopped at 11:17 and heard the knocking from beneath the floor.

It was there now, standing in the twilight, dark against the grey. The windows were dark. The front door was open.

A Watchman would have noted it. He would have written the time, the weather, the position. He would have walked to the edge of the common to confirm what he saw. And then he would have reported it, and the report would have gone into the town's book, and the town would have decided what it meant — which is to say, the town would have decided that it meant nothing.

I did not walk to the edge of the common. I did not approach.

I stood at the road and looked at the house for a long time.

The door was open. I had gone in, the spring before, and the house had been empty except for the clock. The clock had stopped at 11:17. I had heard three knocks from beneath the floor. I had not learned what they were.

I could have gone to the constable. I could have gone to Crowe. I could have written the house's appearance into the town's book, and let the town decide it meant nothing.

I did none of those things.

I went home.

I sat at my desk.

I opened my notebook.

I wrote: The house appeared at the edge of the common. Door open. I did not enter.

Then I wrote: I have decided not to tell anyone about the stones. Or the door. Or the house.

I looked at the sentence. It was a small sentence. It did not look like a decision. But it was the first decision I had made as keeper.

A Watchman reports. A Keeper —

I do not yet know what a Keeper does. But I know what a Keeper does not do.

A Keeper does not let the town decide that a door means nothing.

A Keeper does not let the Order decide when it opens.

I closed the book. I put it with the agreement, in the drawer, beneath the parish account.

Then I took the gear from my coat and placed it on the desk where I could see it.

I did not know what the mark meant. I knew only that it would not be put into that door by accident.

Whatever was behind the door would wait. And whoever had stood at the mechanism in the soft ground would come again.

When he did, he would find the door closed. He would find the keeper. He would find me.
