CHAPTER 88 — THE SEPARATION

I did not hear from the office for three days after the mill. I had expected a note, a summons, something that would tell me whether the failure at the mill had ended my part in the examination. But nothing came, and I found myself walking the lane each morning, watching the door, waiting for a note that did not arrive.

On Thursday morning a note came. It said only: The office. Nine o’clock. Bring nothing.

I put on my coat and went.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open, and the crowd was already there. There were fewer of them than before—perhaps twelve, perhaps thirteen. Some of the faces I remembered. Some I did not. The man with the notebook was there, standing apart from the others, his notebook closed but in his hand. The older man with the frayed cuffs was there, leaning against the wall by the pump. Peale was there, standing near the door, and he looked at me when I arrived and nodded once. Ellison was standing near the back, his hands in his pockets, his face unreadable. Merritt was standing alone by the gate, not looking at anyone.

The man I had seen at the market square was there. He was standing near the wall, his arms crossed, his head tilted slightly. He was not looking at anyone in particular. He was looking at the room as a whole, his gaze moving slowly from one face to another. I still did not know his name.

Calloway came out. He did not speak at first. He looked at the crowd the way he always looked at it, as though he had seen it before and knew he would see it again.

“You have completed the tasks,” he said. “Some of you succeeded. Some of you did not. The office does not care which. The office cares how you chose to look.”

He paused.

“The previous groupings are finished. You will now be assigned to new groups. The clerk will read the names.”

He stepped back. The clerk stepped forward, carrying the ledger. He opened it and began to read.

I listened. I heard names I recognized—Elton, Thorne, Archer, Finch, Salter. I heard names I did not recognize. I heard the man with the notebook’s name called with two men I did not know. I heard the older man’s name called with two men I had never seen before.

I heard Ellison’s name called with two men I did not know. He did not react. He simply stood still, his hands in his pockets, his face unreadable.

I heard Merritt’s name called with two men I did not know. He did not move. He did not look at anyone.

I heard Peale’s name called with two men I did not know. He nodded once, as though he had expected it.

Then the clerk said, “Leon Ashcroft, with the man from the market square and John Weaver.”

I did not know John Weaver. I looked for him in the crowd and saw a man about my age, with a plain coat and a quiet face, standing near the door. He was not looking at me. He was looking at the floor.

The man from the market square did not move. He did not react. He simply stood where he was, his arms crossed, his head still tilted.

The clerk closed the ledger. Calloway stepped forward again.

“You will go to your assigned locations,” he said. “Each group will be given a description of what to bring back. The description will be incomplete. Each member of the group will receive a different part of the description. You will have to share what you know to determine what you are looking for. You have until noon.”

He turned and went inside.

We stood in silence. Then the clerk began to hand out small slips of paper to each group. When he reached us, he handed one to me, one to the man from the market square, and one to John Weaver.

I opened mine. It said: *Dark, bound in leather, approximately this size.* There was a line drawn below the words, indicating a length of perhaps six inches.

I looked at John Weaver. He was reading his slip. He did not look at me.

The man from the market square did not open his slip. He held it in his hand, looking at me.

“We should compare what we have,” I said.

“We should,” he said. He opened his slip and read it aloud. “The binding is worn at the corners.”

Weaver looked up. “Mine says there is a mark on the spine, a line and a circle.”

I looked at my slip again. “Dark leather, about six inches.”

“That is three pieces of information,” the man from the market square said. “They do not tell us what it is, only what it looks like.”

“We have to find something that matches all three,” I said.

“We have to agree on what that is.”

Weaver looked at us. “Do you know where we are supposed to go?”

The man from the market square shook his head. “The instruction did not say.”

Weaver looked at me. “Did yours say?”

“No,” I said. “Only the description.”

We stood in silence. I looked at the other groups. Some were already moving. The man with the notebook was walking, his group following him. Ellison was walking, his hands in his pockets, his group behind him. Peale was walking, his head down, his steps even.

“We need to find out where we are going,” Weaver said.

“We need to find the object first,” the man from the market square said. “The location will follow.”

“How?”

He did not answer. He looked at me. “You were at the mill. You saw the light. You did not say anything.”

I said nothing.

“You will have to decide whether to speak again,” he said. “This time, you will have to speak to people you do not know.”

He turned and walked toward the gate. I followed him. Weaver followed behind us.

We walked through the lanes, past the market, past the church, into the older part of town where the streets were narrower and the houses were closer together. The man from the market square walked without hesitation, as though he knew where he was going.

“Where are we going?” I said.

“I do not know,” he said. “But I know we are going in the right direction.”

“How?”

“Because the object we are looking for is not a book,” he said. “It is a box. I have seen a mark like that before, on a door near here.”

Weaver nodded. “I think I saw a box with that mark in a shop window once, but I’m not sure.”

I looked at the man from the market square. “So we are looking for a box with that mark?”

“We are,” he said. “I do not know exactly where it is, but we can start by looking for doors with that mark.”

We walked along the street. The houses were old, the doors dark. We passed several, stopping to look at each door, but found nothing. Then Weaver stopped.

“Here,” he said. He pointed to a door near the end of the street. There was a small carving near the hinge—a line and a circle, the same mark.

“That is it,” the man from the market square said. He knelt and examined it. “The mark is recent. Someone has been here recently.”

“The box is inside?” I said.

“Probably,” he said. “But we do not know which room.”

Weaver looked at the house. “The door is locked.”

The man from the market square stood up. He did not try the door. He looked at the windows, at the side of the house, at the roof.

“There is a window at the back,” he said. “We can try that.”

We walked around to the back of the house. The window was small, but it was open. I could see inside, a room with a table and a chair, and on the table, a small dark box.

“There,” I said.

Weaver looked at me. “We need to get in.”

The man from the market square did not move. He looked at the window, then at me. “You saw it first,” he said. “You decide how we proceed.”

I looked at the window. I looked at the box. I thought about the light on the stairs at the mill.

“We go in together,” I said. “We share what we find.”

He nodded, as though that was the answer he had expected. He lifted the window and climbed through. I followed. Weaver followed last.

The box was on the table. It was small, dark, bound with a leather cord. I picked it up. I untied the cord and opened the lid. Inside was a single piece of paper, folded. I unfolded it. It was blank.

I looked at the man from the market square. He looked at the paper, then at me.

“It is empty,” he said.

“It matches the description,” I said. “Dark leather, worn corners, the mark on the spine. That is what we were asked to find.”

“We do not know if it is the right one,” Weaver said.

“We will not know until we bring it back,” I said.

We walked back to the office. I carried the box. Weaver walked beside me. The man from the market square walked behind us, his steps even, his head tilted.

We placed the box on the clerk’s desk. He looked at it. He did not look surprised.

“You found it,” he said.

“We found it,” I said.

He wrote something in the ledger. He did not say anything else.

We walked out. The man from the market square stopped on the step.

“You spoke,” he said. “This time, you spoke.”

“I did,” I said.

“Why?”

I thought about it. “Because I did not know whether I would get another chance.”

He looked at me for a long time. Then he nodded, once, and walked away.

Weaver looked at me. “What did he mean?”

“I am not sure,” I said.

I walked home slowly. I thought about the man from the market square, about the box, about the task we had completed. I thought about Weaver, who had shared his information without hesitation. I thought about the light on the stairs, and how I had said nothing, and how that silence had cost us the document.

I thought about the new group. I did not know Weaver. I did not know the man from the market square. I did not know whether I could trust them, or whether they could trust me.

But I knew that the separation had happened. The people I had worked with—Ellison, Merritt, Peale—were no longer beside me. I did not know where they had been sent, or what they had found, or whether they had succeeded or failed.

I sat in the chair by the window and looked out at the lane. The sun was low, the shadows long. I thought about the task—how we had each held a piece of the description, and how we had to put them together. I did not know whether we had found the right object, or whether the office would consider it a success. I only knew that we had worked together, and that we had shared what we knew.

I did not know whether I would see Ellison again. I did not know whether Merritt would ever speak to me again. I did not know whether Peale would find what he was looking for.

But I knew that the group I had learned to read was gone. And that I would have to learn to read the people I was with now.

I sat in the chair until the light faded, and I did not move.

I waited.

John Weaver noticed the hesitation. "You don't trust him?" he asked.

"I don't know him," Leon said.

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Leon said. "It isn't."
