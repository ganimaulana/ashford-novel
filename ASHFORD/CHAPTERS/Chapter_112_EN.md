CHAPTER 112 — THE WALL OF STONES

I returned to the boundary the following day, and the day after, and the day after that.

I walked the line of stones from the eastern marker to the corner where the wall turned, and I looked at each stone, and I counted them, and I looked for the pattern — the pattern of the count, the pattern that the stones were marking.

I counted the stones. More than a hundred. I counted them again. The number was the same.

But the number was not the tally.

I knelt at the first stone. I looked at the first sign, cut into the base of the stone, hidden where the earth covered it. The first sign. The mark of the maker.

I moved to the second stone. The same sign. The same mark, cut into the base, hidden.

The third. The fourth. The fifth.

Every stone had the first sign at its base.

Every stone carried the mark of the maker.

I walked the length of the wall, and I looked at every stone, and every stone had the first sign at its base.

The tally was a number. The stones were a number. Every stone had the first sign.

But the number of stones was not the tally.

I stood at the end of the wall, and I did not know what the stones were counting.

---

I went to the vestry.

The clerk was there.

"The wall," I said. "The stones. Every stone has the first sign at its base."

"Yes."

"Every stone is marked."

"Yes."

I stood in the vestry.

"Then the stones are not the count," I said. "The stones are the counters. Each stone is a counter, and the tally is the record of what they count."

The clerk was silent.

"The tally is a number," he said. "The stones are a number. The number of stones is not the tally. So the stones are not counting themselves."

"Then what are they counting?"

The clerk was silent a long moment.

"I do not know," he said. "I have kept the books for forty years, and I have not known what the stones are counting."

I stood in the vestry.

The stones were counters. The tally was the record. And no one knew what they were counting.

---

I went back to the boundary.

I walked the line of stones again, and I did not count them. I looked at them — looked at the stones themselves, not the number of them.

The stones were old. They were worn by weather, by years, by the passing of hands that no longer remembered whose.

Every stone had the first sign at its base.

Every stone carried the mark of the maker.

I knelt at the fourth stone, where the hairline mark ran across its face.

The first sign at the base.

The hairline mark across the face.

Two marks on the same stone.

I looked at the hairline mark. It was a crack — or a mark, I did not know which. It ran across the stone, straight, too straight to be natural.

I had watched it appear, in the spring of the first year. It had appeared, and it had not grown, and I had not known what it meant.

I looked at the first sign at the base of the stone.

The first sign. The mark of the maker.

The hairline mark. The mark that had appeared, and had not grown, and had not been explained.

Two marks on the same stone.

I did not know what they meant.

But I knew that the stones were counting something.

And I knew that the marks on the stones were the language of the count.

I stood.

I walked home.

I sat at my desk.

I opened the red book. I wrote:

*Every stone has the first sign at its base. Every stone is a counter. The tally is the record of what they count.*

*I do not know what the stones are counting.*

*But I know that the stones are counting something.*

*I will learn what it is.*

*The hunt has led me to the wall.*

*And the wall is leading me to the count.*
