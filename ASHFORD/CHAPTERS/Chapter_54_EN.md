# Chapter 54 — The Key's Journey

I sat at my desk on the morning after I had written the two explanations, and I looked at the list of established facts. The one thing I had not yet examined was the key itself. It had been issued to the morning visitor. It had been on the hook by noon. The evening visitor had possessed it. Between those three points, there was a gap I had not filled. I had traced the promise, measured the room, counted the steps, weighed the boot prints. But I had not held the key in my hand and traced its possible path through the hours of that Tuesday.

I decided to go to the Greyhound, not to interview anyone, but to trace the key's possible path with my own feet and eyes. The innkeeper was in the bar, wiping the counter with a cloth that had seen better days. He looked up as I entered, and I asked him only to let me examine the hook and the door of Room 4. He gave me the key—the same key that had been on the hook that Tuesday—and I took it and went to the room.

The key was heavier than I had expected. It was a large iron key, with a wooden tag attached by a cord. The tag was worn smooth, the number '4' carved into the wood and darkened with age. The iron was dark and slightly rough, and the bit was cut with three steps. I turned it over in my hand, feeling its weight. It was the only key to the room, or so the innkeeper had said. He had not had a duplicate made, and the original was kept on the hook behind the bar.

I walked from the room to the bar, timing myself with my pocket watch. The route was short: down the corridor, past the common room, and to the hook behind the bar. The corridor was narrow, the floorboards worn smooth in the centre. The common room was empty at that hour, but the innkeeper could have been at the bar, or in the kitchen, or in the yard. The route took less than a minute at a normal pace. A man could move from the room to the hook without being seen if the innkeeper was occupied. I repeated the walk three times, varying my speed, and each time it was under a minute.

I returned to the room and examined the door. The lock was simple, a standard iron lock with a small brass plate. The key turned smoothly, and the bolt slid home with a solid click. There was no sign that the lock had been forced or tampered with. The key was the only means of entry. I looked at the hinges; they were iron, set into the frame with square nails. They showed no signs of recent removal. The window was fastened from inside, as I had already noted. The room was secure except through the door, and the door was secured by the key.

I went to the bar and looked at the hook. It was an iron peg, set into a wooden beam, about six feet from the floor. The key hung on it by the cord, and I reached up and took it down. The hook was visible from the bar, but the innkeeper was not always behind the bar. The maid passed through the kitchen, which was separate. The stableman worked in the yard. There were times when the bar was unattended—the innkeeper had admitted as much. A person could stand at the hook for a few seconds and take the key without being noticed, provided they knew when the innkeeper was away.

I asked the innkeeper how often he left the bar unattended. He thought for a moment. "Often enough," he said. "I go to the kitchen, or to the yard, or to talk to a guest. The bar is not a prison. If someone wanted to take the key, they could have done it."

I considered the possible sequences. I wrote them down in my notebook, one after the other, leaving space between each for my thoughts.

If there were two men, one possible explanation was that the morning visitor placed the key on the hook sometime before noon. He could have done so when he left, or he could have returned it later. The evening visitor had then taken the key from the hook and entered Room 4. That required only that the key was accessible, which it was. The morning visitor did not need to return to the inn after the initial rental. He could have walked away, and the key could have been on the hook for hours, waiting.

If there was one man, the same man must have placed the key on the hook at some point, and then later taken it from the hook to enter the room. That meant he had to return to the inn at least twice: once to place the key on the hook, and once to retrieve it. Or, he could have kept the key and not placed it on the hook at all—but then the key would not have been on the hook at noon. The evidence showed it was on the hook at noon, so he must have returned it at some point. That required an extra trip, an additional risk of being seen.

I traced the possible timeline. The morning visitor had left around ten, according to the innkeeper. The key was on the hook by noon, according to the maid. That gave a two-hour window during which the key could have been returned. If the morning visitor was the same man, he could have returned the key during that window, then later taken it again before six. But that required him to come back to the inn before noon, which might have been noticed—though not necessarily, as the inn was busy with travellers and the innkeeper was often away from the bar.

I walked the route again, this time from the front door to the hook. It took thirty seconds. From the back door to the hook, it took forty-five seconds. The inn was not large. A man could enter, place the key, and leave without being seen if he was quick and knew the patterns of the house.

If there were two men, the morning visitor could have returned the key at any time, and the evening visitor could have taken it later. That did not require the morning visitor to return to the inn. The key could have been on the hook for hours, and the evening visitor could have arrived at six, taken it, and entered the room. The innkeeper's expectation did the rest.

I sat in the common room and wrote the two scenarios in full, with their steps and assumptions.

**One man scenario:**
- Morning visitor rents Room 4, receives the key.
- He leaves the room, goes toward the front door, but his departure is not observed.
- At some point before noon, he returns the key to the hook behind the bar.
- He leaves again, or stays away, until the evening.
- Around six in the evening, he returns to the inn, takes the key from the hook, and enters Room 4.
- He stays overnight and leaves before light.
This scenario requires two separate returns to the inn after the initial rental: one to return the key, and one to retrieve it. Each return carries a risk of being seen, and neither was observed. The innkeeper's assumption that he had left in the morning is unsupported.

**Two men scenario:**
- Morning visitor rents Room 4, receives the key.
- He leaves the room, goes toward the front door, but his departure is not observed.
- He returns the key to the hook at some point before noon, or simply leaves it there when he leaves.
- A second man, who resembles the first in face and build, arrives in the evening, takes the key from the hook, and enters Room 4.
- He stays overnight and leaves before light.
This scenario requires only one return to the hook: the morning visitor's placement of the key, or the key being available. The evening visitor does not need to have been at the inn earlier. The key transfer occurs at the hook, and the innkeeper's expectation does the rest.

I looked at the two scenarios. The two-man scenario was simpler in terms of actions: it required one fewer return to the inn. The morning visitor did not need to return later to retrieve the key. He only needed to place it on the hook, or leave it there. The evening visitor could take it later without coordination. The one-man scenario required the same man to return twice, which was an additional complication.

I considered whether the morning visitor could have handed the key directly to the evening visitor. If he had done so, the key would not have been on the hook at noon, unless the evening visitor had returned it to the hook. That seemed unlikely, because the evening visitor would have needed to place the key on the hook and then later retrieve it—which would add even more steps. The simplest explanation for the key being on the hook at noon was that the morning visitor placed it there, either when he left or shortly thereafter. The evening visitor then took it later.

I walked to the hook and examined its position again. It was behind the bar, but the bar was not a closed counter. There was no gate or barrier; anyone could reach it. The key could have been taken by anyone who knew it was there. The innkeeper had said that during busy periods, the bar was often unattended. The maid had confirmed that she had not seen the key being returned. The stableman had not noticed anyone near the bar. The absence of witnesses did not prove that no one had taken the key; it only proved that no one had noticed.

I returned to the room and looked at the door again. The key was the only way in. If the evening visitor had the key, he must have obtained it from the hook or directly from the morning visitor. The hook explanation was simpler because it did not require a meeting between the two men. The morning visitor could have placed the key on the hook and walked away, and the evening visitor could have taken it later without ever meeting him. That arrangement was possible, but it was also possible that the two men had met and exchanged the key directly. The evidence did not distinguish between those possibilities.

I wrote in my notebook:

*The key-custody evidence does not resolve the identity question. Both hypotheses are possible. However, under the current reconstruction, the two-person hypothesis requires fewer unexplained actions: the morning visitor returns the key to the hook, and the evening visitor takes it, whereas the one-person hypothesis requires the same man to return the key and later retrieve it, adding an extra step. This comparison of explanatory cost slightly favors the two-person reconstruction, but it does not prove that two people were involved. The key could have been taken by anyone who knew where it was, and the innkeeper's absence from the bar created an opportunity that could have been exploited by either explanation.*

I looked at the entry. It was not a conclusion, but it was a narrowing. The one-person hypothesis now required an additional assumption: that the morning visitor had returned to the inn to retrieve the key. The two-person hypothesis required no such return. That difference was real, and it tilted the weight of the evidence, however slightly, toward the two-person explanation.

I shut the notebook. I had not solved the case, but I had identified a difference in explanatory cost. That was a concrete advancement—not a proof, but a step toward a more precise understanding.

I walked home through the cold, and I thought about the key. It had been on the hook at noon, and the evening visitor had it at six. The missing interval was not the key's journey; it was the absence of anyone who could explain it. The key could have been taken by anyone, at any time, and the innkeeper's expectation had filled the gap. That was the shape of the opportunity: a key, a hook, a moment of inattention, and a man who acted as though he belonged.

I did not know who had placed the key on the hook. I did not know who had taken it. But I knew that the two-person reconstruction was simpler in this one respect, as a comparison of explanatory cost. That was not an answer, but it was a direction—a path to follow if more evidence ever appeared.

That evening, I wrote in my book:

*The key's chain of custody is incomplete. Under the current reconstruction, the one-person hypothesis requires an additional return visit to retrieve the key, while the two-person hypothesis does not. This comparison of explanatory cost tilts slightly toward the two-person reconstruction, but it does not identify who actually returned or took the key. I need a witness who saw the key being returned, or a record of the evening visitor's arrival. Until then, both remain possible. The key itself tells me nothing about the men who held it—only that it was available, and that someone took advantage of that availability.*

I closed the book and let the night settle. The case was not solved. But I had moved from uncertainty to a more precise uncertainty. That was enough for one day.
