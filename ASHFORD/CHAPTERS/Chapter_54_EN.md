CHAPTER 54 — THE OFFICE'S BOOK

I counted the eight again on Wednesday morning, because counting was the part of the work that asked nothing of me.

There were eight of them, tied as they had lain in the box. Eleven had gone out of this room on Monday, in Calloway's hands. Nineteen had come home in the box the first night in October. That was the arithmetic of the week, and it held, and holding it was what the morning was for. I went through the count three times, the way a man sweeps a floor that is already clean, because sweeping is the part of housekeeping that does not ask you to feel anything.

The rest of the box sat where I had left it the night before: the letters, the journal, the map, in the order he had buried them in. The bottles, the button, the ring, the cloth, at the very bottom, where the things that were only his were kept. On the lid, the three bundles, with the slips I had written for them. And on top of everything, the paper I could not yet name, lying there because the method says that a thing that does not fit is kept where you will see it rather than where you will lose it. He had separated his own papers before he died, and I had separated them after, and between the two separations there had been a funeral and a visit and a week of my life I did not care to examine. The arrangement survived all of it. That was the arrangement's whole business: to survive it.

I put the kettle on, because there was a bowl promised.

The Hartley girl came before ten with it wrapped in a cloth, washed and dried and turned upside down to drain, the way his house had been kept by the lane in turns for years without my knowing. She set it on the dresser as though it had always lived there, and she asked after nothing at all. That was the kindness. Then she went, and I stood in my own cold kitchen with two cups and one man's habit of pouring them both, and I poured the second out of habit and returned it to the pot without anger at the habit, and I drank the one cup standing, slowly, so that it took time.

I saw the office walk up the lane at twenty past ten, and I knew it was alone the way I knew the weather, because I had watched the end of the lane each morning since Monday. Nobody stood at it. Nobody stood anywhere near it. The absence was itself a thing I wrote down in my head, because noticing the lane empty of him was as much a record as noticing him in it, and I had been taught that what is seen is the whole of what you can trust until it is put next to something else.

Calloway came in without waiting to be asked twice.

His coat was buttoned wrong at the throat, and there was the look on his face of a man who has sat in lamplight since before dark the night before and gotten up into it again at dawn. Nothing about him was different from the two visits I had received, except that the office had obviously kept no hours this week, and that the keeping of hours had always been the one thing the office could be relied upon to do.

"I have not brought the book," he said, by way of standing in my room. "I have brought a leaf of it, copied out, in the clerk's hand. I thought you would want to know which hand you were reading."

"I want to know what I am reading," I said.

"That is in order," he said, and he drew out a single page, folded once, and laid it at the end of the table without haste. "You said to me on Saturday, in this room, that if the office named the papers, you could tell me whether you had seen them. The office thought about your sentence all weekend, which is the wrong thing for an office to do with a thing a man has said to it, because the office then has to act on it. This week the office acted on it. So this morning the office names them. Everything on this leaf is named."

I looked at the page. The hand was steady, and small, and utterly unfamiliar. It was not Vale's hand. It was not Calloway's. Before I looked at anything else I had to take in the fact of that third hand lying there in my room, because everything since Sunday had been Vale's handwriting or the office's mouth, and a third hand was a third thing that had been in those rooms for twenty years without a name, and a thing that is in a room for twenty years without a name is worth a look.

"The office keeps an entry-book of what Vale's desk sent out," Calloway said. "Every paper, its date, its subject, and, in the margin, the mark of its return. The clerk set the mark when the paper came back and was stood on the shelf. One hand makes all of the marks in all the years, because it is one clerk's hand through two constables since I sat there. The mark means the shelf has it. That is all the mark has ever meant, and the office has had no reason to ask it to mean anything else. Until this week."

He read the first two lines aloud, to show me he meant the word named. A theft at the ironmonger's, June 1803. A death at the poor-house, September 1804, with the date of its return and the mark.

"I have seen those," I said. They were among the eleven. "They are already in your hands."

"Yes," he said. "Eleven of them are. The marks matched the shelf, and the shelf matched the names, and Monday's papers stood at their lines and I slept that night like a man who has found his house locked properly. Then the book was asked to do what a book does when a man with it has a suspicion. It was asked whether it held everything it swore to. And there is the answer, and I have copied it out myself, and there it is beneath the last true line, in my room, on your table, the way I asked the clerk for it before I walked out here. Look at the foot of the leaf."

I moved my eyes to the foot of the leaf. There was a block of writing there, cut off from the lines above, and I read it once before the shape of it came home to me.

Nine lines. Eight of the nine had dates, and the dates had subjects, and I knew every date as I knew the room, because they were written on the backs of eight tied papers on the lid of the box. *Second copy. Returned.* Eight marks of his own, eight receipts of his own, eight lines here in the office's book in the clerk's steady hand, each with a date that agreed with his. And beside every one of the eight, in the office's margin, the small even word that meant: *received, stood on the shelf, kept.*

"The eight you left with me," I said.

"The eight that the office's book says the office has," Calloway said. "Since the day they came back. Marked, on that day, by the only hand that keeps the marks. Received. Kept. And there is no shelf that holds them now, because I have put every shelf in the office under my hands since Monday. The book promised, years ago, that the office held those eight papers, and the office believed the promise the way a man believes his own doors. There is nothing in the office that knows when the papers stopped being behind them. And the book will say no more about who made the marks than that it was the office's ordinary clerk, and the office's ordinary clerk has held them through two constables and forty years."

I looked at the ninth line then, at the bottom, and I read it with my hand on the table, so the paper would not prove me.

The ninth line was of this year. In late February, in the same steady hand. *Inquiry made at the house in the lane concerning office papers. Account of the same transmitted to the office.* And in the margin, where the receipt stood against the other eight, nothing. No word. No mark. Just the small, level blank the clerk leaves when a paper has not come.

"There was a man at his door in February," Calloway said. "You stood in this room when I told you that on a Saturday. I told you what he had said to me on his own lips: a man came and asked questions in the right order, and Vale gave him nothing. There it was in the entry-book, on the same day. The book says Vale sat down and wrote out an account of the visit and sent it to the office, as was his way in everything, and the office's shelf answered to the words: received, kept, since Monday. It never came. The book has no mark. It was never in the office. Vale wrote it as though the office would have it, but it was not marked, and the account of that visit — I have been at my own desk since Monday and I say it to you in your room — the account is out of the office's hands because it had never been in its charge."

I looked up from the leaf.

"Or," I said.

"Or," Calloway said, "it came, and it stood somewhere, and someone put a hand on it, and no one wrote the mark, because it was the sort of paper a man takes who has reasons for what he has taken. A mark is made when a paper comes and stays. No mark is made when a paper comes and does not stay. Those are the two ways an office can lose a paper, and any book that has stood forty years has both of them in it somewhere, and the office never expected the two of them to be standing in the same week, in one man's name, in the book that meant the most. Eight in the office's hands, gone from the shelf. One in the road between his desk and mine, this February, gone out of the road before it ever came. The books cannot tell me which way the ninth went, and I did not walk up your lane to guess. The office named the papers because you asked it to on my step, and the office's answer is the leaf, and the leaf is the answer, and the rest is the office's place to be standing in."

I heard him out. I turned the leaf in my mind as I heard it, because he had given me the shape of something, and the shape was not the fact, and in that week the difference between a shape and a fact was the only thing I owned.

The kettle had gone cold while he spoke. I did not pour it out. I let it stand, because the standing was itself a kind of record.

"Which hand writes the mark?" I said.

"The clerk's," Calloway said. "He has written every mark since before I sat at the desk. Two constables, forty years. One hand. One key is mine. One is his. Those are the only keys. No third hand goes in that room without one of us walking it in or locking it behind it."

"And the shelf."

"The shelf is in the room. The room is locked. The lock is the desk's own lock. The clerk has his key and I have mine and there is no third key in Alderwick that opens it, and if there were I would have found it by now. The shelf is bare. The book is not. That is what I have to tell you, and it is the whole of what the office can tell any man, because the office's own telling of it is that it does not know the telling."

I looked at the leaf again. The ninth line stared back at me, its margin empty. A paper that had been sent and never received, or received and never marked, or marked and never shelved. The book could not tell me which, and the book was the only record.

"You have asked me once already," Calloway said. "On Saturday. You asked whether the office named its papers, and the office did not. The office named them this morning. The office has named them in your room. Now the office asks its own question. The eight papers with the marks are the office's papers. The book says they were sent out, came back, and were received. The book says they belong to the shelf. I am not disputing that you have them. I am saying the book says one thing and the shelf says another. May I see them?"

He said it without raising his voice. He said it the way a man reads a road sign — without enthusiasm, because the sign is not there to be liked.

I thought about the eight tied papers on the lid. I thought about the marks on their backs. I thought about the shelf in the office that stood bare of them, and the book in the office that said the shelf held them, and the clerk's hand that had made the marks, and the two keys that opened the room. I thought about the February line with its blank margin, and the road between the desk and the room, and the man who had put questions in the right order at the door in February, and the account that Vale had written and sent, and the account that was not on the shelf, and the account that was not in the box, and the account that had no mark.

I stood up. I took the eight tied papers from the lid of the box and I set them on the table between us, and I did not hand them to him. I set them down the way a man sets down a thing he is not yet sure he wants to keep, and I stood behind my chair.

"I am not disputing the office's claim to them," I said. "I am saying the book and the shelf do not agree. Until I know why, the papers stay here."

Calloway looked at the papers. He did not reach for them. He looked at the marks on their backs, and then at the leaf, and then at the marks in the margin of the leaf, and then at me. The heel of his hand came up to his jaw and stayed there, and I watched him decide.

"May I look at them?" he said.

"Yes."

"May I make a record?"

"Yes."

He took a clean sheet from his pocket — a ruled page from the office's own book — and he laid it beside the leaf. With his own pencil he wrote the eight dates, one by one, beside each the subject, and beside each the mark as it appeared on the back of the paper. He wrote slowly, the way a man writes when he is not sure the paper will be there when he is done. When he finished, he set the pencil down and folded the ruled page and put it in his coat, and the leaf he left on the table.

"The office's copy," he said. "The clerk's hand, my hand, your table. The book has the eight lines and the nine lines. The office now has a record of what it does not have. That is what the office can do, and the only thing it can do. The book cannot make the shelf hold what it does not. The book cannot make a mark where no mark was made. The book can only say what is true, and what is true is that the nine lines are the office's lines and the nine papers are not where the book says they are, and to hold the nine is to hold the office's own not-knowing."

He looked at me.

"But you will not give them to me."

"Not yet."

"You understand what you are doing."

"I am trying to."

He nodded.

"The office will come again."

"Then come again."

He stood. He buttoned his coat.

"The man that came in February," he said, at the door, without turning. "The office did not send him."

"I know," I said.

"You knew from the book," he said. "I knew from the man. We have both known since Monday. The question is who else knew, and what he wanted, and what he took, and the book does not answer that, and the office cannot. The office's answer to that question is your room, and the eight papers, and the man who has them. The office does not ask you to answer it. The office asks you to keep the papers safe while the office looks for the answer. That is the arrangement."

He went out. The lane held him. I shut the door.

---

I sat down.

The kettle was cold. The Hartley girl's bowl was where she had left it. The leaf lay on the table. The eight papers lay beside it. The box stood in the press by the window, and on its lid the three bundles I had made the night before, and the uncertain paper that had no name.

I took out my notebook. I wrote what I had seen, in order. What I had been told, in order. What I had concluded, in order. I kept them apart, the way he had taught me, because keeping them apart was the only part of the method that was mine.

What I saw: a leaf with nine lines. Eight marked. One blank. The clerk's hand. The shelf bare. Two keys. One man's hands. One man's road. February.

What I was told: the office had looked. The shelf was bare. The clerk was the same. The keys were two. The February account was not marked. The man in February had asked questions in the right order. The office had not sent him.

What I concluded: nothing yet. The conclusion would come when the book answered its own question, and the book would not answer it on a Wednesday.

I closed the notebook.

I carried the box to the table and I opened it, and I re-sorted what was inside it, because the morning's events had made a new category out of an old one, and the old categories would not hold the new fact.

The office's papers: the eleven Calloway had taken on Monday. They were at the office. They were the office's, and the office had proved itself, and they were where they belonged. That pile was empty.

His papers: the letters, the journal, the map, the bottles, the button, the ring, the cloth. They were where they had been. They were his. They were not the office's. They would stay where they were.

And the third pile: the eight marked papers, and the leaf with the nine lines, and my own copy of the ninth line that I had made in the ledger while Calloway watched. Those were the office's papers, held here because the office's own book and shelf could not agree on where they were. They were not his papers, because they were not the things he had written for himself. They were the papers that belonged to the road between his desk and the office's shelf, and the road did not own them, and the shelf did not own them, and the desk did not own them. They were the papers of a question that had not been answered, and a question is not a paper, and a paper that is a question is not a thing any man can sort into a pile that already had a name.

I set the third pile on top of the box, and I set the box back in the press, and I closed the press, and the room was the room.

The Hartley girl's bowl was still on the dresser. I washed it and dried it and carried it up the lane to her door, because it was not mine to keep, and she had brought it in good faith, and keeping good faith was the one thing the week had not touched. She took it without saying anything, and I walked back down the lane, and the lane was the lane, and the house at the end of it was shut, and the chair was at the window, and the press was in the room, and the box was in the press, and the papers were in the box, and the question was in the papers, and the answer was not in any of them.

The office kept a book. The book kept a mark. The mark kept a promise. The promise had not been kept. The book said the eight papers were in the office's hands. The shelf said they were not. The hands that held them were somewhere else. The somewhere else was this room. The office knew that. The book knew that. The man who had the keys knew that. The man who had the papers knew that. The man who had asked the questions in February knew that. The man who had taken the account knew that the account was not there. The knowing of it was the not-knowing of it. The not-knowing was the paper that was not there.

I lit the fire. I sat in the chair. I did not open the notebook again. The kettle was cold. The bowl was gone. The box was shut. The book was on the table. The marks were on the papers. The paper that was not there was not there. The office had its leaf. I had its copy. The lane was quiet. The week was not over. For now.
