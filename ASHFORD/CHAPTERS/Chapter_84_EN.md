CHAPTER 84 — THE OTHER SIDE

I did not hear from the office for three days after we returned the box. I had expected something—a word, a summons, a sign that the examination was continuing. But nothing came, and I found myself walking the lane each morning, watching the door, waiting for a note that did not arrive.

On Thursday morning a note came. It said only: The office. Nine o’clock. Bring nothing.

I put on my coat and went.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open, and the crowd was already there. There were fewer of them than before—perhaps twenty, perhaps fewer. Some of the faces I remembered. Some I did not. The man with the notebook was there, standing apart from the others, his notebook closed but in his hand. The older man with the frayed cuffs was there, leaning against the wall by the pump. Peale was there, standing near the door, and he looked at me when I arrived and nodded once.

I nodded back.

I looked for Ellison and Merritt. Ellison was standing near the back, his hands in his pockets, his face unreadable. Merritt was standing alone by the gate, not looking at anyone.

I walked over to Ellison.

“You are here,” I said.

“I am here,” he said. “I did not think we would be called again so soon.”

“Do you know what we are to do?”

“No,” he said. “But I know who does.”

He pointed to Calloway, who was standing on the step, looking at the crowd the way he always looked at it.

Calloway spoke.

“You have completed the first tasks,” he said. “Some of you found what you were looking for. Some of you did not. The office does not care which. The office cares how you chose to look.”

He paused.

“The next task is a single objective. There is a book in the old mill, a small one, bound in dark leather, with writing in it. You will retrieve it and bring it to the office. You have until noon. That is all.”

He turned and went inside. The clerk did not come forward. There were no papers, no instructions, no division into groups.

We stood in silence.

“The old mill,” Ellison said. “We were there before.”

“We were,” I said.

“We know where it is.”

“We do.”

“Then why are we waiting?”

I looked at the other candidates. Some were already moving. The man with the notebook was walking, his group following him. The older man with the frayed cuffs was walking, his hands in his pockets. Peale was walking, his head down, his steps even.

“We should go,” I said.

We walked. The old mill was on the other side of the river, the same place we had been before. The wheel was broken, the roof was sagging, the windows were dark and empty. The door was open, and the wind blew through it with a sound like a voice I could not understand.

We went inside. The floor was wooden and rotting, the walls black with damp. The light came through the broken windows in narrow strips.

“We are looking for a book,” Ellison said.

“We are,” I said.

“It could be anywhere.”

“It could.”

Ellison walked to the old machinery. He began to examine the broken wheel, the rusted gears, the spaces between them. He moved methodically, his hands following the contours of the metal, as though he expected the book to be hidden in plain sight.

Merritt walked to the far end of the room. He stood still, looking at the wall. He did not move. He simply stood there, his eyes fixed on the plaster, as though he were listening to something I could not hear.

I began to search the corners. I looked under the broken beams. I looked in the spaces where the floorboards had rotted away. I found nothing.

“There is nothing here,” Ellison said.

“I have found nothing,” I said.

I looked at Merritt. He had not moved.

“Merritt,” I said.

He did not respond.

“Merritt,” I said again.

He turned his head slightly, but he did not look at me.

“Have you found anything?” I said.

He shook his head once, then turned back to the wall.

I walked over to him. “What are you looking at?”

He did not answer. He simply pointed to a spot on the wall where the plaster was darker, as though something had been pressed against it long ago. I looked at it, but I could see nothing remarkable.

“It is nothing,” I said.

“It is nothing,” he repeated, but his voice carried no conviction.

I left him there and continued searching. I looked behind the old millstones. I looked in the dust and the cobwebs. I found nothing.

We were still searching when the door opened. A man came in. He was not Calloway. He was not the man in the grey coat. He was one of the candidates, a man I had seen before but whose name I did not know. He was carrying a small book, bound in dark leather.

He looked at us, and then he looked at the book in his hand.

“You are looking for this,” he said.

I looked at the book. It was the same size, the same colour, the same binding as the book we had found in the garden. The handwriting, I could see even from where I stood, was the same brown ink.

“We are,” I said.

“I found it,” he said. “I was told to bring it to the office.”

“We were told the same,” I said.

He looked at me, and then he looked at Ellison, and then he looked at Merritt. He did not say anything. He turned and walked toward the door.

Ellison moved first. “Wait,” he said.

The man stopped.

“We were told to bring the book,” Ellison said. “We were not told there would be others.”

“Neither was I,” the man said.

“Then what are we to do?”

The man looked at the book in his hand, and then he looked at the door.

“I am going to the office,” he said. “You may come with me. But I will not give you the book.”

He walked out.

Ellison looked at me. “We should follow him.”

“We should,” I said.

Merritt finally turned from the wall. He looked at us, then at the door, then at the spot on the wall he had been studying. He did not speak, but he walked toward the door, and we followed.

We caught up to the man on the path. He was walking quickly, his hand on the book. He did not look back.

Ellison walked beside him. “How did you find it?” he said.

The man did not answer.

“We searched the same room,” Ellison said. “We found nothing.”

Still the man did not answer.

“I am asking because I want to know,” Ellison said. “I am not asking because I want to take it from you.”

The man slowed slightly. He looked at Ellison, then at me, then at Merritt.

“I looked where you did not look,” he said.

“Where?”

“The wall,” he said. “The east wall. There is a loose stone near the floor. I moved it, and the book was behind it.”

I stopped walking. The east wall. That was where Merritt had been standing, where he had pointed to the darker plaster. He had seen it, or sensed it, but he had not said anything.

“You knew about the stone?” I said to Merritt.

He did not answer. He did not look at me. He simply kept walking.

Ellison looked at me, his eyes narrowing. I did not know whether he was angry or merely thoughtful.

“You knew where it was,” Ellison said to Merritt. “You did not tell us.”

Merritt did not respond.

We walked in silence for the rest of the way. The man reached the office, placed the book on the clerk’s desk, and was dismissed. The clerk looked at us, recorded our presence, and said nothing.

We walked away.

“He knew,” Ellison said, once we were outside. “Merritt knew where the book was. He could have told us, and we could have found it before that other man.”

“Perhaps,” I said.

“Why would he not say?”

I did not answer. I had no answer.

I walked home slowly. I thought about Merritt, standing at the wall, pointing at the darker plaster. I thought about the man who had found the book, who had known exactly where to look. I thought about what Ellison had asked, and what the man had answered.

I did not know why Merritt had held back. I did not know whether he had wanted the book for himself, or whether he had simply not cared, or whether he had been testing us.

But I knew that the real prize was not the book. It was the knowledge of where to look. And that knowledge was something that could be given, or withheld, or traded.

I did not know what I would have done if I had found the book first. But I knew that I would have had to decide—whether to share how I found it, or to keep that knowledge for myself.

I did not know whether I would have shared.

But I knew the question would come again.

The candidate with the book did not look back. Leon noticed how quickly the others began making assumptions about him. One thought he had cheated. Another thought he had been given the answer. Leon had no proof of either. He kept walking.
