# Chapter 38 — The Interlude

The days between the Greyhound and the next case were quiet, the way the days between cases often are. I had learned that the room would not change unless someone spoke the name of the man who had died there, and I had learned that no one knew the name. That was the shape of it: a room that listened, a trigger that could not be pulled, and a mystery that would not be solved.

I went to Margaret's for bread, and the shop was emptier than it had been the week before. Two customers sat at separate tables, and neither spoke. Margaret wiped the counter and did not smile.

"You look like a man who has found a door and cannot open it," she said.

"I have found a door," I said. "And I have found that the door is locked, and the key is lost, and no one remembers what the door was for."

She set the loaf on the counter and did not let it go. "That is the way of this town. There are doors everywhere, and most of them are locked, and the ones that are open lead to rooms that do not stay as they are left." She paused, and looked at me. "You haven't been in for three days. I was beginning to think you'd found another door."

I took the bread and went home, and I spent the afternoon reading the old notebooks I had found in the drawer. They were the notebooks of a man I did not know, written in a hand I had not seen before, and they spoke of rooms that changed, of letters that came from nowhere, of clocks that struck hours they had not been wound for. They were the notes of someone who had been asking the same questions I was asking, and they had not found the answers either.

I shut the book and looked out at the lane. The house at the end of it stood with its rosemary on the sill and its glass open to the cold, and the lamp was not yet lit, because the hour was not yet come. I had begun to think of the house as a kind of clock, keeping its own time, indifferent to the town's.

---

On the Saturday, I went to Calloway's office to report what I had seen. He listened without interrupting, and when I had finished, he did not speak for a long time.

"A room that listens," he said at last. "And a name that is lost."

"Yes."

He looked at me the way a man looks at a thing he has been trying to remember. "There is a woman on High Street," he said, "who has been receiving letters. Letters that speak of tomorrow. I think you should see her."

I nodded, and he gave me the address, and I went out into the cold.

The woman's name was Mrs. Hart, and she lived in a house that had seen better days—the paint was peeling, the gate was loose, and the windows were dark even in the daylight. She opened the door when I knocked, and she looked at me the way a woman looks at a man who has been sent by the constable: with caution, and with hope, and with the suspicion that he will not be able to help.

"I have been receiving letters," she said. "They come in the post, with the same stamp, the same hand, the same paper. They speak of things that have not yet happened. And they have been right, every time."

"May I see one?"

She went to a drawer and took out a thin envelope, yellowed at the edges, and handed it to me. I opened it and read:

*The flour will be up a penny before the week is out. The man who lives across the lane will take ill on Tuesday. The rain will come on Wednesday, and it will not stop until Friday.*

The date on the envelope was a week old. I looked at her.

"All of those things came true," she said. "The flour went up. The man fell ill. The rain came and did not stop."

"Who sent these?"

"I do not know. They are addressed to me, in my name, but they have no sender. I have asked the postmaster, and he says they come through the regular post, but he cannot tell me who sent them."

I looked at the letter again, and I saw something I had not seen before: the date on the envelope was tomorrow's date. The letter had been postmarked for the day after it was written, but it had arrived the day before.

"These are not predictions," I said. "They are instructions."

I left Mrs. Hart with the letter and went home to write it down.

---

*16 Jan. The letters: they come from the future, but not the way a prediction comes. They come as a description of what will happen if nothing is done. The flour will rise, the man will fall ill, the rain will fall—all because no one has acted. I do not know who sends them, but I know that they are not warnings. They are the shape of the world as it would be, if the world were left alone. I am beginning to see that these cases are not separate. They are all asking the same question: what happens when the world is not watched?*

I shut the book and sat with the question. The room at the Greyhound had taught me that a trigger could be impossible to pull. The letters on High Street were teaching me that a prediction could be a condition. I did not yet know what the clock on Mill Lane would teach me, but I was beginning to suspect that all three cases were parts of the same pattern—a pattern of triggers and conditions, of watching and being watched, of the world responding to the act of attention.

I was learning, slowly, that the anomalies were not the things themselves. They were the responses. And the responses followed rules.

I did not know the rules yet. But I was beginning to see their shape.
