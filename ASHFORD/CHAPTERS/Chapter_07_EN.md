# Chapter 7 — An Incomplete Address

I stared at the envelope from the day before for a long while.

On the front: Leon Ashcroft.

I turned it over.

No return address.

No stamp.

No post office mark.

Only a plain white envelope.

And at home, there was the paper I had found — the sheet with the older handwriting, the two faint words on the back. Ashcroft. Bellweather.

Two things. One name, repeating.

I looked up.

Margaret was still standing before me.

"You've been carrying that for two days," she said.

I looked at her.

"How did you know?"

Margaret raised an eyebrow.

"Because you haven't once put it down. Not even to take a sip of your coffee."

She pointed at the envelope in my hand.

"You must be reading far too much into a name."

I nodded.

"True."

"Aurelius Ashcroft," said Margaret.

"You've heard it."

"Yes."

Margaret nodded.

"Then you're already close."

"Close to what?"

Margaret did not answer at once.

She went behind the counter.

"Daniel will come within the hour."

"Why?"

"Trust me. Daniel knows more than he ought to."

I went back to my table.

The newspaper I had been reading was still open.

But now I could not concentrate.

I read one article twice without taking in what it said.

Margaret looked at me.

"If you keep staring at the paper like that, he won't give you the answer."

"I know."

"Good."

She went back to her work.

The local paper on the next table carried far more ordinary news: the weekend's sporting fixtures, shop advertisements, vacant positions, a notice of a residents' meeting, and a few events to be held at the town centre.

There was something strange about how life went on around a private matter.

On one page someone was looking for work.

On another someone was selling a house.

On the next there was a notice for a community event.

While here, at this table, I was trying to understand why a single name could appear in more than one place — a sheet of paper found in my own house, and an envelope sent to me with no explanation.

Alderwick did not stop simply because I had a question.

No town did.

---

About an hour later, Daniel came.

He carried the same book as a few days before.

The moment he saw me, he said:

"You look like a man thinking about something."

"I am thinking about something."

"A problem?"

"Not necessarily."

Daniel sat down.

Margaret brought his coffee.

He looked at me.

"Don't tell me you've started investigating this town."

"I'm not investigating anything."

Margaret said from behind the counter:

"Aurelius Ashcroft."

Daniel turned.

"Ashcroft?"

"Yes."

Daniel raised an eyebrow.

"Margaret says you've been carrying that name around for days."

"I said two."

Margaret, from behind the counter:

"It counts as one whole."

Daniel laughed.

I smiled as well.

Margaret looked at us.

"You two are far too serious."

Daniel said:

"He started it."

Margaret smiled.

"Good. Then you can go on arguing without me."

She went to the counter.

Daniel looked at me.

"All right. Tell me."

"I found a sheet of paper in my house. Older handwriting on the back. Two words. Ashcroft. Bellweather."

Daniel fell silent.

Not for long.

But long enough to make Margaret pause for a moment.

"Ashcroft? Bellweather?" Daniel repeated. "Have you heard of either?"

"What? I'm writing a thesis on the older records of Alderwick. And that combination — a name and a street — is not an ordinary thing."

"What do you mean?"

Daniel set down his cup.

"The older records don't always agree with the newer ones. Places that appear in one and not the other. Names that were used once and then dropped. If you're looking for the record of a house on a street, sometimes the house is there in one source and absent in another. Sometimes the name attached to it changes."

"Like Bellweather Lane."

Daniel looked at me.

"Yes."

Margaret said from behind the counter:

"So now your name is Ashcroft. And you arrived in Alderwick. And the name Aurelius Ashcroft has followed you here."

"True."

Daniel pulled the newspaper from his table.

He laid it in front of me.

"If you're curious, tomorrow morning I'll bring something from the Academy. Old records. And we can see what the older sources say about that street."

"Would you?"

"Yes."

"Good."

---

In the afternoon I left the café early.

I walked toward Bellweather Lane.

Not to go into the empty house.

I only wanted to look at it once more.

The house still looked the same.

Old fence.

Closed windows.

Paint peeling from the walls.

No sign that anyone lived there.

I stood on the far side of the road.

I took the map from my bag.

I opened it.

The street looked simple.

The house numbers ran in order.

I looked at my house.

Then at the empty house.

No number after 13.

I folded the map.

Then I looked at the empty house again.

There was something I had only just realised.

The distance between house number 13 and the empty house was quite close.

Too close to be called a separate building in some old numbering systems.

Perhaps the house had once been part of number 13.

I made a note of that possibility.

Then I went home.

---

That night I looked for information in Arthur's history book.

I opened the section on the development of Bellweather Lane.

Several pages explained the changes of street names.

There was a picture of an old map.

I read slowly.

Year by year.

Street names changed.

Some buildings vanished.

Some new buildings were raised.

I found Bellweather Lane.

But the house at the end of the road was not mentioned.

I took out the map Arthur had given me.

I compared it with my own.

The same.

No additional house.

I leaned back.

Perhaps the house had indeed been built after that map was made.

That still made sense.

I took up my notebook.

I wrote:

Empty house — possibly built after the map was made.

To be asked of Arthur.

I closed the book.

I opened the history section on the street again.

There were a few notes about changes to the district after the horse-carriage lines developed and the town centre began to grow.

The streets that had once merely connected small houses slowly became part of a denser neighbourhood.

Some houses were extended.

Some plots were divided.

New buildings appeared among the old ones.

House numbers could have changed along with those changes.

Nothing strange about it.

At least in theory.

But one thing still did not fit.

If the empty house had existed for a long while, why was there no trace of it on the map I held?

I closed the book.

Tomorrow I would ask Arthur.

---

The next day I returned to Arthur's shop.

A bell rang as I entered.

Arthur was sitting behind his desk.

He looked up.

"Leon."

"Sorry to disturb you."

"You're not disturbing me."

I laid the map on the desk.

"I want to ask something."

Arthur looked at the map.

"Bellweather Lane?"

"Yes."

He put on his spectacles.

"What do you want to know?"

"There's an empty house at the end of the road."

Arthur went quiet.

Not for long.

But long enough for me to notice.

"Which house?"

I pointed at the map.

"Beside house number thirteen."

Arthur looked at the map.

Then looked at me.

"Beside thirteen?"

"Yes."

He picked up a pencil.

He drew a small line.

"Leon."

"Hm?"

"On this map there is no house there."

"I know."

"So why are you looking for it?"

I explained everything.

The empty house.

Aurelius Ashcroft.

The paper found in my house.

The envelope with no return address.

The repeating name.

Arthur listened without interrupting.

When I had finished, he did not answer at once.

He took a large book from the shelf.

He laid it on the desk.

The dust was quite thick.

Arthur blew on its surface.

Dust scattered into the air.

I drew back a little.

"You could open the window," I said.

"I know."

"Why don't you?"

"Because it's cold."

I gave a small laugh.

He began to turn the pages.

"These are old building records."

"Official?"

"Partly."

"Partly?"

"Private records."

He went on searching.

I watched his hands.

Page after page.

Then he stopped.

His finger rested on a section that had yellowed.

I moved closer.

Arthur read something in silence.

"There is a house there."

I looked at him.

"Where?"

Arthur pointed at a small note.

"At the end of Bellweather Lane."

"Its number?"

Arthur read again.

Then shook his head.

"None."

I was silent.

"Its name?"

Arthur looked at the page a long while.

Then he said:

"Bellweather House."

I frowned.

"Bellweather House?"

"That is what's written."

I looked at the note.

There was a date.

1791.

I looked at Arthur.

"Why isn't the house on the map now?"

Arthur closed the book.

"That is a better question."

I waited.

He smiled faintly.

"And I am afraid I don't have the answer yet."

I said nothing.

Arthur stood.

He returned the book to the shelf.

Before sitting back down, he said:

"If you find anything else, bring it here."

I nodded.

"Very well."

I took the map.

Then I stopped.

"Arthur."

"Yes?"

"Does Bellweather House still exist?"

Arthur looked at me.

"If you mean the building..."

He paused.

"...I don't know."

I asked:

"Did Aurelius Ashcroft ever live there?"

Arthur did not answer at once.

He looked at me.

For a long while.

"The name is written there," he said finally. "Bellweather House. 1791. That is all the record says."

"Did he live in the house?"

Arthur was quiet.

"The record doesn't say who lived there. It only says the house was there. Names on old records can mean many things. Ownership. Occupancy. Something else entirely."

I was silent.

"And I," I said, "am an Ashcroft."

Arthur looked at me.

For a long while.

"The name is the same," he said. "What that means is not something I can tell you from a book."

---

I left the shop.

The little bell rang behind me.

Before I went out, I caught sight of the shelves around Arthur.

Among the local history books stood several old maps, town charts, property lists, and building catalogues.

All of them looked like things that would draw the attention of most people not at all.

Yet in an old town like Alderwick, documents of that kind could hold something that existed on no modern map.

A street name that had changed.

A building that had been pulled down.

A property that had changed hands.

A house that had once stood but had since vanished from the general record.

I was beginning to understand why Arthur kept all of it.

A town did not change only when new buildings were raised.

It also changed when something disappeared.

For the first time since I had found the paper in my house, I felt that perhaps there was something I truly did not know.

Not because I had not been careful enough.

Not because there was too little information.

But because the record of the house itself was incomplete.

And that troubled me far more than an envelope with no sender.

---

That evening I met Daniel at Margaret's.

He looked at me the way he did when he thought I was hiding something.

"You've been different," he said. "Distracted."

I told him it was nothing he needed to hear.

But the question had begun to press on me, so I asked him plainly: if a man found something at his own house that he could not explain — something he could not even be sure was meant for him — where was the proper place to begin?

Daniel did not press for the reason.

"The constable," he said. "Calloway. His office is near the Market Square. He's the one who deals with what people would rather not mention."

I thanked him and said no more.

---

I walked home.

In my jacket pocket, the envelope was still there.

This time I did not intend to throw it away.

Nor did I yet intend to open any possibility.

I would only gather the facts.

One by one.

Until something began to fit.
