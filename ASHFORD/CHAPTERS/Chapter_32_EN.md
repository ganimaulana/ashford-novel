# Chapter 32 — The Distant Echo

Tuesday came with a sky like lead and a wind that cut through wool, and the town went about its business with the particular silence of a place that knew the cold would not lift. The clouds hung low and heavy, pressing down on the rooftops, and the chimneys laboured to carry the smoke up against the weight of the air. I had not slept well. The second questioner at the inn had stayed in my mind the way a name stays on a page when the ink is still wet, and I had turned it over enough times to know that I would not learn more by turning it again. The letter from the previous day lay in my drawer, its warning still fresh: *Do not seek the man from the inn.* I had not sought him, and yet he had sought me.

I needed to learn something else. The questions that had begun with Foster had spread like ripples in a pond, touching the inn, the church, the house at the end of the lane. Each answer I found only opened another question, and I had begun to feel that I was not so much solving a puzzle as being drawn into a web whose edges I could not see.

I went to the pump in the morning, because the pump is where the town speaks without noticing it speaks, and I stood in the line with my bucket and listened. The women spoke of the price of coal and the weight of the snow on the church roof and the date of the Christmas service. They spoke of the cold and the frost and the difficulty of keeping the pipes from freezing. I heard no mention of Foster. I heard no mention of the inn. I heard no mention of a stranger asking questions. The town had taken in the news and had already begun to let it go.

That was the first thing I learned: a question asked and answered is soon forgotten, unless someone chooses to remember it. The town's memory was short, and its attention was divided among the thousand small concerns of winter. I was not the town. I had chosen to remember, and that choice was beginning to have consequences.

I went home and sat at the desk with the fire low, and I thought of the list, and of the name Hodge on it, and of Arthur's words: *a name that appears in the margins, in places where the record does not quite fit.* I had not tested that name. I had asked Arthur about it, but I had not put it into the town's mouth to see where it would go. The name had been in the margins of the parish books, in a hand that was not the clerk's. That meant someone had wanted to keep it, and I did not know whether that keeping was protection or erasure.

I decided to do so.

I took the book and went to Margaret's at noon, because the room was warm and the tables were full, and because a question asked in a room full of men is a question that does not have to be answered to be heard. The shop was crowded, the windows steamed, and the smell of stew and bread filled the air. I bought a cup and stood at the counter, and Margaret set it before me with the cloth in her hand. Her eyes met mine for a moment, and I saw that she had already heard something, but she did not speak it aloud.

"Foster is gone," she said, as if we had been speaking of it.

"He is," I said. "I am not asking about Foster."

She looked at me, and I saw her measure the words before I spoke them. The room was full of talk, but I felt the silence that formed around us, the quiet attention of a few who had learned to listen.

"Arthur showed me a name in the parish books," I said. "Hodge. The name appears in the margins, in places where the record is not complete. I do not know what it means. I thought you might have heard it."

Margaret's hand did not stop moving on the cloth. "Hodge," she said, as if tasting the word. "I have heard the name. Not often. It is one of those names that belongs to the town but does not belong to any house in it. I do not know who he was. I do not know whether he is still living. I know only that the name is spoken sometimes, by the older men, in the way they speak of things that were before them."

"Was he a keeper of keys?"

"I cannot say."

"A keeper of records?"

"I cannot say."

"A keeper of the house at the end?"

She looked at me then, and her hand was still. The cloth hung motionless in her grip, and I saw the weight of the question settle on her.

"I cannot say what he kept," she said. "I can say that the name is not spoken often, and that when it is spoken, it is spoken in the same breath as the house. Whether he kept it or was kept by it, I do not know."

I thanked her and drank the cup, and I left the name in the room as I left the cup, and I did not know whether it would travel, but I had placed it in the town's mouth. I walked out into the cold, and the wind bit at my face, and I knew that I had done what I had set out to do. The name was no longer mine alone.

I went home and wrote the date in the book, and I did not write what I had done, because the doing of it was the test, and the test was not complete.

I waited.

The afternoon passed as afternoons pass in a town that is settling into the week before Christmas: the shops shut early, the road grew quiet, and the man who tended the lamps came out before the dark had fully settled, and I watched him from my window, moving from post to post, and I thought of Hodge, and of the lamp at the end of the lane, and of the house that had no number, and of the name that appeared in the margins.

At six, the knock came.

It was not the postmaster's boy. It was not Daniel. It was the verger's boy, the one who carried messages between the church and the town, and he stood at the door with his hat in his hand and said: "If you please, sir, the verger said I was to tell you that the name was spoken at the church this afternoon."

"What name."

"Hodge, sir. The verger said you would know the name. He said it was spoken by a man who came to the church to ask about the parish records, and the man asked whether there was a record of a Hodge in the margins, and the verger told him he did not know, and the man went away."

"Did the man give a name?"

"No, sir."

"Did he say why he was asking?"

"He said he was looking for a record that had been moved. That is what the verger told me to tell you. He said the man was not from this town, and he did not stay long, and he went away, and the verger thought you should know."

I thanked the boy and he went away, and I stood at the door with the cold coming in, and I understood what had happened. I had spoken the name Hodge at Margaret's at noon. By the afternoon, a stranger had asked the verger about a record involving Hodge. I had not told the stranger. Margaret had not left her counter. The room had been full of men, and any one of them could have carried the name out of the room and into the church, and any one of them could have been the stranger, or could have been the one who told the stranger.

I did not know whether the name had moved through gossip, or through deliberate transmission. I did not know whether the stranger was the same man who had asked about Foster at the inn. But I knew only that the name had reached the church, and that it had reached it quickly.

I sat with the fire low and the knowledge of it in my pocket, and I did not write what I had learned, because the learning of it was the thing I wanted to keep. I had given the town a name, and the town had passed it to a stranger, and the stranger had gone to the church, and the verger had told me that a record had been moved.

That was the detail I had not expected.

*A record that had been moved.*

I had not mentioned records to Margaret. I had mentioned the name and the margins. The margin was a record detail, but I had not said that any record had been moved. Someone had added that. Or someone had known about it already. I could not tell which was more unsettling—the speed of the name's return, or the new detail it had carried with it.

I went to bed with the cold at the pane and the name Hodge in my head, and I knew that I had learned two things that day: that information moved, and that it carried other details with it. I did not know whether those details were true, or whether they were added to guide me, or to mislead me. I did not yet know whether I was the one being tested, or whether I was simply the instrument by which the name had reached its destination.

I lay awake for a long time, listening to the wind in the eaves. The town was quiet, but I knew it was not sleeping. Somewhere, someone was writing something down. I did not know who, or why, but I knew that the name Hodge had moved beyond the room where I had spoken it. I could not say how, but I could no longer pretend that information moved without consequence.

The next morning, I woke with the name still in my mind. I went to the pump again, and the women were there with their talk of the price of coal and the weight of the snow on the church roof. They spoke of nothing unusual. The name Hodge had not been mentioned. The stranger had not returned. The town had moved on, but I had not. I carried the name with me, and I knew that I would not be able to put it down until I understood what it meant.

I walked home through the lane, and I thought of the moving record, and of the hand that had moved it, and of the hands that had carried the name from Margaret's counter to the church door. The town was full of hands, and I did not know which of them were mine.
