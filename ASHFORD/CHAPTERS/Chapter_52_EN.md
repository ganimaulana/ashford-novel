CHAPTER 52 — THE SECOND COPY

The parish requires a surgeon before it will put a man in the ground.

I had not known that until I needed to know it. At a little after eight on the Friday morning I stood outside the house at the end of the lane with the key in my pocket, and I understood that everything the old constable had ever done for the town now fell to me in its least interesting form.

The body first. Then the certificate. Then the sexton. Then the paper.

The surgeon was a careful man who asked me no questions I did not want, and who examined the body and pronounced what everyone already knew. He wrote his certificate and named no cause beyond the one that needed no naming, and I paid him and he went.

The sexton knew the old constable by sight, which in Alderwick is a form of record. He set the burial for Saturday.

"The third day," he said. "It is the custom."

I said that was well.

The Hartley girl came while I was still there. She stood at the door with her shawl over her head and looked at the chair the way people look at a thing that has been moved out of its place.

"I brought him his soup on Tuesday," she said.

"I know."

"He told me to take the bowl back when I was done. I've still got the bowl." She looked at me. "I can bring it for the tea, if there's a tea."

I said she could bring the bowl, and she went away wiping her eyes with the corner of the shawl, and I understood then that he had been fed by that house for years, and that I had not known it, and that there were probably a dozen such arrangements in the town I had not known about, and that the man I thought I had been visiting alone had in fact been visited by the whole lane in turns, each of us supposing the others did not come.

That was the first thing of the morning that I wrote down. Not because it was important. Because it was true, and the page was for true things.

---

We buried him on Saturday, in the churchyard of St. Bartholomew's, in weather that had turned cold and still.

There were more people than I expected. Not many. Perhaps twenty. Most of them older than him, or near it, and most of them standing at the distance of people who knew a man by his office and not by his room. I saw the woman from the pump. I saw a man I took for a farmer, who stood with his hat in his hands and never put it back on. I saw Calloway, who came alone and stood at the edge of the group and nodded once when the service was over, and did not speak to anyone.

No one said anything that was not required.

The old constable had left no instructions, and no family, and no money for stone. The sexton said the parish would mark the grave in wood until someone saw fit to order otherwise, and I said I would see to it, and he looked at me in the way people had begun looking at me that week, as though I had taken on some office that no one had offered me.

I did not correct him.

I stayed after the others went, and stood by the grave a while, and then I went home, because there was nothing else to do there and because I had learned from him not to make a show of remaining.

---

Calloway came to my door that afternoon.

He was not alone. The man with him was younger, broad through the chest, with a face that had been broken once and set badly, and he stood half a step behind Calloway and said nothing from first to last. He had the look of a man who was used to going into rooms first, and who had been told not to, today.

I let them in. I had nothing else to do. The house was cold, because I had not lit the fire, and I did not offer them a chair because I had not thought of it, and they stood.

"I am sorry about Vale," Calloway said.

"Thank you."

"You were with him at the end."

"I found him."

"Found him." He repeated it without weight. "When?"

"Before dawn. Thursday. I went up the lane as he had asked me to, and he was in the chair."

"You had been visiting him."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Since the spring."

He let that stand. He rubbed his jaw with the heel of his hand, slowly, the way a man rubs a thing to make it warm.

"He did not speak to me of it," Calloway said. "Of your visiting."

"No."

"No. Nor to me of him, except once, and that was only to say there was a man came to his door in February who asked questions in the right order." He said it watching me. "That was all he said. I have turned it over since. It is not the sort of thing he was given to saying."

I had nothing to add to it, so I said nothing.

The younger man's eyes moved once around the room. I noticed it because it was the only thing he did.

"You were the last to see him," Calloway said.

"So far as I know, yes."

"Tuesday. The girl at the pump says you were there Tuesday, and Thursday before dawn."

"The girl at the pump is right."

"And between Tuesday and Thursday you were not up the lane?"

"No."

He asked it again, ten minutes later, in the middle of a question about the doctor, and gave it no weight the second time either. I had been taught by the old constable to notice when a question came twice, and I had been taught by the old constable not to let the noticing show. I answered it the second time exactly as I had answered it the first.

Calloway looked at me for a moment longer than the answer deserved.

"Did he give you anything?"

"He gave me nothing."

That was true, and it was not the whole of the truth, and the old constable had taught me the difference between the two, and the use of it. There were things of his that had come into my keeping. I had no reason to tell Calloway more than he had asked.

"Did he leave anything in your keeping?"

"Some of his papers came into my hands. They were his."

"His papers."

"Personal things. Letters. A journal."

"Where are they now?"

"With me."

He rubbed his jaw again. The younger man had not moved, except that he had put his weight onto his other foot, which I noticed, and which I could not have said why I noticed.

"There are some documents belonging to the office," Calloway said. "From Vale's time. Reports that were never returned. I am not saying he had them. I am saying they were never returned, and he was the man who held them last, and the office has an interest in knowing where its own papers went."

"If the office names the papers, I can tell you whether I have seen them."

"That is not what I asked."

"No."

He was quiet a while. Outside, someone passed the window, and I heard the cart go by that passed every afternoon, and the town continued around us, doing the thing it had done every day of the old constable's life and every day since.

"You will understand," Calloway said, "that a man who keeps an office for fifty years and leaves no account of it leaves a question. I am not here to take anything from you, Ashcroft. I am here because the question will be asked of me, and I would rather carry an answer than have to say I never thought to ask."

"I understand."

"Then you will give me the papers."

"I will think on it."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the one I have."

He looked at me then with something that was not anger and was not kindness, and I understood that he was deciding what I was, and that the deciding was not finished. The old constable had told me once that most men give you a refusal shaped like an answer and you go away satisfied. I had tried to give him the other kind, and I did not know whether he was the sort of man who could tell the difference.

"The journal," he said. "You said he left a journal."

"Yes."

"Is it complete?"

"I have not read it."

That was true. I had not read it. I had read some of it, in order, as I had been told, and I had stopped where I had been told to stop, and the rest of it was unopened, and I did not owe this man the distinction.

Calloway nodded. He turned toward the door. The younger man turned first, which I noticed, and which I also could not have said why I noticed.

At the threshold Calloway stopped.

"One thing more. He kept copies."

"Copies."

"Of reports. When he was constable here, he kept a second copy of everything that went out of the office. Every man who has held this desk since has supposed it was a habit of a careful man. I have come to suppose it was something else, because the copies are the ones that are right, Ashcroft, and the ones that came back to the office are not always the ones that went out." He looked at me steadily. "If you find any such thing among his papers, you will know now what it is. That is all."

He went out. The younger man had already gone down the step, and stood in the lane with his face turned the way they would walk, and I noticed that he did not look back at the house, and that this also was a thing done on purpose.

I shut the door.

I stood in the cold of the room and turned it over.

He had known about the Tuesday. He had known about the Thursday. He had not known about the October night, because no one had seen me carry the box home, or no one had thought to say. He had asked me twice about the interval between, and he had named the copies without being asked, and given me the reason for them, and I understood that the giving was not for my benefit. It was a thing set down between us, like a subject the old constable used to put down and leave, to be returned to when he chose.

And underneath it, the thing that stayed with me the rest of the day:

The office knew that Vale had kept second copies. Calloway had said the copies that came back were not always the ones that went out. If that had been happening for twenty years, then the office had known something was wrong for twenty years.

The old constable had kept his copies because he did not trust the road between his desk and theirs. He had told me that in his own way, in February, about a transcription, about a quotation, about a sentence. *If there is only one copy, there is no copy.* I had written it down as wisdom. It had not been wisdom. It had been practice.

I took out my notebook.

I wrote:

*Calloway came. Asked about the papers, the journal, the visits. Twice about the Tuesday–Thursday. He knows Vale kept second copies of reports; says the copies that came back were altered. Did not ask about the mill. Did not ask about the door. Never said the word agreement.*

I looked at the last three sentences for a long time.

A man who asks about everything except three things has told you where the three things are.

I did not write what I thought that meant. I had been taught to keep what I concluded apart from what I was told, and that day I was told very little, and I concluded a great deal, and I knew the difference between the two was the only thing I had that nobody else in Alderwick had.

I closed the book.

Outside, the lane was quiet, and the evening was coming down, and somewhere a lamp was being lit for the dark, and I knew that the questions would come again, because Calloway was a man who carried a question until it was answered, and because I had given him a refusal instead of an answer, and because and I knew the questions would come again

I lit the fire.

Then I went to the press where the box stood, and I did not open it, and I sat with my hand on it until the room was warm.
