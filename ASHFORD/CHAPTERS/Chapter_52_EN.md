# Chapter 52 — The Window

I went back to Bellweather Lane on a Thursday. The frost had melted, and the lane was muddy underfoot. I had not brought bread this time, and I had not planned to go. I had finished my morning errands and found myself taking the road without having decided to.

The door was not latched when I arrived. It stood open a few inches, enough to let the smoke from the chimney drift out sideways. I pushed it open and found him at the back of the cottage, not at the door. He was standing on a low stool, one hand braced against the wall, the other working at the frame of a window. The glass was cracked, a thin line running from the upper corner to the latch. He had a roll of putty and a small pane of glass on the ground beside him.

He did not turn when I came around the corner. He was concentrating on the frame, his fingers moving slowly, carefully. I waited.

"The glass broke," he said, still not turning. "Yesterday. I have not had time to fix it."

I looked at the frame. The old putty was dry and crumbling. The cracked pane was still in place, but it would not hold much longer. I could see where he had already tried to work the putty loose; the edges were scored with a knife, and there were small chips of dried putty on the floor below the window. He had been at it for some time.

"Do you have a spare pane?"

"On the ground."

I picked up the glass and examined it. It was the right size, cut cleanly. He had prepared it already. He was just having trouble removing the old pane.

I set the glass aside and stepped up beside him. "Let me take the old one out."

He looked at me for a moment, then stepped down from the stool. He did not argue. He handed me a small pry bar and a knife.

"The putty is hard," he said. "You will need to work it loose."

I knelt by the window and began to work the old putty out of the frame. It was stiff, but not impossible. I worked slowly, careful not to crack the old glass further. He watched, but he did not correct me. His hands were unsteady when he tried to hold the frame. He put them down before the tremor could make the work worse.

After a few minutes, the old pane came free. I set it aside and cleaned the frame, scraping out the remaining putty with the knife. He handed me the new pane, and I fitted it into place. He showed me how to press the putty into the gaps, how to smooth it with a knife so it would seal. I did it. He watched. When I had finished, he stepped forward and ran his finger along the edge of the putty.

"It will hold," he said.

He picked up the old pane and carried it to a pile of broken glass at the edge of the yard. I washed my hands at the pump. He came back and stood beside me.

"You did not have to do that," he said.

"I know."

He looked at me for a moment. "Then why did you?"

"Because you were doing it alone."

He gave a small nod, as if that answer required no further examination.

"But you did."

I did not say anything. I dried my hands on my coat.

He went inside and put the kettle on. I followed. The fire was low, and he added wood before he sat down. I noticed that he had set two cups on the table before I even arrived—they were already there, waiting, as though he had expected someone to come. He did not mention them. I did not ask.

He poured the tea and handed me a cup. We sat in silence for a while. The window was fixed. The room was warmer for it.

After a time, he stood and walked to the cupboard. He took out a loaf of bread and a small piece of cheese. He set them on the table between us, without asking whether I was hungry. He cut two slices and pushed one toward me. I took it. We ate in silence.

"Thursday," he said, after a while.

"Thursday?"

"You came on Thursday last week as well. You are becoming predictable."

I considered that. "Is that a problem?"

He shrugged. "It means you are settling in. The town will not notice you until you are predictable. Then you become part of its routine."

I chewed the bread. It was dense, slightly stale, but good.

"And you?" I said. "Do you notice?"

He did not answer immediately. He took a sip of his tea, then set the cup down.

"I notice the window," he said. "It is fixed."

I let that sit. We finished the bread and cheese. He collected the plates and set them in the sink. I offered to help, but he waved me off. Instead, I picked up the broom and swept the fragments of putty and dust from the floor beneath the window. He watched me do it, and he did not stop me.

"It is a good thing," he said, "to know where the broom is."

I swept the dust into a pan and carried it out to the edge of the yard. When I came back, he was standing by the window, looking at the new pane. The light was fading, and the glass caught the last of the grey afternoon.

He did not answer. He turned from the window and went to stoke the fire. I watched him for a moment. He moved slowly, deliberately, but there was a steadiness in his hands now that had not been there when I arrived. The trembling had stopped.

            I looked around the cottage while the fire caught. The room was small, but it held the marks of a long life: a worn chair, a table with a single scratch running its length, an old clock on the mantelpiece that ticked steadily. I had not noticed the clock before. It was old, its face yellowed, its hands moving with a quiet precision.

            "That clock has been here longer than I have," he said, following my gaze. "It was here when I arrived. The man who sold me the cottage said it had belonged to his father. It keeps good time."

            I looked at it. It was keeping the correct hour.

            I noticed a loose nail on the window frame where the putty had been. I found a hammer by the hearth and tapped it back into place. He watched me do it, and when I was done, he nodded.

            The fire was burning steadily now, and the room had warmed. I sat down again, and he sat across from me. We did not speak for a while. The silence was not empty; it was full of the small sounds of the cottage: the ticking of the clock, the crackling of the fire, the wind moving through the cracks in the window frame.

            I looked at the window again. The new pane was in place, the putty smooth along the edges. The light caught the glass differently now; it was clearer, less clouded than the old pane had been.

            "It lets in more light," I said.

            "It does. The old glass had gone dark over the years. I had meant to replace it before."

            He did not say why he had not. I did not ask.

            After a time, he reached for the kettle and poured more water. He did not ask if I wanted more tea; he simply poured a second cup and pushed it toward me. I took it.

            "You are learning to sit still," he said.

            "I am learning."

            "I can see that."

            We drank the tea. The fire settled, and the room grew darker. I looked at the clock again. It continued its steady ticking.

            I heard a shutter shift outside, caught by the wind. It was loose, banging softly against the stone. I stood and went to the door. The cold met me as I stepped out. The shutter was hanging by one hinge, the other broken. I lifted it and propped it against the wall. It was not a repair, but it would hold for the night.

            When I came back inside, he was watching me.

            "You do not have to fix everything," he said.

            "I know."

He looked at me for a moment. "Then why did you?"

"Because you were doing it alone."

He gave a small nod, as if that answer required no further examination.

            "But you did."

            I dried my hands on my coat.

            "The frost will be harder tomorrow," I said.

            "It always is after a thaw."

            I looked toward the woodpile by the wall. It was low. "I could bring some wood next time."

            He did not answer immediately. He reached for his tea, took a sip, and set it down.

            "That would be a good thing," he said.

            I nodded. I did not say that I would come again. I had already said I would. It was settled.

            "I should go," I said, after a while.

            "You should." He did not move to see me out. "But you will come again."

            "Thursday?"

            "If you want."



When I left, the afternoon had darkened, and the lane was quiet. I walked home through the cold, and I thought about the clock on the mantelpiece, and the way he had said I was becoming predictable. I had not planned to come on Thursday. But I had come on Thursday, and now it was a thing he knew about me.

            That evening, I wrote in my notebook:

            *The window is fixed. Vale had already set out two cups before I arrived. He gave me bread and cheese without asking whether I was hungry. He said I was becoming predictable. He asked if I would come again, and I said I would.*

            I shut the book. I had not fixed anything that would last beyond the season, but I had fixed the window and the nail and the shutter, and I had spent an afternoon with an old man who did not ask me to explain myself.
