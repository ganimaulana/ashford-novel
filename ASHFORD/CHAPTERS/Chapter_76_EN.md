CHAPTER 76 — THE ACCOUNT

The autumn came to Ashford quietly. The leaves turned. The nights lengthened. The frost began to lie on the fields in the early mornings. And I kept the account.

I had written in the red book every day since the night I had understood what I was keeper of. I had written what I had learned, and what I had felt, and what I had decided. I had written the account of the place beneath.

I had not told anyone what I had learned. I had not told Crowe. I had not told Eleanor Shaw. I had not told the clerk. I had kept the account, and I had kept the medallions, and I had kept the gear, and I had kept the key to the tower. I had kept everything. And I had begun to understand that keeping was not the same as knowing.

I knew what I was keeping. I could not yet tell why. I could not yet tell what the place beneath was. I could not yet tell why it had been kept. I could not yet tell why I had been given the first sign, and why I had been led to the second, and why the bell had rung for me. I knew only that I was the keeper. And that the keeping was the thing.

---

On the first day of October, I went to the vestry. The clerk was there. I had not come to ask about the wall, or the signs, or the medallions. I had come to ask about the account.

"The records," I said. "The books you keep. The records of the town. What do they record?"

The clerk looked at me. "The records of the town," he said slowly. "The births. The deaths. The marriages. The rates. The accounts. The work of the wardens. The business of the vestry."

"And what do they record of the boundary? The wall? The signs?"

The clerk was silent. "Very little," he said. "The survey records the wall. The book of the wall records the stones. But the signs — the keeper's signs, the first signs — are not in the records. They are kept in the margins, in the hands of clerks who did not know what they were recording."

"And the bell?"

"The bell is in the record of the church," he said. "It is recorded as a bell. There is no record of when it rang, or why, or that it is the keeper's bell."

I stood in the vestry. The records of the town did not record what I was keeper of. They recorded the town. They recorded the births, and the deaths, and the rates, and the accounts. They did not record the keeping.

I understood, standing in the vestry, that the keeping had never been recorded. It had been kept in the margins. In the hands of clerks who did not know. In the red books. In the notebooks. In the accounts of keepers who had kept without understanding. And I understood that the account I was writing was part of the keeping. I was writing what the town did not record. I was writing the account of the place beneath.

And I understood that when I was gone, the account would be all that remained.

---

I went home. I sat at my desk. I opened the red book. I had been writing in it for weeks. I had written what I had learned. I had not written what I had decided. I had not written what I intended to do.

I sat at the desk, with the red book open, and I thought about the town. The town that did not record the keeping. The town that had forgotten the bell. The town that had forgotten Bellweather House. The town that had forgotten the keeper.

The town had an agreement — the agreement to see nothing, the agreement that had been written so that the town could forget. I had the agreement. The agreement signed by Aurelius Ashcroft. I had read it in the spring. It did not record the keeping. It recorded the forgetting. It recorded that the town agreed to see nothing, and to speak of nothing, and to forget the door and the sign and the bell.

I had been keeper for two months. I had been keeping the account. And I understood that the account was the opposite of the agreement. The agreement was the forgetting. The account was the remembering. And I was the keeper of the remembering.

I sat at the desk, in the dark, and I wrote:

The agreement is the forgetting. The town agreed to see nothing, and to speak of nothing, and to forget. The account is the remembering. I am writing what the town does not record. I am the keeper of the remembering. I do not know what I will do. But I know that I will not forget. I will keep the account. I will remember.

I closed the red book. I put it in the drawer, with the agreement, and the map, and my notebook. I did not put the medallions away. I left them on the desk.

And I sat in the dark, and I thought about the agreement, and the account, and the town, and the keeping. The town had agreed to forget. I had chosen to remember. That was the whole of my duty. I was the keeper of the place beneath. And the place beneath was remembered in the account. I would keep it. I would remember. And when the town forgot again, the account would remain.
