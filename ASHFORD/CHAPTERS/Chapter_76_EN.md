CHAPTER 76 — THE OTHERS

I went to the office on Thursday morning, because Tuesday had ended with the examination continuing and the constable had sent for me.

The lane was quiet. The common was empty. The office door was open. I had been there nine times before. But I had never been there as a man who was expected to sit among other men who were also being examined, and I had never been there as a man who was not answerable to the office at all.

I stepped inside.

The room was not the same. The chairs had been moved. There were six of them now, arranged in a rough circle near the desk. The clerk was there, sitting in one of the chairs, his ledger closed on the desk. Calloway was standing by the fireplace. Orme was there, sitting in the chair opposite the door.

There were three other men.

One was about my age, with the look of a man who had spent more time indoors than out. He wore a dark coat that was too clean for the lane. He had a notebook in his pocket and a pencil behind his ear. He looked at me when I came in and did not look away.

One was older, perhaps forty, with hands that looked as though they had done practical work. He wore a plain coat, the cuffs frayed. He had a hat in his hands and was turning it the way a man turns something when he is waiting for something to begin.

One was younger, perhaps twenty, with the eager look of a man who had heard good things about himself and believed them. He sat straight. He looked at the ceiling. He looked at the clerk. He looked at me. He did not look at Orme.

Calloway looked up. He set the paper down. He took off his hat and set it on the desk.

"You have done individual tests," he said.

"I have," I said.

"From this point, the office will examine you together," he said. "You will be given tasks that require more than one man. The clerk will record what each man does. The record will be the office's record. The conduct will be yours."

I did not say anything.

"The office does not explain the examination," Calloway said. "The office tells you what to do. You do it. That is the record."

He looked at the clerk. The clerk opened the ledger. He turned the page. He looked at us in turn.

"You will go to the room upstairs," he said. "You will wait. The constable will come."

We went. The clerk led us through the door, down the passage, up the stairs. The man with the notebook walked beside me. The older man went ahead. The younger man came last, his boots loud on the wood.

The clerk opened the door at the top of the stairs. The room was larger than the one I had been in before. The walls were plain. The floor was wooden. The window was high. There was a long table in the centre. There were chairs around it. There was a door at the far end, closed.

"The room is yours," the clerk said. "You may sit. You may talk among yourselves if you wish. You may not leave until the constable comes. The constable will tell you what to do."

He closed the door.

The man with the notebook sat at the table. He opened his notebook and wrote something in it. He did not offer to show us.

The older man sat. He turned his hat in his hands. He looked at the door at the far end. He did not say anything.

The younger man sat. He looked at the ceiling. He looked at the window. He looked at the door. He did not look at us.

I sat. The table was ordinary wood. The chairs were ordinary wood. The room was ordinary.

The man with the notebook looked at me.

"What did they ask you in the individual tests," he said.

"A room," I said. "Two statements. Four objects."

"Four objects," he said. "What were they."

"I do not know whether I should say."

He looked at me. He did not seem surprised. He wrote something in his notebook.

"The older man has not said anything since we came upstairs," he said.

"He has not," I said.

"Does he know something we do not."

"I do not know," I said.

The older man turned his hat. He looked at the door at the far end. He did not say anything.

The younger man leaned forward. He looked at the man with the notebook.

"What did they ask you," he said.

"The same as everyone," the man with the notebook said. "A room. Two statements. Four objects."

"Four objects," the younger man said. "What were they."

"I do not know whether I should say."

The younger man frowned. He sat back. He looked at the ceiling again.

I looked at the door at the far end. It was closed. It was wooden. It had a handle. It did not have a lock.

I looked at the window. It was high. The light came through it in a rectangle on the floor. The rectangle was still.

The man with the notebook leaned across the table. He lowered his voice.

"Do you think they are going to tell us what they are looking for," he said.

"I do not know," I said.

"Because if they are not," he said, "then we are all guessing."

He sat back. He wrote in his notebook. He did not show us what he wrote.

The younger man looked at the older man.

"What do you think," he said.

The older man turned his hat. He looked at the younger man for the first time.

"I think the constable will come when he is ready," he said. "He will tell us what to do. We will do it. That is all."

The younger man frowned. He looked at the ceiling again.

I looked at the door at the far end. The handle was brass. It was polished. It did not move.

The door opened. Calloway came in. He had his hat in his hands. He looked at each of us in turn. He did not seem surprised by anything he saw.

"You will wait," he said. "The constable will come. He will tell you what to do. You will not leave the room until it is finished. You will each do the work as the office requires."

He looked at me. He looked at the man with the notebook. He looked at the older man. He looked at the younger man.

"The office is not looking for friends," he said. "The office is not looking for rivals. The office is looking for what happens when men are in the same room and do not know what is expected of them."

He went out. He closed the door.

The man with the notebook looked at the door. The older man turned his hat. The younger man looked at the ceiling.

I did not know what the task would be. I did not know whether the man with the notebook had already decided what it would be. I did not know whether the older man had already decided he would do nothing. I did not know whether the younger man was eager because he was competent or because he was afraid of seeming incompetent.

But I knew we were in the room together, and the room was not going to explain itself.
