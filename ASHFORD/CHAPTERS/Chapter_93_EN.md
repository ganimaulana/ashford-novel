CHAPTER 93 — THE SECOND ROUND

The morning was cold and damp. I stood at the edge of the field with the other twenty-three candidates, waiting. The grey-coat man was not there. Instead, Calloway stood on the low steps of the main building, a sheet of paper in his hand. He did not look at us for a long moment.

“You have been assigned to groups of four,” he said. “The groups are listed here. When I read your name, step forward and find your group.”

He began to read.

I heard names I knew and names I did not. Ellison was placed with three men I had not spoken to. Peale was grouped with two candidates I barely recognized and one I had never seen. Hunter’s name was called with three men who looked as though they had worked together before—they moved to the side without speaking.

Then I heard my name: “Leon Ashcroft, with John Weaver, Thomas Merritt, and Bishop.”

I did not know Bishop. I looked for him and saw a man about my age, with a plain coat and a cap pulled low. He was not looking at anyone.

Weaver stepped toward me. Merritt walked over slowly, his face unreadable. Bishop followed last, his hands in his pockets.

“Four of us,” Weaver said.

“Four of us,” I said.

Calloway continued reading until all six groups were formed. Then he set the paper down.

“Each group will be given a task,” he said. “You will move a crate from one end of the field to the other. The marked route shows the required course. There are obstacles—ropes, low barriers, a patch of soft ground. You will receive a map and a list of numbered markers. The markers are not in order. You must determine the correct sequence and move the crate through each marker. The first five groups to complete the task will continue. The last group will be recorded as not passing and will leave the grounds.”

He paused.

“You may begin when the bell rings.”

He stepped back. A clerk handed each group a bundle of papers. Ours contained a map, a list of numbered markers, and a slip with a warning: “The soft ground will slow the crate.”

Weaver took the map. Merritt took the list. Bishop took the warning. I took nothing.

The bell rang.

Weaver spread the map on the ground. “The markers are here, here, and here,” he said, pointing. “But the numbers on the list are jumbled.”

Merritt read the list. “They are numbered one to eight, but they are not in order. We need to reorder them.”

Bishop said, “The warning says the soft ground will slow the crate. We need to decide whether to go around or through it.”

I looked at the map. The soft ground was marked with cross-hatching. There was a longer path around it. “We can go around,” I said. “It will take more time, but we won’t get stuck.”

Weaver looked at the crate. “Bishop, you’ll be pulling. Which route is better for you?”

Bishop considered. “The soft ground will be harder to pull through. The longer route is firmer but longer.”

Merritt said, “If we take the longer route, we need to make up time elsewhere.”

I looked at the map again. “The markers form a loop. If we reorder the markers correctly, we can save time on the turns.”

Weaver nodded. “We’ll take the longer route. Merritt, reorder the list. Bishop, you pull. I’ll guide. Leon, you handle the map and call the markers.”

We moved. Weaver handed me the map. I called out the next marker. Bishop pulled the cart. Merritt pushed from behind. Weaver guided.

At the first marker, we stopped. Merritt checked the list. “This is marker three. The next should be marker seven.”

We continued. The cart wobbled on the uneven ground. Bishop’s arms strained. Merritt’s boots slipped in the mud.

I saw Hunter’s group ahead. They had chosen the shorter path through the soft ground. They were moving faster but struggling with the mud.

We took the longer, firmer route. It cost us time, but we did not slow.

At the fifth marker, we encountered a low rope barrier. We had to lift the cart over it. It took all four of us.

Weaver said, “We are losing ground.”

Merritt said, “We are steady.”

Bishop said nothing.

I looked at the map again. There was a gap in the barrier, unmarked. I pointed to it. “We can go through here.”

We did. It saved us a few minutes.


The first problem appeared before we had gone twenty yards. The map showed the first marker beside a straight section of ground, but the field in front of us was cut by a shallow ditch that did not appear on the drawing. The crate's front wheel dropped into it and stopped.

Bishop swore quietly.

Merritt crouched beside the wheel. “We can lift it.”

“All four?” Weaver asked.

“All four.”

We lifted. The crate came free with a sucking sound from the wet earth. For several seconds we stood still, breathing hard.

“We're losing time,” Weaver said.

Merritt looked at the map. “The ditch isn't marked.”

“Then the map isn't complete,” I said.

Bishop looked at me. “You think that's deliberate?”

“I think we know the map is incomplete.”

That was enough. We stopped expecting the paper to answer every question.

At the second marker, we found two routes. One followed the chalk line exactly. The other cut across a patch of grass where several candidates had already passed. The second route was shorter, but the ground had begun to soften under the traffic.

Weaver looked at me. “Which one?”

I looked at the cart, then at Bishop's hands. His knuckles were already red from the first lift.

“The marked route.”

Merritt shook his head. “Longer.”

“Yes.”

He considered it, then nodded. “Fine.”

We moved on.

At the third marker, however, the marked route was blocked by another group. Their cart had tipped sideways, and there was no room to pass. The shorter grass route was now clear.

I looked at Weaver. He looked back. Neither of us spoke.

“Take the grass,” Bishop said.

This time I agreed.

The route was uneven, but we crossed it without getting stuck. We lost less time than we would have waiting for the other group. It was a small decision, but it felt different from the first round. I was not choosing the safe route because it was safe. I was choosing a risk because the alternative had become worse.

When we reached the final marker, Merritt looked at me. “You changed your mind.”

“The circumstances changed.”

He smiled for the first time. “Good answer.”

We finished fourth. It was not an impressive position, but the cart arrived upright, and none of us had been forced to stop.

We reached the final marker and crossed the line. Hunter’s group had finished first. Two other groups finished before us. We were fourth. One group was still struggling at the barrier.

The clerk recorded our time. Calloway did not look at us.

The last group finished well after us. They had taken the soft ground and overturned their cart. Their crate was half-buried in mud.

Calloway stepped forward.

“The first five groups are recorded as passing,” he said. “The last group is recorded as not passing. They will not continue.”

The four men in the last group stood still. One of them looked at the ground. The others did not look at anyone.

I watched them walk away. They did not speak.

I walked back to my room. The sun was rising, but the air was still cold.

I sat on the bed and thought about the task. We had not been the fastest. We had not been the strongest. We had chosen what we could manage—the longer, firmer route—and avoided the risk of getting stuck. Whether that was the right choice, I did not know. But we had made it together.

I looked out the window. The field was empty. Twenty of us remained.

For the first part of the route, I kept expecting the task to punish our choice. The other groups were ahead. Hunter's group was already visible near the far side of the field. Every time we passed another cart, Weaver looked toward it as though measuring the distance.

“We can still catch them,” he said.

“Not if we overturn the crate.”

He glanced at the crate. “You really believe that.”

“I believe we only need to finish.”

Merritt adjusted his grip. “Then stop looking at the others.”

I did. It made the work easier. The cart still felt heavy, but the four of us settled into a rhythm—Bishop pushing, Weaver pulling, Merritt watching the wheels, and me calling the turns when the route narrowed. None of us could have done the task comfortably alone.

At the final stretch, the group ahead of us stopped at the rope barrier. We gained the time we had lost earlier. I almost told Weaver to hurry. Instead I watched the ground. The grass was wet, and the cart was already leaning slightly.

“Slow,” I said.

We crossed without tipping.
