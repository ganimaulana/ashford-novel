CHAPTER 49 — THE SUMMONS

A week passed after the burial before Crowe came to me again.

I spent most of it at the mill. Not at the mill-yard gate, where the town might see me and wonder, but beyond it, where the wall turned and the stones stood in their line and no one had any reason to walk. I went in the early mornings, before the lamplighter was done, and in the evenings after the streets emptied. I told no one where I went. I kept the account the way I had decided to keep it — in my own hand, in my own book, in my own room.

The fourth stone had not changed.

I had expected it to. I had expected the hairline mark to widen, to deepen, to become something I could no longer mistake for the ordinary weather of old stone. It had not. It sat in the wall as it had sat for God knew how many years, a thin grey line running the width of the stone, faint as a thread, and I knelt beside it each morning and put my finger to it and felt nothing.

That, more than anything, unsettled me.

A crack that does not grow is not a crack. It is a mark. And marks — I had learned — were never accidental in Ashford. The circle and the short line were on the stones. The circle and the short line were on the door. The circle and the short line were on the gear I carried in my coat, warm only with the warmth of my own body, cold the moment I set it down.

I had begun to think of the mark as a signature.

Not a warning. Not a symbol of what was locked away. A signature — someone's way of signing a thing they had made, so that anyone who came after would know whose work it was.

I did not know whose.

That was the trouble with signatures. They told you the work was made. They did not tell you the maker's reasons.

I carried that question with me through the week. I carried it to the mill in the mornings, and to my rooms in the evenings, and I carried it to the boundary stones, where I knelt and read the marks and found no answer.

And then, on the eighth morning, I found the ground disturbed again.

---

Not the mill mechanism. I had gone there first, as I always did, and knelt where the soft ground held the record of every visitor. My own prints were there, deep from the repeated kneeling. The narrow heel was there too — unchanged, pointing away from the door, back toward the town. Whoever had left that print had not returned, or had returned and had taken care not to leave another.

Then I had walked to the stones.

And there, at the fourth stone, in the soft earth at its base — where I had knelt the day before — there was a new mark.

Not a print. A scratch.

A line drawn in the earth, clean and deliberate, no more than the length of my hand. It ran beside the stone, parallel to its base, and at its end the earth had been scored three times, short strokes, close together.

Three strokes.

I stared at it for a long time.

The mark on the gear was a circle and a short line. The mark on the door was a circle and a short line. The mark in the earth was a line and three strokes.

Not the same. Not the same at all. And yet.

I knelt and put my fingers to the scored earth. It was fresh — an hour old, perhaps less. The earth had not dried. Whoever had made it had done so since the rain, and the rain had stopped before first light.

I looked at the mill. I looked at the town. There was no one.

The sun was not yet over the rooftops. The lane to the mill-yard was empty. The wall threw a long shadow across the stones, and in that shadow the scored line looked black, deliberate, patient.

Someone had come to the fourth stone while I was not there. Someone had drawn a line and three strokes in the earth beside it. Someone had done it deliberately, and left it for me to find. Or had not known I would find it.

I did not touch the mark again. I stood, brushed the earth from my hands, and walked back toward town with the image of it in my mind — a line, and three strokes, and a question I could not yet ask aloud.

---

Crowe was waiting for me at my rooms.

Not at the door. Inside.

He sat in the chair by the window, in the thin morning light, with his hat on his knee, and he looked up when I entered as if he had been there some time and was in no hurry to say so.

"You let yourself in," I said.

"I did."

"The door was locked."

"It was." He turned his hat in his hands. "You keep the agreement in the drawer," he said. "Beneath the parish account."

I stopped in the middle of the room. I did not move toward the desk. I did not look at it. I looked at Crowe.

"Who told you that?"

"No one told me." He set the hat down. "It is where I would keep it."

"Then you did not look."

"I did not need to look." He met my eyes. "I came to tell you something, and I will tell it, and then I will go. What you choose to do with it is yours."

I waited.

"The Order has taken note of the old constable's death," he said. "And of you."

"Taken note."

"They have been watching the house on Bellweather Lane since before you came to it. They watched Vale. Now they watch you. That is not a threat — it is what they do. It is what we do. We watch the keeper. It is in the nature of the thing."

I thought of the open window in Vale's house. I thought of the sash raised exactly two inches, and of Crowe standing in that room the evening Vale died, and of how I had not watched him every moment.

"Then you know," I said, "who has been going to the mill."

Crowe was still.

"Someone has been going to the mill," he said slowly. "Someone has been kneeling at the stones. Someone has been standing at the door where a keeper stands." He shook his head. "I thought it was you."

"It is not you," I said. "You are telling me it is not you."

"It is not me."

We looked at each other across the small room.

"You want the door opened," I said. "You said so, the night he died."

"I want to know what it is for," Crowe said. "There is a difference between wanting to open a door and wanting to know what the door is for. The Order forgets the difference. I do not."

"Then who draws lines in the earth at the fourth stone?"

Crowe frowned.

"Lines in the earth?"

"Three strokes. Beside the stone. Fresh this morning."

He was silent a moment. When he spoke, his voice was lower.

"Show me."

---

I did not show him.

Not then.

It was the first time I had refused him anything, and I saw him take note of it, the way a man takes note of a changed step on a stair he walks every day.

"You have decided not to trust me," he said.

"I have decided not to trust anyone," I said. "You told me the Order watches the keeper. Then the Order knows things it does not tell me. You came here to tell me they have taken note. You did not come to tell me what they have decided. I will not hand you a mark in the earth when I do not know what it is for."

Crowe stood. For a long moment he did not speak.

"You are right to be careful," he said at last. "But there is a cost to being careful alone. Vale paid it for fifty years. He trusted no one, and no one trusted him, and the town forgot he had ever been anything but an old man who kept to himself. When he died, there was no one left who knew what he had known. That is what your care will buy you, if you let it."

"Is that a warning?"

"It is a fact." He picked up his hat. "I did not come to warn you. I came because there are those in the Order who believe the new keeper should be tested. They have asked me to bring you before them."

"Tested how?"

"I do not know. That is the truth. There is a meeting, tomorrow night, in the room above the old brewery. They want to see you. They want to hear from you what you have seen of the boundary. Some of them believe the boundary is failing and that the Agreement is a lie written by frightened men. Some believe it is holy and must not be touched. And some — " he stopped. "Some believe you are not the keeper at all. That Vale meant to name someone else, and that you took what was not offered."

I felt the cold settle in my chest.

"Who believes that?"

"I do not know," Crowe said. "That is also the truth." He moved toward the door. "I will not tell you what to say. But I will tell you this, because it is true and because you will learn it sooner or later: they will not all want the same thing from you, and whatever you answer, some of them will have decided against you before you opened your mouth."

He paused at the threshold.

"The mark in the earth," he said. "Three strokes. Do not speak of it at the meeting."

"Why not?"

"Because if it is what I think it is, there are men in that room who have been waiting a long time to hear that the keeper has seen it. And they will not be glad that you have seen it first."

He went out.

I stood in the room a long time after the door closed, looking at the chair where he had sat, and at the drawer where the agreement lay, and at the coat on the hook where the gear rested in its pocket.

Three strokes.

I had not asked Crowe what he thought the mark was. I had not asked him why he had come to warn me against the men he served. I had asked him nothing, and he had told me three things I could not verify, and one thing I already knew — that he wanted the door opened, and that he drew a line between wanting to open it and wanting to know what it was for.

I did not know whether to believe the line.

---

I went to the mill that night.

Not to the stones — I left the fourth stone untouched, the mark in the earth unread, as Crowe had advised and as my own distrust advised as well. I went to the mechanism, to the door, where the ground held its record.

The door was as I had left it. Closed. Sealed. The depression empty of the gear that would not fit it.

But I did not kneel at the mechanism as I had done before.

I stood at the door, and I put my hand flat against it, the way Vale had shown me in the last weeks of his life, and I stood that way for a long time, listening to nothing.

The door was not warm. It had never been warm. It was stone and wood and old iron, fitted so precisely that a man could not slide a knife between the frame and the seal, and behind it there was nothing I could hear and nothing I could feel and nothing at all to tell me what it held.

I had opened it an inch, once. I had heard something. I had never told anyone what I heard.

I told no one now.

I took my hand away. There was a line in the dust at the base of the door where my fingers had rested.

And beside it, faint, half-erased, almost invisible in the dark —

a line, and three strokes.

Scratched into the stone itself, worn almost smooth by years, by weather, by the passing of hands that no longer remembered whose.

I did not touch it. I looked at it until the dark made it indistinguishable from the stone, and then I walked back through the town with the image of it in my mind — a line and three strokes on the door, worn thin by time.

The mark on the gear was a circle and a short line. The mark on the door was a circle and a short line — above the seal. And below the seal, hidden where the dust gathered, worn almost to nothing: a line, and three strokes.

Two marks. Two signatures.

I had begun to think there was only one. I had been wrong.

---

The meeting was in the room above the old brewery, as Crowe had said.

I came late, as I had decided to come — not so late that they would think I was afraid, but late enough that they would know I had considered whether to come at all. The stairs were narrow and unlit, and the room at the top was lit by a single lamp set in the middle of a long table, and around the table sat seven men and one woman, and I did not know all their names.

Crowe was there, at the end of the table.

Graves was there — Nathaniel Graves, the Purist, who had spoken to me once before, in the weeks after the stone had been moved, and who had not liked what I had said then and did not like me now.

The woman I did not know sat across from Graves, with a ledger closed in front of her, and she looked at me the way Eleanor Shaw had looked at me in the spring — as if I were a page she had read before and was waiting to see whether I had changed.

I stood at the head of the table. No one offered me a chair.

"You have come," Graves said.

"I have come."

"You have not come before. Vale never came. He sent word, when he sent anything. He did not trust the room."

"Vale is dead," I said. "I am here in his place. If you want a keeper who sends word instead of coming, you should find another man."

There was a silence. The woman with the ledger closed it.

"We have heard," she said, "that the boundary is failing. That stones have moved. That the keeper's house has stood empty at the end of the lane for years, and that the thing beneath Ashford is waking."

"Who told you that?"

"It is said."

"Then you know more than I do," I said. "I am the keeper. I have walked the boundary. The stones have not moved since the eastern marker was set right in the spring. The door is sealed. The house at the end of the lane is not empty — it is the house where Vale lived and died, and it stands empty now only because he is buried." I looked around the table. "If you have heard otherwise, you have heard it from someone who was not at the boundary."

Graves leaned forward.

"And the Agreement. You carry it. You keep it in a drawer, beneath the parish account. What does it tell you of the door?"

"It tells me the door was made to be closed," I said. "It tells me the Agreement was signed by a man who believed closing it was necessary. It does not tell me what is behind it."

"And you will keep it closed?"

"I will."

"Even if the Order tells you otherwise?"

"The Order does not sign the Agreement," I said. "Aurelius Ashcroft signed it. He is dead. The town that watches the door is dead. I am the keeper. The door stays closed until I understand it."

Graves was silent. I could not tell whether I had answered what he wanted or what he feared.

The woman with the ledger spoke again.

"There is a mark," she said. "A mark that some of us believe appears on the boundary when the keeper is tested. A circle and a line. Have you seen it?"

"I have seen it," I said. "On the stones. On the door. On the gear."

"On the gear," she repeated. She looked at Crowe. "You did not tell us the gear was given."

"It was given," I said. "Vale gave it to me before he died. It is mine now."

"There was no gear," Graves said flatly, "before this spring."

"There was. You did not know of it."

There was a long silence.

I did not know what I had walked into. I had come expecting to be tested about the boundary, about the Agreement, about my fitness as keeper. They were asking me about the gear. They were asking me about a mark they had not been told was on it. And they were looking at Crowe as if he had failed to tell them something they had expected him to tell.

I understood, in the moment of silence, that I had made a mistake. I had told them about the mark. I had told them what I had decided to tell no one. And I did not know which of the eight faces around the table had been waiting for exactly that.

---

The woman stood.

"The meeting is over," she said. "The keeper has spoken. We will speak again when there is something to decide."

They rose. They left in twos and threes, without looking at me, without farewell, the way men leave a place when they have gotten what they wanted and are not yet ready to say so.

Graves paused at the top of the stairs.

"You have chosen," he said quietly, "to keep the door closed."

"I have."

"And you have told the room you carry the gear."

"I have."

He looked at me a long moment.

"There is a cost to carrying it," he said. "Vale carried it for fifty years and told no one. You have carried it for a week and told the room. You are not Vale. Remember that, when you wonder why the Order watches you."

He went down.

Only Crowe remained, at the end of the long table, the lamp between us.

"You told them," he said.

"They asked."

"They always ask. They have asked every keeper since there were keepers. Vale never told them." He shook his head slowly. "I did not think you would tell them."

"Then you came to the meeting expecting me to lie."

"I came to the meeting expecting you to choose what to say." He picked up his hat. "You have chosen. Now the Order knows you carry the gear, and the Order will ask what it is for, and the Order will not all ask for the same reason."

"Is that what the mark in the earth was for?" I said. "To make me walk into this room and tell them what I carry?"

Crowe was very still.

"There is a mark in the earth," he said, "that I did not make, at a stone I have not visited, drawn with three strokes that I did not draw, and you are asking me whether it was a trap."

"Was it?"

"No." He looked at me. "But if there is such a mark, and I did not make it, and you did not make it — then there is a third hand in this, and it is not in this room, and it has been waiting longer than any of us have been alive."

He went out.

I stood alone in the room above the brewery, the lamp burning down, the chairs empty, and I thought of the worn line and three strokes on the door beneath the seal, hidden where the dust gathered.

Two signatures. One I had been shown. One I had found by accident. I did not know which was older. I did not know which was the true keeper's mark.

I only knew that I had walked into that room thinking the Order was one thing, and I had come out of it understanding that it was eight things, and that they did not all want the same door, and that the mark in the earth had been drawn by a hand that belonged to none of them.

---

I did not sleep that night.

I sat at my desk in the dark, the gear before me, the agreement in the drawer beneath the parish account, and I opened my notebook and wrote:

Fourth stone. Fresh mark in the earth beside it. A line and three strokes. Made since the rain. Not mine. Not Crowe's — he denies it, and I cannot prove him a liar, which is worse.

Door: second mark found beneath the seal, worn almost smooth by years. Circle and line above. Line and three strokes below.

The Order met tonight. They did not all want the same thing. Some want the door open. Some want it sealed forever. Some want to test me. None of them asked what I would find behind the door. None of them asked what the mark in the earth was for.

I told them I carry the gear. I should not have.

I do not know who drew the line and three strokes. I do not know whether it is a signature, a warning, or a key. But I know it is not the mark I carry. And I know that whatever it is, it was left for me to find — on the fourth stone, and on the door, hidden where the keeper alone would look.

Two marks. I have been keeper for a week. I have been watching the wrong one.

---

I closed the notebook. I put it with the agreement, in the drawer, beneath the parish account.

Then I took the gear from the desk and put it back in my coat, where I had carried it every day since Vale died.

Whatever was behind the door would wait. But whoever had drawn the line and three strokes — on the stone, and on the door, and in the earth while I slept — that one had not waited for me. That one had come to the fourth stone while I was not there. That one had drawn the mark, and left it, and gone.

And I had walked into the room above the brewery and told eight strangers I carried the gear, and I had come out understanding that I did not know a single one of them, and that the one hand I could not place was the only hand that mattered.

The door was still closed. The Agreement was still in the drawer. The town was still asleep.

But somewhere in Ashford, there was a hand that drew lines in the earth beside the keeper's stones, in the hours before first light, and it had not drawn them for the Order. It had drawn them for me.

And I did not know whether that was a message, or a test, or the beginning of a question I would spend the rest of the book learning to answer.
