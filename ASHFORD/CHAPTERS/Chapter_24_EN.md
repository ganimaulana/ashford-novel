# Chapter 24 — The Saturday Round and the Empty House

The Friday lamp burned at nine. I saw it from my window, steady at the end of the lane, and then it moved—carried along the rooms of the house in the old way, room to room, until it reached the upper window and went out. No sound came. I set it down in my book and wrote nothing else.

I had kept the reading nine nights. I did not know what it meant. I wrote it anyway.

---

The Saturday came in with the snow gone off under a warm rain. The gutters ran brown, and the cobbles shone darkly under a grey sky. The round was set for ten, and I did my part of the day as a man does the day before his turn. I went to the pump, I bought bread, I spoke to no one. The town was quiet, and I was quiet with it.

I slept between the two nines. I was up at the quarter to ten.

I took my lantern and went out at my own door. The lane was wet and dark, and the water lay in pools along the worn stones. I went the round as it was set for me: from my door to the mill-yard gate corner, and back. The lantern cast a yellow light that moved with me, and my shadow stretched and shrank against the wet walls as I walked. The air was still and cold, and the only sound was the drip of water from the eaves and the soft crunch of my boots on the frost-covered cobbles.

At nine, I looked for the lamp at the end of the lane. It did not come.

I waited. The lane was dark. The house stood black against the sky. I wrote nothing. I went on.

The round went as rounds go. The church stood dark. The gate stood shut at the corner, a heavy iron gate set into a stone arch. I passed it at the quarter past ten. Shut. I passed it at the quarter to eleven. Shut.

At eleven, as I came round the third time, the gate stood open.

The padlock hung on the hasp. The hasp was drawn.

I stood a moment. I did not enter. A round is for watching, not entering. I held the lantern at the opening and saw only the black, wet yard, empty and still. The well at the centre stood like a dark eye. The walls were wet, and the cobbles were slick, and there was no sound but the drip of water from the eaves. I looked for footprints, for disturbed water, for any sign that someone had passed through recently. I saw nothing.

I shut the gate. I replaced the padlock. I continued my round, writing in my book:

*Gate open at eleven; padlock unhung; shut again; no person seen; no light in the yard. Third round.*

A padlock does not usually leave its hasp by itself. Someone had handled it, unless I had missed something. I could not tell whether the person had gone in or out, and I kept that distinction in my notes.

The round came home at the quarter to twelve. I stood at my own door with the lantern in my hand and looked at the end of the lane. The house stood there in the wet dark, its windows black, its rosemary dark. No light in it. The light had been carried away late, at ten, after the nine had failed.

I went in. I set the lantern down. I stood in the dark hallway a moment, listening. Then I sat at the table.

Three things had happened: the lamp failed at nine, the light appeared later, and the gate stood open at eleven. They might have been connected. They might simply have shared a night. I had learned not to promote coincidence into pattern too quickly. I thought about the gate—who would open it and not close it? The yard man said it was open at six, but I had shut it at eleven. That meant someone had opened it again after I left, or it had never been fully closed. I could not tell which. The padlock was on the hasp when I found it, but it was hanging loose. That meant someone had removed it and not replaced it properly. I did not know whether that was carelessness or intention.

At a little past one, as I was on the point of the candle, the sound came.

Once. A pause. Twice. Low and full and at the ground, moving along the lane toward the corner, along the water and the stone.

It came on the lamp's night. It came after the gate. It came at the hour of the house's own deep quiet.

I stood at the window with the candle in my hand. I looked out at the lane. I saw nothing. I heard nothing more.

I set the candle down and wrote:

*The bell came at one, on the lamp's night, after the gate opened at eleven. I do not know what it means. The round kept the lane and did not keep the gate. The gate is kept for something that comes and goes. I do not know whether it came or went tonight.*

---

The Sunday came in with a clear frost after the rain. The town walked to church on the hard road, the stones white with rime and the air sharp and clean. I walked the middle of the lane as the way is. I came by the end house, took in the rosemary, and gave it out again. I went on to the church with the town, and did the not-seeing as the town does it.

The church was cold. The pews were full. The verger stood at the back with his hands folded. He caught my eye once, and I saw he had something to say.

The service went through as services go. I listened to the words and did not hear them. My mind was still at the gate and the bell and the lamp. The hymns rose and fell, and the congregation knelt and stood, and I moved with them, but my thoughts were elsewhere.

At the door going out, the verger gave me my hat and stood so that the giving took the length of a word. His fingers brushed the brim, and I felt the weight of his hesitation. He had been waiting for this moment since the service began.

"The gate, sir," he said, low. "The yard man found it shut this morning, with the padlock to it. He says it was open when he came to the yard at six, and shut again by the time he came back with his key. He has told the constable. The constable has noted it."

"Noted," I said.

"Noted," said the verger. "And I have told you, sir, because you keep the round. The yard man will say it shut itself. The constable will say the wind. The bench will not say. And I have told you what I saw. That is the four tellings of it. You may keep them as you keep the other things."

He paused. His eyes held mine a moment.

"You were there, sir. You saw what you saw. That is the only telling that counts."

---

I thanked him and went home by the middle of the lane. I understood that the gate had been told four ways before the noon of Sunday, and that the telling of a thing in a town is the keeping of it. The town would keep the gate four ways. It would not keep it one way. The one way was in my book, and nowhere else in the town.

I sat at my desk and wrote the entry again, cleanly. I wrote the hour, the gate, the padlock, the bell at one. I wrote that the pattern I had been keeping had not held. I wrote that the town had four accounts of the gate, and that none of them was the whole truth.

I set the book aside and looked out at the lane. The frost was settling. The end house stood dark, its windows empty and its door closed. I thought of the lamp that had failed at nine, the light carried at ten, the gate open at eleven, the bell at one.

I did not know whether the hand that opened the gate belonged to a man or to something else. I did not know whether I was meant to find out.

That evening, the lamp came up at the end of the lane at nine. Steady. Its old height. I watched it from my window. I wrote nothing.

The Greyhound had been a case. This was only an observation I could not yet explain.

I went to bed with that, and the cold at the pane, and the knowledge that the town kept its own accounts, and that mine was not the same as theirs.
