CHAPTER 92 — THE REMAINING

The dining hall was quieter that evening. Seven places were empty, and the men who remained sat farther apart than they had the night before. I took a seat near the end of a long table, where I could see the room without being seen too clearly. Weaver sat down across from me without asking.

“You helped me,” he said. “I remember.”

“I remember too,” I said.

“It cost you places.”

“It cost me a few.”

He was silent for a moment, turning the bread in his hands. Then he said, “I would not have done it.”

I did not know what to say to that. I looked at the room. Ellison was sitting with a man I did not know, speaking in low tones. Merritt was alone at the far end, his back to the wall. Peale was near the door, his head tilted as though he were listening to something no one else could hear. Hunter was not there.

“He eats alone,” Weaver said, following my gaze. “He always eats alone.”

“He is difficult to read.”

“He is difficult to trust.”

Weaver said it without bitterness. It was simply an observation. I did not disagree.

The meal ended. I walked back to my room, but I did not feel like sleeping. I stood at the window and looked out at the field. The posts and ropes were still there, dark against the grass. The lamp in the far window was lit again. Someone was watching, as before.

I heard footsteps in the corridor and opened my door. Ellison was passing. He stopped when he saw me.

“You are still awake,” he said.

“I am still awake.”

“You helped Weaver today.”

“I did.”

He looked at me for a moment. “I would not have done it either,” he said. “But I am not sure that makes me right.”

He walked on. I closed the door.

The next morning, the remaining candidates gathered in the courtyard. The man in the grey coat was not there. Calloway was not there. Only the clerk stood on the steps, his ledger open.

“You will have a rest day,” he said. “There will be no examination. You may walk the grounds, speak with one another, or remain in your rooms. You will be told when the next round begins.”

He turned and went inside.

The candidates dispersed. Some walked toward the field. Others stood in groups, talking. I saw Weaver standing near the well, and I walked over to him.

“A rest day,” I said.

“Perhaps they want to see what we do when we are not being tested,” he said.

“You think so?”

“I do not know. I am guessing.”

I looked at the courtyard. Some men were already forming clusters. Others stood alone. Ellison was speaking with a candidate I did not know. Merritt was walking toward the gate. Peale was standing near the dining hall, his hands in his pockets.

Hunter was standing near the wall, his arms crossed. He was not looking at anyone. He was looking at the field.

I walked over to him.

“You are not talking to anyone,” I said.

He did not look at me. “I am talking to you.”

“That is not the same.”

“No,” he said. “It is not.”

He turned his head slightly. “You helped Weaver yesterday. You lost places.”

“I know.”

“Would you do it again?”

I thought about it. “I do not know.”

He looked at me then, directly, for the first time. “We’ll see.”

He turned and walked away.

I stood there for a moment, watching him go. I did not know whether he had tested me or simply observed me. I did not know whether he approved or disapproved. I only knew that he had asked a question I had not expected.


By midday the grounds had settled into an uneasy quiet. Without instructions, the candidates seemed unsure what to do with their hands. A few tried to practise running along the field. Others slept. One man spent an hour copying the layout of the grounds into a notebook. The clerk ignored all of us.

I went to the well and found Weaver there. He had washed the mud from his boots but not from the hem of his trousers.

“Did you sleep?” he asked.

“Not much.”

“Neither did I.”

He drew water and drank. “I keep thinking about the seven who left.”

“So do I.”

“Do you think they were worse than us?”

I considered the question. “I don't know them well enough to say.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“I know.”

He smiled faintly. “You answer questions as though they are reports.”

“Perhaps reports are easier.”

“Perhaps.”

We walked along the wall after that. He pointed out small groups forming and separating. One man had attached himself to Ellison. Another had tried to speak with Merritt and had been left standing alone after Merritt walked away. Peale had spoken to three candidates and then returned to his room without eating.

“You see all that?” Weaver asked.

“I see people moving.”

“That's the same thing.”

“No. It isn't.”

He looked at me. “You really don't like saying what you think.”

“I say what I know.”

“And the rest?”

“I keep separate.”

He nodded, but I could tell he did not entirely understand. I was not sure I did either.

In the afternoon, the clerk finally appeared and asked for volunteers to carry firewood from the storehouse to the kitchen. Several men immediately stepped forward. Others waited to see whether it counted as examination work. The clerk said nothing about it being a test. That was enough to make some of them suspicious.

I carried two armfuls. Weaver carried one. Hunter did not volunteer. He passed us once on the path and glanced at the wood, then continued walking.

Later, I saw him sitting alone beneath the wall, sharpening a pencil with a small knife. I almost asked what he was writing. I did not.

The day taught me little about the next task. It taught me something about the men who would face it with me. That was perhaps more useful.

The rest day passed slowly. I walked the grounds, watching the other candidates. I saw three men move their chairs to sit together at the next meal. I saw another approach a group and leave again, not invited. I heard someone ask who had chosen the red gate, and the answer was met with silence. Weaver noticed that people were beginning to choose their seats based on who they trusted. Merritt ate alone despite empty places nearby. Peale shifted tables when a man began to discuss the results of the first round.

I did none of those things. I simply observed.

That evening, I found Weaver sitting on the steps of the main building.

“You are not forming alliances,” he said.

“Neither are you.”

“I am not good at it,” he said. “I am better at following than leading.”

“I am not sure what I am better at.”

He laughed, a short, quiet sound. “That is honest.”

“Honesty is not the same as certainty.”

“No,” he said. “It is not.”

I sat down beside him. The sun was low, the shadows long. I looked at the field, empty now of ropes and posts. I thought about the man with the notebook, his careful writing, his methodical approach. I had not seen him since he left. I wondered where he was now, whether he had already been sent to another office or was walking home.

“Do you think about the ones who left?” I said.

Weaver was silent for a moment. “I think about how I am still here and they are not. I do not know what I did differently.”

“You chose the green gate.”

“I chose it because I could not decide quickly. It was luck.”

“It was not luck,” I said. “You waited. You chose a route that worked.”

He looked at me. “And you chose to help me. That was not luck either.”

We sat in silence. The lamp in the far window was lit again. Someone was still watching.

The next morning, a note came under the door. It said only: The field. Ten o’clock.

I put on my coat and went.

The field was empty. The posts and ropes were gone. The markings on the ground had been erased. Only the grass and the dirt remained.

The man in the grey coat was standing at the edge of the field. He looked at us as we gathered.

“The next round will begin tomorrow,” he said. “You will be working in groups.”

He turned and walked away.

I stood in the field, looking at the empty space where the ropes had been. The candidates around me were already shifting, some drawing closer to one another, others standing apart. I did not know who I would work with. I did not know whether I would be chosen or left aside.

I walked back to my room. The sun was high. The field was empty.

I sat on the bed and looked out the window. Tomorrow, they would tell us who we were to work with. I was less certain that they would tell us why.

I waited.
