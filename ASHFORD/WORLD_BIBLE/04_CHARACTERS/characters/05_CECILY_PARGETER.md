## Why this role is historically plausible

She is not a female constable. No such thing exists and none is invented here.

In this period women were routinely used, informally and per occasion, to **search and examine women** — a female suspect could not be searched by men, a female corpse was laid out by women, a lying-in was closed to men entirely, and midwives and searchers gave evidence at inquests as a matter of course. The parish paid such women a few shillings and forgot them.

Cecily's position is that, made regular by one constable's convenience: **she goes into the rooms Leon and Owen may not enter.** Women's chambers. The women's side of the poor-house. A laying-out. A birth. A room where a wife will not speak in front of any man.

This is not a modernisation. It is the one genuine field function in 1817 Alderwick that only a woman can perform, and it is why she is on the team.

## Personality

Exact. Quiet. Frightened a good deal of the time and present anyway, which is a different and better thing than courage.

She was raised behind a counter where the difference between two grains and three grains was the difference between a medicine and a coroner's inquest, and she has never recovered from learning that. She says *probably*. She says *I could not tell*. She signs everything she writes, in a small upright hand, because a thing unsigned was never said.

- **Dominant traits:** precise, scrupulous, physically timid, quietly stubborn
- **Virtues:** honest past the point of usefulness; will not be argued into certainty; kind to the dead
- **Flaws:** over-qualifies; slow to commit; assumes she will not be believed and therefore sometimes does not try
- **Worldview:** the body does not lie, but it does not explain itself either. People lie constantly and mostly by accident.
- **Habit:** touches the back of her own wrist when thinking. Washes her hands more than is necessary.
- **Speech:** careful, complete sentences with the doubt built in. Never raises her voice. Repeats a question rather than answer one she has not understood.
- **Under pressure:** goes very quiet and very slow, which reads as fear and is actually method. She does not run. She has never once run.

## Strengths

- **Reads a body**: can date a bruise within a day or two, tell a fall from a blow, a blow from a strap, a strangling from a hanging, a burn from a scald, the marks of restraint from the marks of struggle
- Knows what was in a cup, a bottle, a poultice, a caudle, a paper of powder — an apothecary's daughter's education, better than the surgeon's on that one narrow point
- Access no man on the team has, and the trust that goes with it: women tell her things because she is the one who came in with clean hands and did not ask the husband first
- Writes a clear, dated, signed, defensible statement of what she found — the only member of the core three who reliably produces a document that survives contact with an inquest
- Notices what has been *moved* in a sick-room: which cup, which cloth, which chair, who has tidied

## Weaknesses

- Every finding arrives wrapped in its proper doubt, in a room where a decision is being made now. Orme hears cowardice. It is not cowardice; it is worse than useless anyway.
- Physically incapable in a confrontation and entirely aware of it
- Her standing is nil: unsworn, female, twenty-four, paid by the job. **Anyone may dismiss her and most do.**
- Assumes disbelief in advance and occasionally therefore under-reports, which is her one genuine professional failure and it costs someone
- Cannot separate what she has found from what it will do to the family in the next room. She has never learned to, and she is not sure she should.

## Skills

Examination of the living and the dead · pharmacy and dose · dating injuries · statement-writing · reading a sick-room · midwifery-adjacent knowledge (observed, not practised) · Latin enough for a prescription and no more · a steady hand

Cannot: fight, run far, ride, force a door, lie convincingly, or make anyone listen.

## Combat competence

**None.** She cannot fight and must never learn to. Her survival in a bad room depends entirely on Owen being in it and on Leon having predicted it. That dependence is the team, stated physically.

## Relationships

**Leon** — the professional marriage of the arc.
She is the only person who understands what *"What I saw. What I was told. What I concluded. Keep them apart"* is actually for, because she has been doing it since she was fourteen without a name for it. She is also the only person who will tell him, flatly, that his conclusion is not supported by his evidence — and be right.
And she is the person he is most tempted to misuse. **If they ever become anything more, it must cost them both professionally**, because they are the two people whose signatures corroborate each other, and that corroboration is worthless the instant anyone suspects there is feeling in it. Denby will be the one who says so, and he will be right.
**Not a romance by default. Do not make it one casually.**

**Owen Trant** — he believes her instantly, completely, and uselessly, since his belief carries no weight anywhere. He is protective in a way she finds patronising and occasionally needs. They are the two ends of one evidentiary problem: he knows and won't sign; she signs and isn't believed.

**Walter Denby** — the hardest and most valuable relationship she has. He is the surgeon; she is the apothecary's daughter who is better than he is on doses and worse on everything else. He does not condescend and does not concede. He will not co-sign a word he cannot defend, including hers. **Late in the arc he signs one of her findings, with three qualifications, and it is the most important sentence in her story.**

**Reuben Orme** — reads her honesty as timidity and her caution as obstruction. He is not cruel about it. He simply has a family waiting for an answer and she keeps not giving him one.

**James Calloway** — pays her, does not think about her, and would be startled to learn she is essential.

**Cuthbert Ingle** — treats her with old-fashioned courtesy and files her statements accurately. She is the only member of the team who has no complaint about him, which is faintly ominous.

**Eleanor Shaw** — eventual, wary, and important: two women holding functions no institution will formalise. **Do not make them instant allies.** If they ever compare notes it should be a late, dangerous, and mutually costly scene.

**Thomas Vale** — **never met him**, but she laid out a body he had asked questions about, years before, and remembers being told by her father that the old constable at the end of the lane had once asked the right question and then not written the answer down. One line. Do not expand it into a connection.

**The Order of Severence** — does not know it exists.

## Relationship to E.R.C.

She is the framework's best practitioner and its permanent victim. Her written findings are exactly what the standing ladder was designed for. Her sensations are exactly what it cannot process. **She is where the system's honesty and its cruelty are the same feature.**

## Knowledge level

- **Knows:** what she has examined; the town's illnesses and its lies about them; that Leon is retained for matters that do not fit
- **Suspects:** that whatever she feels in doorways is not a nervous complaint, and has never said so twice to the same person
- **Does NOT know:** the Agreement · the door · the Gear · the Order · the keeping · the count · that Tokens exist

## Secrets

1. She has felt the cold since she was a child and has told exactly two people: her father, once, who told her not to; and — eventually — Leon.
2. She kept her mother's second child's laying-out record. She wrote it herself, at nine, in a child's hand, because no one else would, and she has never shown it to anyone. It is why she signs everything.

## Character arc

**Start:** the one they send in when the room has a woman in it. Competent, unbelieved, and expecting nothing else.
**Middle:** reports the cold, is disbelieved, is proved right by material evidence Leon finds — and discovers that being proved right does not confer standing. The second time she reports it she is disbelieved again.
**Late:** stops asking to be believed and starts building records that will be believed *after* she is gone, which is Vale's method arrived at independently and from the other end. Denby's one countersigned line.
**Unresolved by design:** whether the cold is anything at all.

## Long-term potential

- The keeper of a parallel record — a body of examinations nobody read at the time, which becomes evidence decades later. Structurally the same instrument as Vale's second copy and Leon's red book.
- The person who can be sent where nobody else can go, therefore the person who can be sent somewhere fatal
- A candidate, much later, for Leon's secret contacts — **but after Shaw, never before, and only if she has stopped expecting institutions to help her**
