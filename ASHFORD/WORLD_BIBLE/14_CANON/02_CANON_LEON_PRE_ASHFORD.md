# Alderwick — LEON PRE-Alderwick CANON

**Document Type:** Development-Only Canon  
**Scope:** Leon Ashcroft's life before arriving in Alderwick  
**Status:** APPROVED CANON  
**Priority:** HIGH  
**Applies To:** Chapter 1 onward unless explicitly superseded by a later canon document

---

# 1. PURPOSE

This document defines the hidden continuity surrounding Leon Ashcroft's life before his arrival in Alderwick.

It exists to prevent later chapters from accidentally contradicting:

- Leon's orphan background
- the circumstances of his childhood
- the origin and history of the medallion
- Leon's recurring dreams
- the relationship between the dreams and Alderwick
- the date-trigger mechanism
- Leon's lack of conscious memory of Alderwick
- the distinction between actual memory and unexplained familiarity

This document is DEVELOPMENT-ONLY.

Its information must NOT automatically become reader-facing exposition.

The story must reveal these facts gradually, indirectly, and only when dramatically appropriate.

---

# 2. CORE CANON

Leon Ashcroft is an orphan.

He was placed in an orphanage when he was very young.

Leon has no reliable conscious memory of his biological parents.

He does not know the complete circumstances of his abandonment.

He does not know why he was given the surname Ashcroft.

He does not know who originally brought him to the orphanage.

The person who brought Leon to the orphanage left him with a small wooden box.

Inside the box was a small medallion.

The medallion bears the same fundamental symbol that later appears in Alderwick:

- a circle
- three lines
- one dot

The medallion is therefore an important physical connection between Leon's unknown childhood and the mystery surrounding Alderwick.

However, neither Leon nor the reader should understand this connection at the beginning of the story.

---

# 3. THE ORPHANAGE

Leon spent his childhood in an orphanage.

The orphanage should be treated as a real part of Leon's emotional history rather than merely a plot device.

Leon grew up without a normal family structure.

He learned self-reliance early.

He became accustomed to possessing very little that was truly his.

The wooden box therefore became unusual in his life because it was something that had been deliberately left for him.

However, the contents of the box were never properly explained to him.

The person who brought Leon to the orphanage did not provide Leon with a complete explanation of:

- the medallion
- the symbol
- Alderwick
- the Ashcroft family
- Bellweather House
- Aurelius Ashcroft
- the Hidden Circle
- Leon's actual connection to the house

None of these explanations are established as having been given to Leon during childhood.

DO NOT invent a childhood conversation in which Leon is told the full truth.

---

# 4. THE WOODEN BOX

The medallion is kept inside a small wooden box.

The box is old enough to suggest that it existed before Leon received it.

The box should feel personal and deliberate rather than valuable in a conventional financial sense.

It is not initially treated by Leon as an important supernatural artifact.

For most of his life, the box remains closed.

Leon eventually becomes accustomed to its existence without understanding its significance.

He does not regularly inspect the medallion.

He does not study its symbol.

He does not connect it with Alderwick.

He does not consciously search for the meaning of the symbol.

The box therefore becomes psychologically similar to an old unresolved piece of his childhood.

It exists, but Leon has learned to live without asking too many questions about it.

---

# 5. LEON'S MEMORY OF THE MEDALLION

Leon does NOT possess a clear childhood memory of receiving the medallion.

His memory of the object, if any, is extremely vague.

He may remember:

- the existence of a small wooden box
- that it belonged to his childhood
- that someone had left it with him
- a vague impression of a metal object inside

But he does NOT consciously remember:

- who gave it to him
- why it was given to him
- what the symbol means
- where the medallion came from
- why it matters
- any explicit connection to Alderwick

The medallion must therefore NOT function as a convenient exposition device.

Leon should not look at it and immediately think:

"That is the symbol from Alderwick."

That would destroy the intended mystery.

---

# 6. THE MEDALLION

The medallion is physically identical in symbolic design to the recurring Alderwick symbol:

CIRCLE + THREE LINES + ONE DOT

The similarity is intentional and canonical.

The medallion is not merely similar to the Alderwick symbol.

It is the same symbolic design.

The exact meaning of the symbol is NOT established at this stage.

The medallion's original purpose is NOT established at this stage.

The identity of the person who possessed it before Leon is NOT established at this stage.

The circumstances under which it entered Leon's possession are NOT fully established.

Do not invent a complete explanation merely to resolve an early scene.

---

# 7. THE MEDALLION AND LEON'S CHILDHOOD

The medallion has been with Leon since childhood.

However, Leon does not understand its importance.

It is possible that Leon occasionally handled the box as a child.

It is possible that he saw the medallion when he was younger.

However, no precise childhood scene involving the medallion is canon unless explicitly written later.

The story should avoid constructing false memories merely to explain the object.

The emotional truth is more important:

Leon has carried a connection to something he does not understand for most of his life.

---

# 8. LEON'S DREAMS

Leon has experienced unusual dreams since childhood.

These dreams are not normal recurring dreams in which the exact same sequence repeats every night.

Instead, they consist of fragmented and incomplete impressions.

Possible elements include:

- an old house
- unfamiliar rooms
- a street
- a child or young boy
- bells
- footsteps
- doors
- darkness
- the sensation of being expected somewhere
- the sensation of arriving too late
- fragments of voices
- locations that Leon cannot consciously identify

The exact contents of childhood dreams are intentionally not fully defined.

The dreams should remain fragmented.

Leon should not be able to construct a coherent story from them.

---

# 9. DREAMS ARE NOT CONSCIOUS MEMORIES

The dreams must NOT initially be treated as recovered memories.

Leon does not consciously believe:

"I lived in Alderwick."

He does not consciously remember:

"I was in this house."

He does not know:

"This is the street from my childhood."

His conscious reasoning remains:

"I have never been here before."

The strange familiarity is therefore not equivalent to factual memory.

The narrative must preserve the distinction between:

A. factual memory
B. dream-derived familiarity
C. unexplained intuition

Leon may experience B or C without mistaking them for A.

---

# 10. THE DATE-TRIGGER MECHANISM

The dreams are connected to specific dates.

This mechanism is CANONICAL at the development level.

However, Leon does not know this.

The reader must also NOT understand the mechanism early in the story.

The trigger is not something Leon consciously activates.

The trigger is not a magical alarm that Leon can control.

The mechanism should remain subtle.

Certain dates can cause the buried connection to become more active.

This can manifest through:

- unusually vivid dreams
- recurring imagery
- stronger feelings of familiarity
- unexplained emotional reactions
- déjà vu-like sensations
- fragments that seem more coherent than normal
- physical or environmental associations

The exact complete rules governing the date mechanism are NOT YET REVEALED.

Do not invent a complete calendar of trigger dates.

Do not state that every year follows an identical pattern unless later canon establishes it.

---

# 11. THE NOVEMBER TRIGGER

The events surrounding 12–13 November 1816 are significant.

The timing is NOT coincidental.

Leon arrives in Alderwick on:

12 November 1816.

The expected arrival associated with the earlier Alderwick mystery is:

13 November 1816.

Leon therefore arrives one day earlier than expected.

The date itself is connected to the deeper mystery surrounding Leon.

However, the precise mechanism by which the date affects Leon is not known to Leon at the beginning of the story.

The reader must not immediately be told:

"The dreams were triggered because November 12 is a special date."

That explanation belongs much later.

---

# 12. WHY LEON DOES NOT NOTICE THE DATE PATTERN

Leon has no reason to suspect a date pattern.

His childhood dreams were:

- irregular from his perspective
- fragmented
- difficult to remember
- disconnected from identifiable locations
- not consciously associated with Alderwick

Leon therefore never creates a record such as:

"Every November 12 I dream about this house."

He has no established basis for making that connection.

Even if some dreams occurred around significant dates, the pattern should remain invisible to him.

This prevents the character from appearing artificially unaware.

---

# 13. WHY Alderwick FEELS FAMILIAR

Alderwick feels familiar to Leon because something connected to Alderwick has existed in his subconscious for years.

The exact mechanism is deliberately unresolved.

Possible manifestations include:

- dream familiarity
- sensory recognition without conscious memory
- emotional recognition
- symbolic recognition
- subconscious association

The story must NOT immediately define which mechanism is responsible.

The important rule is:

Leon has never consciously visited Alderwick before Chapter 1.

He has no factual memory of living there.

He has no conscious memory of its streets.

He has no conscious memory of Bellweather House.

His familiarity must therefore feel uncanny rather than explanatory.

---

# 14. LEON'S STATEMENT THAT HE HAS NEVER BEEN THERE

This statement remains truthful.

Leon is not lying.

Leon is not secretly remembering a previous physical visit.

Leon genuinely believes he has never been to Alderwick.

This distinction is critical.

Do NOT later reveal that Leon actually lived in Alderwick as a child unless this canon is explicitly replaced.

Do NOT use a false-memory twist to solve the familiarity mystery without explicit author approval.

---

# 15. "DON'T BE LATE AGAIN"

The phrase:

"Don't be late again."

is connected to Leon's dream experience.

It is deliberately ambiguous.

At the beginning of the story, neither Leon nor the reader knows:

- who is speaking
- who is being addressed
- what "again" refers to
- when the previous occasion occurred
- whether "late" means physical lateness
- whether "late" refers to an event
- whether "again" refers to something in Leon's childhood
- whether the speaker is real, symbolic, remembered, or connected to the supernatural mystery

The word "again" must NOT be casually explained.

It is a long-term mystery.

---

# 16. "EARLY" AND "LATE" MUST REMAIN DISTINCT

Two apparently contradictory ideas exist:

Leon arrives EARLY according to the expected Alderwick arrival date.

Leon FEELS LATE during his exploration of Alderwick.

These are not necessarily contradictions.

"Early" refers to the external timetable:

Leon physically arrives on 12 November instead of the expected 13 November.

"Late" refers to Leon's subjective or deeper relationship with whatever is waiting in Alderwick.

Therefore:

EARLY = chronological / external arrival

LATE = subjective / mysterious / potentially deeper significance

The narrative must not automatically equate the two.

---

# 17. THE DEEPER MEANING OF "LATE"

The ultimate meaning of "late" is intentionally unresolved.

The phrase may eventually relate to:

- Leon's relationship with the boy
- an event from the past
- a cycle
- a forgotten obligation
- the Hidden Circle
- the Ashcroft family
- the medallion
- the date mechanism
- or another deeper explanation

No final explanation is canon yet.

Do not choose one prematurely.

---

# 18. THE MEDALLION'S FUTURE ROLE

At some later point in the story, Leon will reopen the small wooden box.

This should be treated as an important narrative event.

The box has remained unopened or largely neglected for a long period.

When Leon finally handles the medallion again, something should feel wrong or unusually significant.

The reaction should initially be subtle.

Examples:

- a sudden feeling of recognition
- an unexplained emotional response
- a dream becoming more vivid
- recognition of the symbol without understanding why
- a physical sensation
- an association with Bellweather House
- an environmental coincidence
- a reaction to a specific date

The story should NOT immediately explain the mechanism.

The medallion should raise more questions before it provides answers.

---

# 19. THE MEDALLION MUST NOT BECOME A MAGIC KEY

The medallion is not automatically a magical key.

It should not simply:

- unlock the house
- open a secret door
- reveal a hidden message on command
- provide instant memories
- explain Leon's identity
- identify Aurelius
- solve the mystery

unless later canon explicitly establishes such a function.

Its first narrative purpose is recognition and connection.

Its ultimate function remains unresolved.

---

# 20. AURELIUS ASHCR0FT

Leon does not begin the story knowing the true nature of his connection to Aurelius Ashcroft.

The existence of Aurelius should be discovered gradually.

Leon must not immediately understand:

"That is my ancestor."

or:

"That is the person who sent me away."

or:

"That is the boy from my dreams."

unless later canon explicitly establishes one of these facts.

The relationship between Leon and Aurelius remains a major mystery.

# 25. CONTINUITY RULE — MEMORY VS FAMILIARITY

Every future chapter must distinguish:

A. MEMORY

Something Leon actually remembers happening.

B. DREAM FAMILIARITY

Something feels known because of his dreams or subconscious associations.

C. INTUITION

Leon feels that something is important without knowing why.

Never write B or C as A.

Forbidden examples:

"I remembered living here."

"I had been here before."

"I knew this street from my childhood."

Allowed examples:

"Something about the street felt familiar."

"I could not explain why the place seemed known to me."

"The recognition came before the memory."

"I had no memory of the place, yet something in me reacted to it."

---

# 26. CONTINUITY RULE — DO NOT RETROACTIVELY INVENT MEMORIES

Later revelations may reinterpret earlier events.

They must not arbitrarily create memories that Leon never possessed.

A later chapter may reveal:

"That dream had been connected to Alderwick."

It should not casually rewrite:

"I remembered that I had lived in Alderwick."

unless such a revelation is explicitly approved as a major canon change.

---

# 27. CONTINUITY RULE — THE ORPHANAGE

The orphanage is part of Leon's backstory but is not automatically a source of answers.

The orphanage staff may know fragments.

Individual people may have known fragments.

Records may contain fragments.

But no assumption may be made that the orphanage possesses the complete truth.

Any future information about the orphanage must be established through narrative evidence.

# 32. AUTHOR-ONLY PRINCIPLE

The author/development layer may know more than Leon and the reader.

However, knowledge in this document must not automatically be converted into exposition.

# 33. FINAL CANON SUMMARY

Leon grew up as an orphan.

A person of unknown identity brought him to an orphanage and left him with a small wooden box.

Inside the box was a medallion bearing the same circle-three-lines-one-dot symbol later associated with Alderwick.

Leon never understood the object's significance.

Throughout childhood and later life, Leon experienced fragmented dreams containing imagery that is connected to Alderwick.

The dreams are affected by specific dates, but Leon never recognized the pattern.

When Leon finally arrives in Alderwick on 12 November 1816, the town feels strangely familiar despite his genuine lack of conscious prior experience there.

The next day, 13 November, is the expected arrival date connected to the deeper mystery.

Leon therefore arrives early in chronological terms while simultaneously feeling late in a deeper, unexplained sense.

His dream contains the phrase:

"Don't be late again."

The meaning of "again" remains unresolved.

The medallion, the dreams, the dates, Alderwick, Aurelius, and Leon's childhood are all parts of the same larger mystery.

The exact causal relationship between them must NOT be revealed prematurely.

END OF CANON DOCUMENT.
