## SECTION 1 — CURRENCY SYSTEM

### A. £/S/D SYSTEM

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Pre-decimal British currency:**

**1 Pound (£)** = 20 Shillings (s)
**1 Shilling (s)** = 12 Pence (d)
**1 Pound** = 240 Pence

**Written notation:**
- £2 10s 6d = 2 pounds, 10 shillings, 6 pence
- 15s 6d = 15 shillings, 6 pence (no pounds)
- 6d = 6 pence (no shillings or pounds)

**Coins:**
- **Gold:** Guinea (21s = £1 1s), Sovereign (£1 = 20s)
- **Silver:** Crown (5s), Half-crown (2s 6d), Shilling (1s), Sixpence (6d)
- **Copper:** Penny (1d), Halfpenny (½d), Farthing (¼d)

**Guinea vs. Pound:**
- **Guinea** = 21 shillings (gold coin)
- Used for luxury transactions (horses, art, professional fees)
- **Pound** = 20 shillings (unit of account)
- Used for everyday transactions

**Plot Implications:**
- **Coin shortage** — 1816 has limited silver/copper coinage (hoarding, counterfeiting)
- **"Want of change"** — difficulty making small transactions
- **Counterfeiting** — common crime (death penalty)
- **Gold hoarding** — wealthy hoard guineas during crisis

## SECTION 4 — DEBT & CREDIT

### A. SOURCES OF CREDIT

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**1. Banks (rare in small towns):**
- **Country banks** — small private banks (unstable, frequent failures)
- **London banks** — more stable but distant
- Lend to gentry, large merchants (secured by land)
- **Interest:** 5% typical

**2. Mortgage (on land):**
- Gentry borrows against estate value
- **Interest:** 4-5% annual
- **Risk:** Foreclosure if cannot pay
- Common for gentry ("mortgaged to the hilt")

**3. Local creditors:**
- **Solicitors** hold mortgages, lend money
- **Wealthy merchants** lend to tradesmen
- **Moneylenders** lend to poor (high interest, predatory)

**4. Informal credit:**
- **Shop credit** — buy now, pay later ("on the book")
- **Employer advances** — borrow against future wages
- **Pawnbrokers** — loan against possessions (high interest)

**5. Usury:**
- **Legal maximum interest:** None (usury laws repealed 1714)
- **Typical interest:** 5-10% (formal); 20-50%+ (informal/predatory)
- **Loan sharks** prey on desperate poor

### B. DEBT CRISIS (1816)

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Post-war economic collapse creates debt crisis:**

**Gentry:**
- Mortgaged estates during war (high grain prices = secure income)
- 1816 grain price collapse → cannot pay mortgage
- **Foreclosure threat** — lose estate to creditor

**Tenant farmers:**
- High rents agreed during wartime prosperity
- 1816 harvest failure + price collapse → cannot pay rent
- **Eviction threat**

**Tradesmen/merchants:**
- Extended credit to customers ("on the book")
- Customers cannot pay → merchant cannot pay suppliers
- **Bankruptcy cascade**

**Working class:**
- Owe shop credit, pawnbroker loans
- Unemployment → cannot pay
- **Debtors' prison** (can be imprisoned for debt)

**Plot Implications:**
- **Foreclosure plot** — gentry loses estate to creditor
- **Bankruptcy scandal** — merchant ruin destroys family
- **Debt-driven crime** — forgery, embezzlement, theft
- **Creditor vs. debtor violence** — murder over debt
- **Debtors' prison** — family destitution when breadwinner imprisoned

### C. BANKRUPTCY & DEBTORS' PRISON

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Bankruptcy (for merchants/tradesmen):**
- Formal legal process
- Assets seized, sold to pay creditors
- Social disgrace, loss of credit
- Cannot engage in trade again (reputation destroyed)

**Debtors' Prison:**
- **Can be imprisoned for unpaid debt** (civil, not criminal)
- Creditor pays prison fees to hold debtor
- Debtor stays until debt paid (by family, friends, or creditor releases)
- **Paradox:** Imprisoned cannot work → cannot pay debt → stays imprisoned
- Family destitution (wife/children become paupers)

**Plot Implications:**
- **Debtors' prison plot** — character imprisoned, family desperate
- **Creditor cruelty** — imprisons debtor out of spite
- **Escape from prison** — crime/supernatural aid
- **Debt forgiveness** — Leon intervenes, pays debt

## SECTION 5 — 1816 ECONOMIC CRISIS

### A. THE YEAR WITHOUT A SUMMER

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Cause:** Mount Tambora volcanic eruption (Indonesia, April 1815)
- Ash cloud circles globe
- Temperatures drop 1-3°C
- 1816 = coldest year in recorded history

**Agricultural Impact (Britain):**
- **Late spring** — crops planted late
- **Cold, wet summer** — crops fail to ripen
- **Early frost** — kills remaining crops
- **Harvest failure** — wheat, barley, oats yield 50-70% below normal

**Alderwick Impact (PROPOSED):**
- **Food shortages** — bread prices double
- **Hunger** — malnutrition, disease, infant mortality
- **Desperation** — theft, poaching, rioting
- **Poor relief crisis** — vestry overwhelmed

---

### B. POST-WAR DEPRESSION

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**End of Napoleonic Wars (1815):**
- **Demobilization** — 300,000+ soldiers/sailors discharged
- **Military contracts end** — factories close
- **Grain prices collapse** — imports resume (Corn Laws passed 1815 to protect landlords)
- **Unemployment soars** — veterans + factory workers compete for jobs

**Alderwick Impact (PROPOSED):**
- **Veterans return** — traumatized, unemployed
- **Wage competition** — wages fall due to labor surplus
- **Business failures** — merchants, artisans bankrupted
- **Social unrest** — radical agitation, potential riots

---

### C. CORN LAWS (1815)

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Law:** Ban wheat imports until domestic price reaches 80s/quarter

**Purpose:** Protect gentry landlords from cheap foreign grain

**Effect:**
- Keeps bread prices HIGH (benefits landlords)
- Hurts working class (cannot afford bread)
- **HATED by poor and middle class**
- Major source of radical agitation

**Alderwick Impact (PROPOSED):**
- **Class resentment** — gentry prioritizes profit over survival
- **Radical target** — reformers demand Corn Law repeal
- **Food riot potential** — anger at high prices
