## SECTION 1 — BRITISH GOVERNMENT STRUCTURE (1816)

### A. THE CROWN

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**George III (reigned 1760-1820):**
- **Age:** 78 years old in 1816
- **Condition:** Severely mentally ill (likely porphyria); unable to reign since 1811
- **Status:** Confined at Windsor Castle; has not appeared in public for years
- **Symbolic Role:** Still technically king; all laws require royal assent

**George, Prince of Wales (Prince Regent):**
- **Age:** 54 years old in 1816
- **Role:** Ruling monarch in all but name since Regency Act 1811
- **Character:** Extravagant, debt-ridden, unpopular with public
- **Politics:** Initially Whig sympathizer, but governs with Tories
- **Lifestyle:** Famous for excess, mistresses, Carlton House extravagance

**Royal Powers (exercised by Regent):**
- Appoint/dismiss Prime Minister
- Dissolve Parliament (call elections)
- Give royal assent to bills (technically can refuse, but rarely does)
- Appoint bishops, judges, magistrates, military officers
- Control patronage (government jobs, pensions, honors)

**Alderwick''s Relationship:**
- **No direct contact** — Alderwick residents never see royalty
- **Indirect effects:** Royal appointments (magistrates, bishops) affect local governance
- **Symbolic presence:** Royal Mail, royal coat of arms on official documents
- **Public opinion:** Mixed — some loyal, some critical of Regent''s excess during economic hardship

### B. PRIME MINISTER & CABINET

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Prime Minister: Robert Banks Jenkinson, 2nd Earl of Liverpool (Tory)**
- **Term:** 1812-1827 (longest-serving PM of 19th century)
- **Character:** Pragmatic, cautious, repressive on reform
- **Policies:** Maintain order, suppress radicalism, protect landed interests

**Key Cabinet Ministers (1816):**

| Position | Minister | Significance |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| **Home Secretary** | Viscount Sidmouth | Domestic security; spy networks; prosecutes radicals |
| **Foreign Secretary** | Viscount Castlereagh | International relations; Congress of Vienna |
| **Chancellor of Exchequer** | Nicholas Vansittart | Taxation; public debt; economic policy |
| **War Secretary** | Lord Palmerston | Army; demobilization of veterans |

**Cabinet Powers:**
- Draft legislation for Parliament
- Enforce laws through magistrates and courts
- Control tax collection
- Direct military and police (limited in 1816)
- Appoint local officials (magistrates, customs officers)

**Alderwick''s Relationship:**
- **No direct contact** — Cabinet ministers never visit Alderwick
- **Indirect effects:** Cabinet policies (taxation, poor law, game laws) affect daily life
- **Local enforcement:** Magistrates carry out Cabinet directives
- **Information delay:** News from London takes days to reach Alderwick

### C. PARLIAMENT

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**House of Lords:**
- **Membership:** ~400 hereditary peers + 26 bishops (Lords Spiritual)
- **Power:** Can veto legislation from Commons
- **Character:** Ultra-conservative; protects aristocratic privilege
- **Voting:** All members can vote (but many don''t attend)

**House of Commons:**
- **Membership:** 658 MPs (Members of Parliament)
- **Voting Rights:** EXTREMELY LIMITED
  - Only ~200,000 men out of ~5 million adult males can vote (~4%)
  - Must own property worth £2+ annual rent (in counties) or meet borough qualifications
  - NO secret ballot (public voting, subject to bribery/intimidation)
  - NO women, working class, or poor can vote
  
**Rotten Boroughs:**
- Many constituencies have tiny populations but send 2 MPs
- Some boroughs controlled by single landowner (pocket boroughs)
- Example: Old Sarum (7 voters, 2 MPs) vs. Manchester (400,000 people, 0 MPs)

**Alderwick''s Parliamentary Representation:**

**PROPOSED: NONE**

Alderwick is NOT a parliamentary borough. It has no MPs. Alderwick residents vote (if qualified) in the **county constituency** where Alderwick is located.

**County Voting (if Alderwick residents qualify):**
- Must own freehold property worth £2+ annual rent (40-shilling freeholders)
- Very few Alderwick residents qualify (only landed gentry, wealthy yeomen)
- Must travel to **county town** to vote (no local polling)
- Voting is PUBLIC — landlords, employers, creditors can see how you vote

**Plot Implications:**
- **Disenfranchisement** — 96% of Alderwick residents have NO political voice
- **Reform agitation** — radicals demand universal suffrage, secret ballot
- **Government repression** — Cabinet uses magistrates to suppress reform meetings
- **Class resentment** — working/middle class excluded from power

## SECTION 2 — NATIONAL LAWS AFFECTING Alderwick

### A. TAXATION

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE + PROPOSED

**National Taxes (paid by Alderwick residents):**

**1. Land Tax:**
- Old tax on landed property
- Fixed assessment (not proportional to income)
- Mostly paid by landowners (gentry, yeomen)

**2. Window Tax:**
- Tax based on number of windows in a building
- Encourages bricking up windows (health hazard)
- Paid by property owners
- **Rate (1816):** 6 shillings per window (for buildings with 10+ windows)

**3. Customs & Excise:**
- Taxes on imported goods (tea, sugar, tobacco, wine, spirits)
- Collected at ports (Alderwick residents pay indirectly through higher prices)
- **Smuggling:** Common way to avoid these taxes (relevant plot?)

**4. Income Tax:**
- **ABOLISHED in 1816!** (after Napoleonic Wars ended)
- Previously taxed incomes over £60/year at 10%
- Abolition was popular but created budget crisis

**5. Stamp Duties:**
- Tax on legal documents (contracts, wills, property transfers)
- Paid by solicitors'' clients
- **Newspaper Stamp Duty:** 4 pence per newspaper (makes newspapers expensive, limits circulation)

**Alderwick Tax Impact:**

| Class | Taxes Paid | Burden |
|-------|------------|--------|
| **Landed Gentry** | Land tax, window tax, customs (indirectly) | Moderate (can afford it) |
| **Middle Class** | Window tax (small), customs (indirectly), stamp duties | Significant (resents it) |
| **Working Class** | Customs (indirectly through food/goods prices) | Heavy (regressive taxation) |
| **Paupers** | None directly (too poor) | Suffers from high food prices |

**Plot Implications:**
- **Window tax** — visible sign of wealth/poverty (count windows to estimate income)
- **Customs duties** — high food prices contribute to 1816 hunger crisis
- **Stamp duty** — limits newspaper access (information control)
- **Tax resistance** — smuggling, protests, resentment of government

### C. POOR LAW

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Old Poor Law (1601-1834):**
- Each **parish** responsible for its own poor
- Funded by **poor rates** (local property tax)
- Two types of relief:
  1. **Outdoor relief** — money/food given to poor in their homes
  2. **Indoor relief** — workhouse (harsh conditions, forced labor)

**Settlement Laws:**
- Paupers can only claim relief in their **parish of settlement** (usually birthplace)
- Parishes can "remove" non-settled paupers (force them to return to home parish)
- Creates conflict between parishes over who pays for paupers

**1816 Poor Law Crisis:**
- **Post-war depression** — massive unemployment from demobilized soldiers, failed harvests
- **Rising poor rates** — parishes struggle to fund relief
- **Speenhamland System** (some parishes) — subsidize low wages with poor relief (controversial)

- **Select Vestry** controls poor relief (see Alderwick_CITY_ARCHITECTURE.md)
- **High poor rates** in 1816 (2 shillings per pound) due to economic crisis
- **Workhouse:** UNKNOWN (does Alderwick have one? Or send paupers to county workhouse?)
- **Settlement disputes:** Alderwick may try to remove non-settled paupers to reduce costs

**Plot Implications:**
- **Life-or-death power** — vestry decides who eats, who starves
- **Corruption** — embezzlement of poor relief funds
- **Cruelty** — workhouse conditions, removal of vulnerable paupers
- **Class conflict** — middle class resents paying for poor relief
- **Desperation** — poverty drives crime, prostitution, radical politics

### D. GAME LAWS

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Purpose:** Protect gentry''s hunting privileges

**1816 Game Laws:**
- **Only qualified persons** can hunt game (deer, pheasant, partridge, hare, etc.)
- **Qualification:** Must own land worth £100+ annual income (only gentry)
- **Poaching** — hunting by unqualified persons
  - Punishment: Transportation for 7 years (first offense), 14 years (repeat)
  - Game laws are HATED by rural poor (who see rabbits eating their crops but can''t hunt them)

**Gamekeepers:**
- Employed by landed gentry to patrol estates
- Authorized to arrest poachers
- Often brutal (beatings, man-traps, spring-guns)

**Alderwick''s Game Laws (PROPOSED):**
- **Gentry estates** around Alderwick have gamekeepers
- **Poaching** is common (hungry laborers risk transportation to feed families)
- **Magistrates** enforce game laws harshly (they are the beneficiaries)

**Plot Implications:**
- **Class warfare** — game laws symbolize gentry privilege vs. poor survival
- **Poaching rings** — organized criminal networks
- **Gamekeeper violence** — potential murder victim/suspect
- **Magistrate corruption** — selective enforcement based on personal interests

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Napoleonic Wars (1803-1815):**
- Ended with Napoleon''s defeat at Waterloo (June 1815)
- Britain victorious but economically exhausted
- **300,000+ soldiers and sailors demobilized** (unemployment crisis)

**1816 Economic Crisis:**
- **Industrial depression** — factories close, unemployment soars
- **Agricultural depression** — grain prices collapse (hurts tenant farmers)
- **Harvest failure** — "Year Without a Summer" (volcanic winter from Mount Tambora eruption)
- **Food shortages** — bread prices soar, riots break out

### B. REFORM MOVEMENT & GOVERNMENT REPRESSION

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**Reform Demands:**
- **Universal suffrage** (or at least expanded voting rights)
- **Secret ballot** (end public voting)
- **Rotten borough abolition** (redistribute seats to cities)
- **Annual Parliaments** (elections every year to increase accountability)
- **Abolition of property qualifications** (poor men can be MPs)

**Reform Organizations:**
- **Hampden Clubs** — middle/working class political clubs (spreading across England in 1816)
- **Corresponding Societies** — exchange radical literature, coordinate protests
- **Luddites** — machine-breaking (peaked 1811-1813, but still active)

**Government Repression:**
- **Spy networks** — Home Office employs informers to infiltrate radical groups
- **Seditious libel** — prosecute publishers of radical newspapers
- **Suspension of Habeas Corpus** (1817, after Spa Fields Riot) — allows imprisonment without trial
- **Six Acts** (1819, after Peterloo Massacre) — ban large meetings, restrict press
- **Local magistrates** — suppress reform meetings, arrest speakers

### B. WEAK CENTRAL GOVERNMENT

**Status:** HISTORICAL_REFERENCE

**1816 British State:**
- **NO professional police** (except Bow Street Runners in London)
- **NO national bureaucracy** (tiny civil service)
- **NO standing army in peacetime** (demobilization after 1815)
- **Relies on local officials** — magistrates, constables, militia

**Central Government Powers:**
- Parliament passes laws
- Cabinet issues directives
- BUT enforcement depends entirely on **local magistrates**

**Alderwick''s Autonomy:**
- **Local magistrates** have enormous discretion in enforcing (or ignoring) national laws
- **No oversight** — unless Quarter Sessions or Assize judges investigate
- **Corruption/incompetence** can go unchecked for years

**Plot Implications:**
- **Local tyranny** — corrupt magistrate operates with impunity
- **Selective enforcement** — magistrates enforce laws that benefit them, ignore others
- **Supernatural cover-up** — local officials can suppress evidence without London knowing
- **Leon as substitute** — Leon fills power vacuum left by weak/corrupt officials
