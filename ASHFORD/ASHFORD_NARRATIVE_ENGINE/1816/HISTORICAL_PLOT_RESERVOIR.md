# 1816 Historical Plot Reservoir

**Status:** PROPOSED DESIGN MATERIAL — NOT CANON
**Created:** 2026-08-29
**Era:** 1816 (Regency Britain, George III / Prince Regent)
**Canon Period:** 12 November – 26 November 1816 (Chapters 001-014)

---

## PURPOSE

This reservoir contains historically grounded pressures, events, and tensions
around 1816 that can be selectively activated when designing arcs, characters,
and world-building for Ashford.

**Do not force every historical event into Ashford.** The purpose is to create
a deep reservoir of possibilities that can be selectively activated when
designing arcs and characters.

---

## SEPARATION OF CLAIM TYPES

Every entry in this reservoir labels its claims as one of:

### FACT
Established historical fact with strong documentary evidence.
These are things that would be true for any informed reader to accept.

### INFERENCE
A reasonable conclusion drawn from facts but not directly documented.
An inference is plausible, defensible, and historically grounded — but
it is not proven. Use with author awareness.

### FICTIONAL ADAPTATION
A creative extension of facts or inferences into the narrative world.
This is what the story *does* with the history. Mark clearly as such.

---

## HOW TO USE THIS RESERVOIR

1. **Find a pressure** that resonates with the character or arc you are
   developing.
2. **Check the confidence rating** to know how solid the historical basis is.
3. **Select the adaptation level:** FACT → INFERENCE → FICTIONAL ADAPTATION.
4. **Activate selectively.** Only 3-5 of these pressures should be actively
   present in any given arc. The rest are dormant, waiting for the right moment.
5. **Never override character.** History provides pressure and possibility.
   Characters make choices. The story is about what those choices are.

## DETAILED PRESSURES (1-2)

### 01 — YEAR WITHOUT A SUMMER / TAMBORA AFTERMATH

- **ID:** HIST-1816-01
- **Date / Period:** Year-round 1816; peak Jun-Oct
- **Historical Fact:** Mount Tambora erupted 10 Apr 1815 (Dutch East Indies). Volcanic winter dropped global temps 0.4-0.7C. Summer 1816: coldest on record in Europe 1766-2000. Persistent "dry fog" dimmed sunlight. Frost in Jun and Sep across Britain. Crops failed across Northern Hemisphere. John D. Post: "the last great subsistence crisis in the Western world."
- **Location / Scope:** Global; most acute in Europe, NE North America
- **Ashford Relevance:** DIRECT. Perpetual grey light, unseasonal cold, struggling crops. Ideal gothic atmosphere. Ashford could experience late spring frosts, unripe harvests, perpetual damp cold in November (canon period).
- **Possible Character Conflict:** A character notices the sky is perpetually grey. A farmer's crop ruined for a second year. Someone blames divine punishment. Someone suspects natural causes no one understands.
- **Possible Social Conflict:** Neighbors blame each other for "cursed" land. A landlord raises rents despite crop failure. Tenants resist. Rumors of witchcraft.
- **Possible Mystery Use:** Strange atmospheric phenomena as omens. Unexplained livestock deaths (cold killed them). A visitor from Switzerland reports even worse conditions.
- **Possible World-Building Use:** Grey light, perpetual damp, cold hearths, vivid sunsets. Colder winters beginning. Food scarcity in winter 1816-17.
- **Possible Supernatural Adaptation:** Interpreted as supernatural omen — or the story's version of a supernatural atmospheric effect, truth left ambiguous.
- **Urgency:** HIGH | **Plot Potential:** 9/10 | **Confidence:** VERY HIGH
- **Sources:** John D. Post, *The Last Great Subsistence Crisis*; Klingaman & Klingaman, *The Year Without Summer* (2013)

### 02 — POST-WAR ECONOMIC CONTRACTION

- **ID:** HIST-1816-02
- **Date / Period:** 1815-1818 (peak contraction)
- **Historical Fact:** End of Napoleonic Wars caused immediate severe economic contraction. Wartime industries collapsed. 200,000+ soldiers demobilized. Income tax abolished 18 Mar 1816 (benefited wealthy only). Poor rates skyrocketed. Trade disrupted.
- **Location / Scope:** Britain; worst in manufacturing centers and military ports
- **Ashford Relevance:** DIRECT. Higher poor rates, unemployed laborers arriving, landlords struggling with rent, reduced consumer demand.
- **Possible Character Conflict:** A gentleman who made money supplying the army now faces ruin. A soldier's wife arrives seeking work. A factory owner cannot compete.
- **Possible Social Conflict:** Landlords vs. tenants. Wealthy rate-payers vs. poor relief recipients.
- **Possible Mystery Use:** Economic ruin as motive for fraud or murder. A destroyed document about wartime dealings.
- **Possible World-Building Use:** Empty shops, reduced social activity, wealth disparity, genteel poverty.
- **Possible Supernatural Adaptation:** Economic pressure mirrors internal decay.
- **Urgency:** HIGH | **Plot Potential:** 8/10 | **Confidence:** VERY HIGH
- **Sources:** Eric J. Evans, *The Forging of the Modern State*; Dorothy Thompson, *The People* (1988)

### 04 — FOOD PRICES AND HARVEST PROBLEMS

- **ID:** HIST-1816-04
- **Date / Period:** Year-round; critical May-Oct 1816 (growing season)
- **Historical Fact:** Bread prices were the single most important economic indicator for the poor. Poor harvests + Tambora cooling drove bread prices sharply higher. Corn laws (1815) kept grain prices artificially high. Poor relief spending increased dramatically.
- **Location / Scope:** Nationwide; worst where already poor
- **Ashford Relevance:** DIRECT. Affects every character. Margaret (the cook) feels it daily.
- **Possible Character Conflict:** A character chooses between feeding household or paying debts. A neighbor starves. Someone hoards food.
- **Possible Social Conflict:** Rioters demanding bread. Landlords refusing rent reductions.

### 05 — LITTLEPORT AND ELY RIOTS (22 May 1816)

- **ID:** HIST-1816-05
- **Date / Period:** 22 May 1816
- **Historical Fact:** A crowd of several thousand attacked Littleport and Ely (Cambridgeshire), demanding food and protesting poor relief cuts. Broke into bakeries and warehouses. Militia fired, killing several. Martial law briefly declared. One of largest domestic disturbances since Gordon Riots (1780).
- **Location / Scope:** Cambridgeshire/East Anglia
- **Ashford Relevance:** INDIRECT — plausible template for local unrest if Ashford is nearby. Shows how food prices trigger violence.
- **Possible Character Conflict:** A character decides whether to join a disturbance or protect the house. A soldier is sent to suppress local unrest.
- **Possible Social Conflict:** Fear among gentry of the "mob." The poor decide hunger justifies violence.
- **Possible Mystery Use:** After a disturbance, a key document is destroyed or stolen.
- **Urgency:** MEDIUM | **Plot Potential:** 7/10 | **Confidence:** VERY HIGH
- **Sources:** H. W. Dickinson, *The English Bread Riot* (1905); Dickinson, *C. Johnson, An Account of the Ely and Littleport Riots* (1893)

### 14 — GAS LIGHTING DEVELOPMENT

- **ID:** HIST-1816-14
- **Date / Period:** Early development; first public gas lighting 1813-1816
- **Historical Fact:** The Imperial Gas Light and Coke Company was founded in 1810. Public gas lighting was being installed in London (Leadenhall Street 1813, Pall Mall 1807 experiment). Feb 1816: Preston became the first English town outside London with public gas lighting (promoted by Jesuit priest Joseph Dunn). Most British homes still used candles, tallow, or oil. Gas lighting was novel, expensive, and associated with urban centers and wealthy establishments.
- **Location / Scope:** London first; spreading to provincial towns; Ashford would be unlikely to have it yet
- **Ashford Relevance:** DIRECT as a contrast and aspiration. A wealthy household might consider installing gas. A character who has seen gas in London brings back stories of the new technology.
- **Possible Character Conflict:** A character wants to install gas and faces opposition. A visitor from London demonstrates gas technology. Someone is fascinated or frightened by the new light.
- **Possible World-Building Use:** Gas lighting as a symbol of progress and danger. The glow vs. candlelight. The smell of gas. The novelty of a house lit by gas.
- **Possible Supernatural Adaptation:** Gaslight creates a different quality of shadow and illumination — useful for gothic atmosphere. Flickering, unnatural, unreliable.
- **Urgency:** LOW-MEDIUM | **Plot Potential:** 7/10 — strong visual world-building element
- **Confidence:** HIGH
- **Sources:** *The Times* (London) archives 1813-1816; R. W. Richmond, *History of the Imperial Gas Light and Coke Company* (1960)

### 15 — DAVY LAMP (JAN 1816)

- **ID:** HIST-1816-15
- **Date / Period:** Jan 1816 (first tested at Hebburn Colliery, Durham)
- **Historical Fact:** Sir Humphry Davy's safety lamp (the "Davy lamp") was first tested underground at Hebburn Colliery on 9 January 1816. It was intended to prevent firedamp explosions in coal mines. Independently, George Stephenson also developed a safety lamp. The lamps proved less safe than advertised and casualties continued growing through the 19th century.
- **Location / Scope:** Northern England (coal-mining regions)
- **Ashford Relevance:** INDIRECT. Coal supply and mining safety are relevant for any household that heats with coal. Could affect a character with mining interests.

### 17 — LAW ENFORCEMENT (PRE-POLICE)

- **ID:** HIST-1816-17
- **Date / Period:** Throughout 1816; Metropolitan Police not founded until 1829
- **Historical Fact:** Britain had NO professional police force until 1829. Law enforcement: Parish constables (unpaid, rotating, untrained); Bow Street Runners (small paid group, London only); Night watchmen (elderly, often drunk); Private informants and bounty hunters; Magistrates' private guards. Crime rates high. Transporting convicts to Australia standard.
- **Location / Scope:** Limited to towns; rural areas had even fewer protections
- **Ashford Relevance:** DIRECT. Who investigates a crime? A parish constable? A household's servants? The local magistrate?
- **Possible Character Conflict:** A constable faces impossible choices. A magistrate enforces law arbitrarily.
- **Possible Mystery Use:** No forensic science. No central records. A crime could go completely uninvestigated.
- **Urgency:** HIGH | **Plot Potential:** 9/10 — essential for crime/mystery
- **Confidence:** VERY HIGH
- **Sources:** Donald Low, *The Regency Underworld* (1999); J. M. Beattie, *Crime and the Courts* (1986)

### 22 — MEDICAL LIMITATIONS

Letters could take days to weeks depending on distance. Lloyd's List (ships) published in London, 3x/week. The "Post Office" handled mail.
