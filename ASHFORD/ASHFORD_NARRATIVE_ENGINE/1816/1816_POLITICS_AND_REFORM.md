## THE GOVERNMENT (1816)

### Incumbents
- **Monarch:** George III (severely ill; unable to reign — permanently blind, possibly psychotic)
- **Regent:** George, Prince of Wales (Prince Regent, later George IV)
- **Prime Minister:** Robert Jenkinson, 2nd Earl of Liverpool (Tory)
- **Home Secretary:** Lord Sidmouth
- **Foreign Secretary:** Viscount Castlereagh
- **Secretary of War:** Lord Bathurst

### Political Structure
- House of Lords (peers)
- House of Commons (elected, but franchise extremely limited)
- No political parties in modern sense — Tories vs. Whigs
- Corruption: "Pocket boroughs" ( constituencies controlled by a single landowner)
- Voting: ~200,000 men could vote out of ~5 million population (4%)

## THE POLITICAL CLIMATE

### Government Fears
The British government in 1816 was terrified of revolution:
- The French Revolution (1789) was still fresh memory
- Napoleon had been defeated (1815) but radical ideas had spread
- Economic distress (post-war contraction + Tambora) created conditions for unrest
- The government believed Britain was on the verge of a French-style revolution
- This fear was not unreasonable — agitation WAS increasing

### Reform Agitation
- Correspondence societies (groups organizing for reform)
- Radical newspapers and pamphleteers
- Public meetings and assemblies (monitored and feared by government)
- Demands for: parliamentary reform, universal suffrage, secret ballot, annual parliaments
- These demands would crystallize into the **Chartist movement** (1830s-1840s)

### Government Response
- Spy networks infiltrating radical societies
- Surveillance of known agitators
- Preparing repressive legislation (Gagging Acts 1817, Six Acts 1819)
- Troop deployments to "troubled" areas
- Magistrates enforcing strict order

## KEY REFORM ISSUES (1816)

### Parliamentary Reform
- Most constituencies were "rotten boroughs" (few voters, but seats in Commons)
- Growing cities (Manchester, Birmingham) had NO representation
- Demand for fair representation was growing but no mainstream party supported it
- The Great Reform Act (1832) was 16 years away

### Catholic Emancipation
- Catholics were barred from Parliament and many professions
- Ireland (where most people were Catholic) was restive
- Question was highly divisive
- Not resolved until 1829

### Poor Laws
- Poor relief was funded by local "poor rates" (taxes on landowners)
- Rates skyrocketed due to post-war unemployment and failed harvests
- Landowners wanted to reduce poor relief
- The poor wanted more support
- This tension would lead to the New Poor Law (1834)

### Labor Rights
- Combination Acts (banning worker unions) were still in force
- Worker organizing was illegal
- Luddite activity (destroying machinery) was a form of labor protest
- Trade unions would be legalized in 1824

## THE PRESS AND INFORMATION

### Newspaper Landscape
- Heavily taxed (stamp duty)
- Radical papers underground (*The Black Dwarf* founded 1817)
- Government papers supported the establishment
- Most working class could not read
- Newspapers were a luxury (pennies to shillings)
- Coffeehouses and taverns were where news was shared orally

### Censorship
- No formal press censorship (prior restraint ended in 1695)
- But taxes made radical papers expensive to produce
- Sedition laws punished those who criticized the government
- The Gagging Acts (1817) would further restrict public meetings and radical speech
- In 1816, the government was testing the waters for repressive legislation

## RADICAL SOCIETIES AND MOVEMENTS

### Correspondence Societies
- Local groups organizing for parliamentary reform
- Communicated across counties
- Monitored by government spies
- Often met in taverns or private homes

### Economic Radicalism
- Luddites: destroying machinery (1811-1818)
- Trade unions: organizing for workers' rights (illegal until 1824)
- Reform societies: demanding political change
- Both working-class and middle-class participation

### Notable Figures (1816)
- Henry Hunt — radical orator (would lead Peterloo 1819)
- William Cobbett — radical journalist and pamphleteer
- Francis Place — reform campaigner
- These figures were dangerous in the government's eyes but respected by reformers
