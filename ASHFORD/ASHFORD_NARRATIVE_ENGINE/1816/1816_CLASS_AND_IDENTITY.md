**Status:** PROPOSED — NOT CANON
**Purpose:** Reference for social class structures and identity questions in 1816

## SOCIAL HIERARCHY (1816)

### PEERAGE (Lords Spiritual and Temporal)
- Dukes, Marquesses, Earls, Viscounts, Barons
- Seats in House of Lords
- Landed wealth + titles
- ~200 people
- **Ashford Relevance:** The highest level of the gentry. May appear in visits or official capacity.

### LANDED GENTRY (Squires and Gentlemen)
- Knights, Esquires, Gentlemen
- Landed wealth (no titles)
- Seat in House of Commons (if they chose to run)
- Local magistrates
- The "established" class
- **Ashford Relevance:** PRIMARY — Bellweather House and the local gentry occupy this level.

### PROFESSIONAL/MIDDLE CLASS
- Lawyers, doctors, merchants, bankers, clergymen (non-gentry)
- Wealthy but not landed
- Increasingly powerful economically
- Socially ambitious
- **Ashford Relevance:** The local doctor, the wealthy merchant — increasingly important in rural towns.

### ARTISANS AND SHOPKEEPERS
- Skilled tradespeople (carpenters, blacksmiths, weavers)
- Small shopkeepers
- Independent but not wealthy
- **Ashford Relevance:** The local tradespeople who serve the gentry.

### AGRICULTURAL LABORERS
- Unskilled farm workers
- Lowest paid class in rural areas
- Seasonal unemployment common
- **Ashford Relevance:** The rural poor around Ashford.

### PAUPERS AND CRIMINALS
- Dependent on poor relief
- Beggars, vagrants
- Criminal underworld
- At risk of transportation to Australia
- **Ashford Relevance:** Visible poverty; the "undeserving poor."

## THE "GENTLEMAN" QUESTION

In 1816, there was NO legal definition of "gentleman." It was a contested concept:

| Definition | Proponents | Reality in 1816 |
|---|---|---|
| **Birth** | Old aristocracy | A gentleman by birth is a gentleman |
| **Income** | Middle class | A gentleman by wealth can buy gentility |
| **Behavior** | Beau Brummell / new fashion | A gentleman is defined by manners, dress, deportment |
| **Land ownership** | Traditional | Only landed wealth is legitimate wealth |

**This tension is a constant source of conflict and anxiety.** A character who is "not a gentleman by birth" but acts like one creates immediate social friction.

## GENDER AND IDENTITY

### Women's Social Position
- Legal status: Coverture (a married woman's property and legal identity belong to her husband)
- Inheritance: Primogeniture favored male heirs (though daughters could inherit if no sons)
- Education: Accomplishments (music, drawing, French) rather than serious study
- Employment: Governess, companion, lady's maid, unmarried life at home
- Social mobility: Marriage was the primary route for upward mobility
- Divorce: Extremely difficult for women; possible for men (scandalous for both)

### Men's Social Position
- Legal authority: Master of household
- Employment: Military (purchase of commissions), clergy, law, land management
- Social mobility: Wealth, marriage, military service, colonial service
- Reputation: Honor, dueling (declining but still practiced), gambling debts
- Beau Brummell's flight (May 1816) over gambling debts shows the fragility of male reputation

## SERVICE AND CLASS

### Domestic Servant Hierarchy
1. Butler (manager of male servants, wine cellar)
2. Housekeeper (manager of female servants, housekeeping)
3. Cook (kitchen management)
4. Under-cook
5. Valet (gentleman's personal servant)
6. Housemaid
7. Undermaid
8. Scullion (lowest — kitchen labor)

**Key fact:** Servants lived in, worked 14-16 hours/day, had limited privacy. They formed an underground communication network. A servant knew more secrets than anyone except the family.

### The Governess
- Uncomfortable position between servant and employer
- Educated but poor
- Socially invisible to other servants
- A common fate for fallen gentry families' daughters
- **Narrative use:** A governess at Bellweather House is a powerful character type — educated, observant, socially precarious.

## CLASS MARKERS

### How to Tell Someone's Class (1816)

| Marker | Gentry | Middle Class | Working Class |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speech | Received, precise | Variable, regionally influenced | Regional, limited vocabulary |
| Clothes | Well-cut, dark, maintained | New but not well-fitting | Hand-me-downs, worn |
| Hand | Soft, uncalloused | Some callouses | Calloused, work-worn |
| Education | Latin/Greek, accomplishments | Basic literacy | Little to no literacy |
| Home | Large house, servants | Modest house, maybe one servant | Room or cottage |
| Food | Multiple courses, wine | Single course, beer/water | Bread, porridge |
| Leisure | Riding, balls, reading | Trade, reading, church | Drinking, simple recreation |

## SOCIAL MOBILITY

### Possible Routes
- Marriage (most common)
- Military commission (purchase)
- Colonial service (EIC)
- Inheritance (rare)
- Business success (middle class only)

### Barriers
- Birth determined more than merit
- Old money vs. new money
- Education access
- Social connections

## ASHFORD RELEVANCE

- Every interaction in Ashford is shaped by class
- The gentry of Bellweather House occupy a precarious position (possibly falling in wealth)
- Servants are the information network of the household
- The local doctor and merchant represent the rising middle class
- Agricultural laborers represent the invisible majority
- A character whose class identity is false is a powerful dramatic device
